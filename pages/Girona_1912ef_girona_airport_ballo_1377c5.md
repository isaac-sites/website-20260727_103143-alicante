## Introduction

[Girona]({{ 'gironas-ufo-cases-solved-weak-and/' | relative_url }}) Airport’s 26 September 1969 sighting is one of the province’s clearest examples of a reported UFO receiving a credible conventional identification. Airport staff saw two bright objects, twenty minutes apart, at an estimated altitude of 25,000 to 30,000 feet. Reports also arrived from elsewhere in the province. Yet the people best placed to assess the objects—including the airport’s meteorological observer—believed they were French weather balloons of a type already familiar in the area. The Spanish Air Force ultimately accepted that interpretation and recorded that no further investigation was necessary because the circumstances appeared clear.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://media.ondisclosure.org/web/official-uap/es/bvd/es-bvd-395884.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: media.ondisclosure.org">[media.ondisclosure.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">media.ondisclosure.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">es bvd 395884</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">es bvd 395884</span></span></span>

The case matters because it shows what an official UFO report could represent in practice. The objects were genuinely unidentified when first communicated through aviation channels, but “unidentified” described a temporary lack of confirmation, not evidence of an extraordinary craft. The surviving file documents the movement from alert and uncertainty to a reasoned, though not physically proven, balloon explanation.

## What airport staff reported in September 1969

The first object was reported at about 17:30 on 26 September. According to the Air Force summary, several witnesses saw a luminous object from different parts of Girona province, estimating its altitude at between 25,000 and 30,000 feet—roughly 7.6 to 9.1 kilometres. At approximately 17:50, a second object with similar characteristics appeared.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://media.ondisclosure.org/web/official-uap/es/bvd/es-bvd-395884.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: media.ondisclosure.org">[media.ondisclosure.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">media.ondisclosure.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">es bvd 395884</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">es bvd 395884</span></span></span>

The duty officer at Girona–Costa Brava Airport described the first light in the south-eastern sky and said the meteorological observer placed it near 30,000 feet. The sighting was promptly reported by teletype to the head of the Catalonia Air Sector and to the civil aviation authorities. The second object, seen towards the north, was also formally communicated. Telephone calls then arrived from several places in the province whose residents said they had observed the same phenomenon.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://media.ondisclosure.org/web/official-uap/es/bvd/es-bvd-395884.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: media.ondisclosure.org">[media.ondisclosure.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">media.ondisclosure.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">es bvd 395884</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">es bvd 395884</span></span></span>

The meteorological observer provided a more detailed account. He said he had used clouds at a known or estimated level as a reference when judging the object’s height. Calls came from Tossa de Mar and nearby places at about 17:45, asking the airport for information about a luminous object visible over the town. He then saw the second object near the airport’s northern runway end.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://media.ondisclosure.org/web/official-uap/es/bvd/es-bvd-395884.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: media.ondisclosure.org">[media.ondisclosure.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">media.ondisclosure.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">es bvd 395884</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">es bvd 395884</span></span></span>

Other airport witnesses broadly agreed on the important features:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* The objects appeared intensely bright.
* They remained high and moved slowly enough to be watched rather than flashing rapidly across the sky.
* The first was reportedly visible for as long as three hours, while the second lasted only a few minutes.
* Airport passengers as well as staff saw at least the first object.
* No unusual acceleration, sudden directional changes, aircraft interaction or other clearly anomalous behaviour was recorded.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://media.ondisclosure.org/web/official-uap/es/bvd/es-bvd-395884.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: media.ondisclosure.org">[media.ondisclosure.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">media.ondisclosure.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">es bvd 395884</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">es bvd 395884</span></span></span>

</div>

These details make the event more substantial than a fleeting report by a single observer. They do not, however, make it inexplicable. Long duration, intense reflected light and slow apparent movement are all features compatible with a large balloon at high altitude.

## Why French weather balloons matched the observation

The strongest evidence for the balloon identification came from prior local experience. The meteorological observer said the object appeared to be a sounding balloon from France, adding that other such balloons had been recovered at the airport. An airport driver independently gave much the same explanation: French observatories frequently launched these balloons, and examples had previously come down near Girona Airport.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://media.ondisclosure.org/web/official-uap/es/bvd/es-bvd-395884.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: media.ondisclosure.org">[media.ondisclosure.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">media.ondisclosure.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">es bvd 395884</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">es bvd 395884</span></span></span>

This was not merely a remote analyst assigning a familiar explanation years later. Staff who handled aviation and weather information were comparing the sighting with objects they had already seen in the same region. The Air Force summary states that the balloons’ size, form, brilliance and movement matched earlier observations so closely that airport personnel were accustomed to recognising them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://media.ondisclosure.org/web/official-uap/es/bvd/es-bvd-395884.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: media.ondisclosure.org">[media.ondisclosure.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">media.ondisclosure.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">es bvd 395884</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">es bvd 395884</span></span></span>

An aeronautical infrastructure engineer also examined the object through tower binoculars and a surveying instrument. He judged that it could be a weather balloon, although he considered a kite another possibility. That alternative did not become the official conclusion, probably because the estimated height, prolonged visibility and previous recovery of French balloons fitted a high-altitude balloon better than an ordinary kite.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://media.ondisclosure.org/web/official-uap/es/bvd/es-bvd-395884.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: media.ondisclosure.org">[media.ondisclosure.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">media.ondisclosure.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">es bvd 395884</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">es bvd 395884</span></span></span>

The airport chief’s final report concluded that both objects could have been balloons released by French observatories. He noted that these objects were often visible at Girona’s latitude and that at least one had previously been recovered nearby. The commanding general of Spain’s Third Air Region subsequently endorsed essentially the same interpretation, observing that French balloons regularly entered Spanish territory near Girona province.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://media.ondisclosure.org/web/official-uap/es/bvd/es-bvd-395884.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: media.ondisclosure.org">[media.ondisclosure.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">media.ondisclosure.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">es bvd 395884</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">es bvd 395884</span></span></span>

The later intelligence summary added broader historical support: France’s National Centre for Space Studies, the CNES, was still operating an intensive stratospheric-balloon programme in 1969, following extensive launch activity during May and September 1968. Contemporary Spanish military files from other provinces likewise associated several high-altitude sightings with French scientific balloons, making Girona part of a recognisable pattern rather than an isolated explanation invented for one case.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://media.ondisclosure.org/web/official-uap/es/bvd/es-bvd-395884.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: media.ondisclosure.org">[media.ondisclosure.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">media.ondisclosure.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">es bvd 395884</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">es bvd 395884</span></span></span>

## How strong was the identification?

The balloon explanation is highly plausible, but the file does not provide absolute physical proof. No document identifies a particular launch station, flight number or recovered instrument package as the source of either object. There is no recorded reconstruction of wind direction, no launch log attached to the file and no serial-number match linking debris to the observation.

That distinction matters. The objects were **identified by informed comparison**, not by retrieving and tracing the specific balloons. The case therefore sits slightly below a fully demonstrated identification in which an object’s origin is independently confirmed. Nevertheless, several factors make the conventional conclusion considerably stronger than a speculative dismissal:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* multiple airport witnesses offered mutually compatible descriptions;
* observers with meteorological and aeronautical experience recognised a familiar type of object;
* similar French balloons had reportedly reached or been collected near the airport before;
* the prolonged, bright and apparently slow observation suited a high-altitude balloon;
* the known French stratospheric programme supplied a realistic source;
* nothing in the statements required unusual performance or technology.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://media.ondisclosure.org/web/official-uap/es/bvd/es-bvd-395884.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: media.ondisclosure.org">[media.ondisclosure.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">media.ondisclosure.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">es bvd 395884</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">es bvd 395884</span></span></span>

</div>

There are small inconsistencies in direction and wording. The duty officer placed the first object towards the south-east, while the meteorological observer’s statement appears to place it towards the south-west. One witness thought it stood roughly over Tossa de Mar. Such differences limit any precise reconstruction, but they do not seriously damage the balloon interpretation. Estimating the bearing and position of a remote object from the ground is difficult, especially when its true distance and dimensions are unknown.

The reported three-hour duration also deserves caution. It comes from one witness statement and may refer to the total period during which the first object remained intermittently visible rather than uninterrupted observation by one person. Even taken literally, however, a long duration favours a slowly drifting balloon over an aircraft, meteor or fast-moving astronomical event.

## How the Air Force handled the report

The file shows a routine but genuine chain of official reporting. The initial airport messages were followed by an inquiry ordered through the Catalonia Air Sector. The Girona Airport chief collected statements from the duty officer, meteorological observer, airport driver and aeronautical engineer. His report then passed through the Third Air Region and the Air Staff, which requested the regional commander’s interpretation before closing the matter.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://media.ondisclosure.org/web/official-uap/es/bvd/es-bvd-395884.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: media.ondisclosure.org">[media.ondisclosure.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">media.ondisclosure.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">es bvd 395884</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">es bvd 395884</span></span></span>

This administrative trail explains why the incident became an “UFO file” despite its ordinary conclusion. At the time of the first teletype, the airport was reporting objects that had not yet been formally identified. Aviation authorities had reason to record high-altitude objects because they could represent balloons, aircraft, scientific equipment or other traffic requiring assessment.

The file was originally marked confidential and later reviewed during Spain’s declassification programme. It was authorised for declassification on 21 September 1993. The Ministry of Defence catalogue now describes it as a 16-page Air Force file under reference 690926.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es/BVMDefensa/exp_ovni/es/consulta/busqueda_referencia.do?campo=idtitulo&amp;idValor=395884" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es">[Biblioteca Virtual Defensa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gob.es.</span></span></span>

## What this case teaches about military UFO files

Girona Airport’s balloon case is valuable precisely because it resists two misleading extremes. It would be wrong to say that nothing unusual was reported: several people did see conspicuous objects, the airport notified senior authorities and an official inquiry followed. It would be equally wrong to treat the resulting military paperwork as evidence that the objects were exotic or extraterrestrial.

The file instead preserves an identification process. An unfamiliar light triggered reporting; witnesses with relevant local knowledge compared it with previous balloons; investigators gathered statements; commanders reviewed the explanation; and the Air Force concluded that no unresolved security or scientific question justified further work. Its 1993 summary explicitly says that no later investigation was recorded because the event was considered clear.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://media.ondisclosure.org/web/official-uap/es/bvd/es-bvd-395884.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: media.ondisclosure.org">[media.ondisclosure.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">media.ondisclosure.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">es bvd 395884</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">es bvd 395884</span></span></span>

Within Girona’s wider UFO history, this provides a useful benchmark for judging less conclusive incidents. Multiple witnesses and an aviation setting can make a report worth examining, but they do not guarantee an anomalous outcome. The crucial difference is whether ordinary explanations are tested against the observed duration, movement, appearance, local traffic and witness experience.

By those standards, the September 1969 sighting is best classified as a **credibly identified weather-balloon case**, with a modest residual gap because no individual balloon was traced through launch records or recovered equipment. Later publication of the complete military paperwork weakened rather than strengthened any extraordinary interpretation: it revealed that the principal witnesses themselves favoured French scientific balloons and that the official conclusion rested on familiarity with repeated local observations, not on an unexplained object left quietly buried in a classified archive.
