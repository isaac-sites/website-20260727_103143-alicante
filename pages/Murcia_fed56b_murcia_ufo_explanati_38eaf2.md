## Introduction

[Murcia]({{ 'why-murcia-opens-spains-official-ufo/' | relative_url }})’s best-known official UFO reports can plausibly be explained by ordinary lights seen under unusually difficult conditions, although the surviving evidence is too incomplete to identify every source with confidence. Aircraft landing lights, bright stars or planets, distant vehicle lights and atmospheric distortion all fit parts of the record. The central problem is not that these explanations are exotic or unlikely; it is that the witnesses generally lacked reliable distance, bearing, elevation and radar data with which to test them.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Murcia_fed56b_murcia_ufo_explanati_38eaf2-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Likely Explanations" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

This matters because the strongest-sounding details in Murcia’s files — hovering, sudden movement, silence and pursuit — are precisely the impressions that isolated lights can create at night. Spain’s military archive preserves genuine reports from San Javier in 1962 and Mazarrón in 1978, but an official file records that something was reported, not that an extraordinary craft was established. The most defensible conclusion is therefore mixed: ordinary explanations remain credible, while missing measurements prevent a clean retrospective solution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es/BVMDefensa/exp_ovni/es/micrositios/inicio.do" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es">[Biblioteca Virtual Defensa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Gracias a su digitalización se pueden consultar a través de internet en la Biblioteca Virtual de Defensa.... * ### Avistamiento de fenóm...</span></span></span>

## Aircraft lights can look stationary

Aircraft are an obvious candidate in Murcia because the earliest reports came from San Javier Air Base, an active aviation setting. On 6 August 1962, the flight officer initially compared the powerful light over the nearby hill with an aircraft’s landing lamps. A plane approaching almost directly towards an observer can appear to hover because its sideways movement across the field of view is very small. Its landing lights may dominate the view while the body, navigation lights and exhaust noise remain invisible or inaudible. Once the aircraft turns, descends or changes its angle to the observer, the light may suddenly seem to dart sideways, fall vertically or fade at high speed.

This is not merely a sceptical theory invented for UFO cases. Aviation authorities treat night-time depth and motion judgement as a real safety problem. Research published by the US Federal Aviation Administration found that important visual movement cues can become ineffective during night approaches. Aviation guidance also warns that a distant stationary light may appear to move, an illusion known as autokinesis.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.faa.gov/data_research/research/med_humanfacs/oamtechreports/1970s/1978/197815" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faa.gov">[faa.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">These findings give further evidence of large visual illusions in the nighttime situation and indicate that the ineffectiveness of relati...</span></span></span>

Airport lights themselves can be surprisingly conspicuous. Modern visual approach indicators can be visible for 20 miles or more at night, while some systems deliberately use red, white, green or flashing signals. A single aviation light may therefore be seen far beyond the area in which an observer assumes it belongs. It can also change colour or disappear when the viewing angle shifts, because many aeronautical lights are directional rather than equally bright from every direction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.faa.gov/Air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aim_html/chap2_section_1.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faa.gov">[Federal Aviation Administration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on faa.gov.</span></span></span>

None of this proves that an aircraft caused the San Javier observations. The reported movements were described as too rapid for a conventional aircraft or helicopter, and more than one person apparently saw them. Yet without timed angular measurements or a plotted track, “rapid” remains an impression rather than a calculable speed. A small apparent movement by a distant light can be interpreted as a large physical manoeuvre if the observer has unconsciously placed the source much nearer.

## Bright stars can seem to manoeuvre

The San Javier controller’s first reaction is particularly revealing: he reportedly thought the stationary light was a bright star. That judgement was later rejected after the light appeared to move, but apparent motion does not necessarily eliminate an astronomical source. A bright point watched against a dark, nearly featureless sky can seem to wander because the eye has no stable visual reference.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/download/SpanishUFOFiles/1962-08-06_avistamiento_en_san_javier.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive AVISTAMIENTO DE FENOMENOS EXTRAÑOS (EXPEDIENTES OVNI)C</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">G. 2 2 ·Nov. 1992\ LUGAR: FECHA: -SAN JAVIER P HRAGA N.o __ J/, \{_~1::~-, -1962 I dias 6,7 y 13 de Agost. Autokinesis is strongest when a small light is isolated in darkness. Tiny involuntary eye movements are normally corrected by reference to surrounding objects. When those references are absent, the brain may attribute the movement to the light itself. The apparent path can be sideways, vertical or irregular, and several observers can influence one another by agreeing where the light appears to move. Aviation organisations teach pilots about this effect because experienced observers are not immune to it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://skybrary.aero/index.php/articles/visual-illusions" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: skybrary.aero">[Skybrary]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">skybrary.aero</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">* Black hole effect: When approaching an airport surrounded by dark terrain with few other lights in the area, pilots can get an illusion...</span></span></span>

The geography around Murcia can intensify the problem. Reports were often made over dark hills, roads, coastal water or sparsely lit countryside. In such scenes there may be no visible objects at the same apparent distance as the light. Night vision also has poor colour discrimination, reduced sharpness and weak depth perception, making it difficult to distinguish a brilliant distant point from a nearer lamp.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://skybrary.aero/index.php/articles/night-visual-approaches" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: skybrary.aero">[Skybrary]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">skybrary.aero</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on skybrary.aero.</span></span></span>

Bright planets are frequent sources of mistaken aerial reports because they remain visible for long periods, appear exceptionally intense and can seem to follow a moving observer. A witness walking or driving towards a distant planet sees it maintain much the same direction, while foreground terrain shifts around it. The natural interpretation may be that the light is keeping pace. This “following” effect requires no movement by the astronomical object itself.

An astronomical explanation is stronger when a report describes a light that is initially fixed, lacks a discernible shape and remains at a low or moderate elevation for a long time. It becomes weaker when observers record accurate changes against fixed landmarks, obtain simultaneous bearings from separated locations or identify a path that cannot match the sky’s rotation. Murcia’s surviving files do not preserve that level of triangulation.

## Atmosphere can change colour and motion

Atmospheric effects do not usually create a structured machine, but they can alter the appearance of a distant light enough to produce many classic UFO features. Refraction bends light as it passes through air layers of differing temperature and density. Turbulence causes rapid changes in brightness and apparent position. Thin cloud, haze or moisture can spread a point source into a larger glow, make it pulse or temporarily hide it.

These effects are most noticeable near the horizon, where light travels through a longer and less stable path in the atmosphere. A bright star, planet, aircraft lamp or ground light may appear to flash red, white, green or blue as different wavelengths are refracted and scattered. Moving cloud can make the source seem to accelerate, extinguish itself or reappear in another place.

Recognised atmospheric optics include refraction, scattering, diffraction, haloes and coronas. Water droplets can spread light into coloured rings or irregular glows, while dust and dry particles strengthen the reddish or yellow appearance of low-angle light. These broad mechanisms do not identify a particular Murcia report, but they show why colour change alone is weak evidence for a multi-light craft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://weather.metoffice.gov.uk/learn-about/weather/optical-effects" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: weather.metoffice.gov.uk">[Met Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">weather.metoffice.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Met Office Optical effects: nature&#x27;s light show</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Met Office Optical effects: nature&#x27;s light show</span></span></span>

The Mazarrón account of July 1978 involved a dominant red light, intermittent whitish-green lights and several less frequent white lights. Such a pattern could reflect more than one ordinary source: vehicles or equipment at different positions, distant aircraft seen at changing angles, or lights intermittently obscured by ridges and atmospheric haze. The military note itself stated that the initial distance was undefined, even though the witnesses later estimated very low heights.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/SpanishUFOFiles/1978-07-14_avistamiento_en_mazarron_%28murcia%29_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">La División de Información del Estado Mayor envió en 1993 al MOA (actual MACOM) una Nota Informativa, que transcribía una información pro...</span></span></span>

That undefined distance is crucial. Apparent size, speed and altitude cannot be calculated from a light alone. A lamp one kilometre away and a brighter light tens of kilometres away may form almost identical images on the retina. If witnesses assume that the source is above a nearby road, they may infer an altitude of eight or ten metres. If it is actually beyond the hills, that estimate has no physical basis.

## Did vehicle lights follow the soldiers?

The Mazarrón case is harder to reduce to one simple explanation because the soldiers described the lights as accompanying them for roughly two hours, moving ahead when approached and apparently illuminating a reservoir wall in red. They also said that the lights went around houses or hills before reappearing in front of them. These details make the report more substantial than a brief sighting of a stationary star.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/SpanishUFOFiles/1978-07-14_avistamiento_en_mazarron_%28murcia%29_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">La División de Información del Estado Mayor envió en 1993 al MOA (actual MACOM) una Nota Informativa, que transcribía una información pro...</span></span></span>

Even so, the account contains several features associated with misjudged ground or distant lights. The observers were moving through uneven terrain during a night exercise. Roads, ridges and buildings repeatedly altered their line of sight. A light beyond an obstruction can disappear as the observer enters a hollow, then reappear after the route bends. Because the source remains in roughly the same direction, it can seem to anticipate the observer’s movement.

Vehicle lights are plausible in such circumstances. A red rear lamp may dominate when a vehicle is travelling away, while white headlamps or work lights become visible as it turns. Separate vehicles can appear to form one object when their distances are unknown. Lights on distant roads may also rise and fall against the skyline, creating the impression of vertical flight.

The reported silence does not settle the issue. Sound intensity declines with distance and is affected by wind, terrain and background activity. If the witnesses greatly underestimated the distance, an aircraft or vehicle could appear close while remaining too far away to hear clearly. Silence is therefore meaningful only when the distance has been independently established.

The red illumination of the reservoir wall is potentially the strongest detail because it suggests interaction with nearby terrain. Yet the surviving file does not include photographs, light measurements, a scaled map of the witnesses’ positions or testimony clarifying whether the wall was directly lit or merely appeared red in the same field of view. The distinction cannot now be reconstructed securely.

Investigators revisited the area the following night and noted a meteorological-station antenna, but the witnesses rejected it as the source of the confusion. That rejection counts against a simple fixed-beacon explanation, although the follow-up found nothing abnormal and did not reproduce the original conditions. More importantly, the declassified dossier says that no other documentation concerning the event was available beyond the intelligence note transmitted years later.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/SpanishUFOFiles/1978-07-14_avistamiento_en_mazarron_%28murcia%29_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">La División de Información del Estado Mayor envió en 1993 al MOA (actual MACOM) una Nota Informativa, que transcribía una información pro...</span></span></span>

The result is not a successful debunking, but a case left vulnerable to several ordinary explanations. The report may describe a combination of distant lights rather than one object. It may also preserve sincere but inaccurate estimates produced by fatigue, darkness, movement and expectation during a military exercise.

## Missing measurements preserve the mystery

Murcia’s reports remain officially unidentified largely because the records do not contain enough information to choose between competing explanations. “Unidentified” is an administrative outcome: investigators could not assign a cause from the material available. It does not mean that known sources were eliminated scientifically.

Three missing forms of evidence are especially important.

**Independent position data.** A bearing measured from two separated locations would allow triangulation. Without it, altitude and distance estimates based on eyesight are highly uncertain.

**Instrumental confirmation.** Radar could establish whether a physical object occupied the reported airspace and provide a track, speed and altitude. Murcia’s key files do not preserve matching radar evidence. A lack of radar contact would not automatically disprove a small or distant object, but it removes one of the best ways to distinguish perception from physical movement.

**Permanent visual records.** Photographs or film could be compared with stars, terrain, aircraft routes and known light patterns. No such record anchors the principal San Javier or Mazarrón incidents. The San Javier observers reportedly used binoculars and a theodolite, but the surviving account does not provide a full sequence of recorded angular readings from which the claimed manoeuvres can be reconstructed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://planetabenitez.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/1962-08-06_avistamiento_en_san_javier.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: planetabenitez.com">[Planeta Benitez]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">planetabenitez.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Planeta BenitezAVISTAMIENTO DE FENOMENOS EXTRAÑOS (EXPEDIENTES OVNI)AVISTAMIENTO DE FENOMENOS EXTRAÑOS (EXPEDIENTES OVNI) AVISTAMIENTO DE...</span></span></span>

## The most likely overall reading

No single explanation neatly resolves every reported detail in Murcia. Aircraft lights are particularly plausible around San Javier; bright astronomical objects and autokinetic motion fit stationary lights that later seem to manoeuvre; atmospheric effects can explain pulsing, colour change and disappearance; and vehicle or distant ground lights remain credible in the moving, terrain-filled setting of the Mazarrón exercise.

These explanations can overlap. A witness may first observe an aircraft, later fix attention on a star and interpret atmospheric flicker as continued movement. Several distant lights may be grouped into one perceived object. Once observers believe that a light is nearby and responsive, every disappearance behind terrain or change in relative position may reinforce the impression of deliberate pursuit.

The strongest argument against a routine explanation is the consistency and duration claimed by the witnesses, especially at Mazarrón. The strongest argument against an extraordinary interpretation is the absence of independent physical evidence and the unreliability of unaided night-time distance judgements. Neither side can honestly remove the uncertainty created by the record.

Murcia’s cases are therefore best classified as unresolved but weakly constrained, rather than as confirmed extraordinary craft or fully debunked mistakes. Ordinary lights explain the kinds of effects reported and require no unknown technology. What prevents certainty is not compelling evidence that conventional explanations failed, but the lack of the measurements that would have allowed investigators to test them decisively.
