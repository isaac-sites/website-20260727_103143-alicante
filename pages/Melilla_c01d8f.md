## Introduction

That distinction matters. Melilla occupies an unusually sensitive position beside the Alboran Sea, with an airport, busy maritime routes and a strong military presence. Those features make unusual aerial and radar reports potentially important, but they also create many ordinary sources of misidentification: aircraft, meteors, re-entering space debris, distant lights over water and radar effects. The evidence therefore supports a cautious history of reported phenomena, not a sequence of confirmed unexplained intrusions.

## The small case record behind Melillas reputation

Most published summaries of UFO incidents in Melilla repeat four episodes. The clearest accessible compilation was published in 2021 by mystery writer Jos Manuel Garca Bautista. It is useful as a starting index, but it does not reproduce contemporary newspaper pages, official case files or complete interview transcripts. Its claims must therefore be treated as leads rather than as proof.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://revistavocesdelmisterio.wordpress.com/2021/06/16/ovnis-en-la-historia-de-melilla/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: revistavocesdelmisterio.wordpress.com">[revistavocesdelmisterio.wordpress.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">revistavocesdelmisterio.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">OVN Is en la Historia de Melilla &#124; La esencia misma del Misterio</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OVN Is en la Historia de Melilla &#124; La esencia misma del Misterio</span></span></span>

The first report is dated 4 April 1950. Two witnesses allegedly saw a fast-moving reddish object, described in terms of the apparent size of the opening of a hat, cross the sky without leaving a trail. Little else is available: the witnesses are not named in the surviving online summary, the observation time and viewing direction are absent, and there is no known photograph or instrument record. That makes it an interesting early claim, appearing only three years after the modern flying-saucer era began, but an evidentially weak one.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://revistavocesdelmisterio.wordpress.com/2021/06/16/ovnis-en-la-historia-de-melilla/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: revistavocesdelmisterio.wordpress.com">[revistavocesdelmisterio.wordpress.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">revistavocesdelmisterio.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">OVN Is en la Historia de Melilla &#124; La esencia misma del Misterio</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OVN Is en la Historia de Melilla &#124; La esencia misma del Misterio</span></span></span>

The best-known local episode occurred at about 2 am on 29 December 1976. Accounts describe an intensely bright object travelling from east to west at high speed, producing pale-blue flashes, briefly illuminating the surroundings like daylight and leaving an orange trail. Read alone, those details sound dramatic. ## Why the 1977 sighting remains uncertain

The 22 August 1977 report is harder to settle. Its description includes features often associated with a fireball: rapid movement, intense copper-gold colour, sudden illumination and a luminous tail. The apparently low altitude and estimated diameter of three or four metres are less useful than they appear, because witnesses cannot reliably judge the size or distance of a bright object against a dark sky without a known reference.

The supposed spiral fading of the tail could reflect a persistent meteor train being distorted by upper-atmosphere winds. Bright meteors can also fragment, creating the impression that the main luminous body and its trail are separated. On the other hand, no sufficiently detailed contemporary astronomical analysis has been located that ties this report to a documented meteor, satellite re-entry or aircraft event on that date.

The multiple-witness element improves confidence that something luminous was seen, but it does not by itself identify the cause. Groups viewing the same unfamiliar phenomenon can share the same mistaken impression of distance, size or motion. The report would become much stronger if contemporary press coverage preserved independent descriptions taken before witnesses discussed the event with one another.

The fairest classification is therefore **unresolved but weakly documented**. It is more substantial than a lone anonymous anecdote, yet far short of a case supported by photographs, calibrated observations, military tracking or complete original testimony.

## What Spains official UFO files do and do not establish

Spains Ministry of Defence began declassifying Air Force records concerning strange phenomena in 1991. The official collection now available through the Virtual Defence Library contains 80 files and roughly 1,900 pages covering incidents from 1962 to 1995 in which Air Force personnel or equipment were involved. The files can include summaries, witness interviews, operational reports and meteorological information, although identifying details were removed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es/BVMDefensa/exp_ovni/es/micrositios/inicio.do" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es">[Biblioteca Virtual Defensa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gob.es.</span></span></span>

This archive is valuable because it shows what a properly documented Spanish military UFO case can look like. It also places limits on claims about Melilla. No dedicated Melilla incident comparable to the better-known Air Force case files emerged clearly from the accessible official catalogue during this research. That does not prove that no military report was ever made in the city; records may have been filed under a wider geographical label, retained by another service, lost, or never formally opened. It does mean that Melillas familiar cases should not casually be described as Defence-confirmed UFO incidents.

The *Cadarso* story illustrates the difference. A naval radar contact would fall outside the narrowest Air Force-centred frame of the declassified collection. But without a ships log, incident report or named crew account, there is no reliable way to determine whether the echo represented another vessel, sea clutter, anomalous propagation, a radar malfunction or a genuinely unidentified target.

## Aviation, military activity and the Alboran setting

Melilla is a place where witnesses naturally encounter many lights whose distance is difficult to judge. Aircraft approach and depart over a relatively confined coastal area. Ships and fishing vessels operate beyond the harbour, while lights on the North African coastline can be distorted by haze and atmospheric refraction. Military exercises and patrols add further traffic that may not always be familiar to civilian observers.

Observation over water is especially deceptive. With few fixed reference points, a light may appear stationary when it is approaching directly, or may seem to accelerate when it changes course. Reflections, layers of warm and cool air, and the obscuring of lower parts of an object by haze can produce apparently detached lights or distorted shapes.

Radar reports require equal caution. Radar does not present an infallible picture of solid objects. Sea return, sidelobe effects, ducting caused by atmospheric temperature layers, interference and equipment faults can generate or erase contacts. A radar event becomes persuasive only when investigators preserve the instrument data, technical settings, operator accounts and independent confirmation from visual observation or another sensor.

Melillas military importance may make its UFO stories feel inherently more significant. In evidential terms, however, location is not a substitute for documentation. A story involving a warship or a senior public figure still needs original records and testable details.

## The local connection to Spains wider UFO culture

Melilla also appears at the edge of one of Spains most influential contactee stories: the Ummo affair. During the 1960s and later decades, recipients in Spain and other countries received elaborate letters supposedly written by extraterrestrials from a planet called Ummo. The material became a major strand of Spanish UFO culture, mixing technical claims, philosophy, anonymous correspondence and staged mystery.

A detailed history of the affair records that Eduardo Garca Guirard, a young UFO enthusiast living in Melilla, was apparently the only known Spanish recipient of a microfilmed circular mailed from Berlin in 1969. Most other recipients were in Argentina, France and Italy. This gives Melilla a genuine place in the social history of Spanish ufology, although it is not evidence of an aerial sighting over the city.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ummo-ciencias.org/historia.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ummo-ciencias.org">[ummo-ciencias.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ummo-ciencias.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Web UMMO-Ciencias. The Ummo claims later lost much of their credibility. Jos Luis Jordn Pea, a central figure in the affair, acknowledged responsibility for substantial parts of the deception, while researchers have documented internal inconsistencies, human production methods and competing layers of later imitation. Melillas importance here lies not in extraterrestrial contact, but in showing how UFO ideas circulated far beyond the better-known groups in Madrid and Barcelona.

This distinction helps prevent two different kinds of material from being merged. A reported light over Melilla, a naval radar anecdote and a mysterious letter received by a local enthusiast are all part of UFO history, but they are not the same category of evidence.

## What could change the assessment

Melillas UFO history is not closed, but any stronger conclusion would require better primary material rather than repeated retelling. The most valuable discoveries would be contemporary issues of local newspapers, police or municipal reports from August 1977, personal papers belonging to Juan Ros Garca, or Spanish Navy records identifying the date and circumstances of the *Cadarso* radar contact.

A credible reassessment would need to establish basic facts: exact time, direction, angular elevation, duration, weather, independent witness locations and whether aircraft, meteors or space objects were present. For radar cases, investigators would also need the radar model, range scale, sea state, maintenance status and any visual confirmation.

Until such records appear, Melilla should not be promoted as the site of a landmark proven UFO encounter. Its more defensible importance is different: it offers a compact example of how local UFO history is built from a mixture of sincere observation, spectacular natural events, incomplete archives, military associations and later cultural retelling.

## Conclusion

Melilla has a recognisable but thin UFO record. The dramatic fireball of December 1976 is the most securely understood case and shows how quickly an unfamiliar astronomical event could become an unknown object in the language of the period. The 1977 copper-coloured disc remains unresolved, though its meteor-like features and poor documentation prevent strong claims. The 1950 and 1983 stories are weaker still because their original evidence has not been made readily available.

The provinces real significance lies less in the number of sightings than in its setting. A strategically sensitive coastal city, positioned between busy sea lanes, air traffic and military activity, is naturally fertile ground for ambiguous observations. Melilla also participated in the wider social world of Spanish ufology through the Ummo correspondence. Taken together, the record is historically interesting but evidentially modest: one strongly explained event, several incomplete reports and no persuasive public proof of an extraordinary craft.
