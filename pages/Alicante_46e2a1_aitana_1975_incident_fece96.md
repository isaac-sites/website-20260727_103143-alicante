## Introduction

The 1975 Aitana incident is often described as [Alicante]({{ 'why-alicantes-ufo-files-still-invite/' | relative_url }})’s strongest unresolved UFO case because it joins two forms of testimony that are rarely found together: an unexplained radar return over the Mediterranean and a low-altitude object reportedly seen by several military personnel at the EVA-5 air-surveillance station. The event occurred before dawn on 28 July 1975, with the visual observation usually placed at about 5.30 am.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://alicantepress.com/archive/33209/defensa-investigo-al-menos-8-casos-ovni-en-la-provincia-de-alicante-en-los-anos-70-y-80" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: alicantepress.com">[ALICANTE PRESS periódico digital]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">alicantepress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on alicantepress.com.</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Alicante_46e2a1_aitana_1975_incident_fece96-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for 1975 Aitana" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

That combination makes the case more interesting than a lone report of a distant light. It does not, however, make the incident conclusive. No original radar plot, instrument printout, photograph or complete contemporary investigation is available in the main online UFO archive of the Spanish Ministry of Defence. The surviving story is instead assembled from a brief military summary, later-published institutional material, private investigative files and witness recollections recorded decades afterwards. The result is a genuinely puzzling case, but also one whose evidential strength is sometimes overstated.

## What personnel reported before dawn

EVA-5 stands on the summit of the Sierra de Aitana, 1,558 metres above sea level and facing the Mediterranean. By 1975 it was an established air-defence installation whose personnel monitored and transmitted radar information rather than merely watching the sky casually. The station had been equipped with newer radar systems in 1970, and its operational purpose was the continuous surveillance of military and civilian airspace.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ejercitodelaireydelespacio.defensa.gob.es/EA/ejercitodelaire/es/Unidades/Unidad/edd28d67-7a2d-11ee-99f3-005056bf91c5/?path=%2Fsites%2Finternet.es%2F.content%2Funidad%2Funidad_00004.xml&amp;resourceId=edd28d67-7a2d-11ee-99f3-005056bf91c5" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ejercitodelaireydelespacio.defensa.gob.es">[Ejército del Aire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ejercitodelaireydelespacio.defensa.gob.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Unidades - Unidad...</span></span></span>

The core account is concise. Shortly before the visual sighting, the station reportedly detected an immobile or stationary radar echo over the sea. At about 5.30 am, several non-commissioned officers then observed an object flying at low altitude over the radar installation itself. Later summaries do not agree on every nuance, but they consistently preserve this basic sequence: radar contact offshore, followed by a close visual observation at the mountain station.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://alicantepress.com/archive/33209/defensa-investigo-al-menos-8-casos-ovni-en-la-provincia-de-alicante-en-los-anos-70-y-80" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: alicantepress.com">[ALICANTE PRESS periódico digital]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">alicantepress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on alicantepress.com.</span></span></span>

This sequence matters because it appears to describe more than an ambiguous point of light near the horizon. The reported object crossed the immediate environment of an operating military site and was seen by multiple personnel. A retired Air Force officer, Francisco Miñano, later identified himself publicly as a principal witness and described the episode as a close encounter near the station’s radar antennas. His testimony gives the event a named participant rather than leaving it as an entirely anonymous legend.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.orm.es/programas/elultimopeldano/testimonio-encuentro-cercano-en-la-base-de-aitana-ovnis-en-instalaciones-militares/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: orm.es">[Onda Regional de Murcia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">orm.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on orm.es.</span></span></span>

There are nevertheless limits to what can safely be said about the witnesses. Public summaries do not provide a complete list of their names, exact positions, viewing directions or separate statements. The Ministry of Defence’s wider UFO collection normally removes the identities of declarants and reporting officers, but the 1975 Aitana material has an additional problem: it was not processed into the central archive in the same way as many other cases.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es/BVMDefensa/exp_ovni/es/micrositios/inicio.do" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es">[Biblioteca Virtual Defensa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gob.es.</span></span></span>

## How the radar and visual accounts align

At first glance, the radar and visual reports appear mutually reinforcing. A radar operator detects something over the sea; soon afterwards, personnel see an object over the station. If both observations followed the same target continuously, the case would represent a valuable example of independent sensor and human confirmation.

The surviving records do not establish that continuity. They do not show a preserved track linking the offshore echo to the object seen above the mountain. No publicly available material supplies the target’s range, altitude, bearing, speed, radar cross-section or movement between successive sweeps. Even the description of the return as stationary is known mainly through retrospective summaries rather than an accessible original radar record.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://alicantepress.com/archive/33209/defensa-investigo-al-menos-8-casos-ovni-en-la-provincia-de-alicante-en-los-anos-70-y-80" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: alicantepress.com">[ALICANTE PRESS periódico digital]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">alicantepress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on alicantepress.com.</span></span></span>

This distinction is crucial. Two unusual events occurring close together may be related, but timing alone cannot prove that the radar and witnesses observed the same physical object. The radar return might have disappeared before the visual report, or it might have represented a separate aircraft, ship, atmospheric effect or technical artefact. Conversely, an unidentified aircraft could conceivably have produced both observations. The surviving documentation is insufficient to choose confidently between those possibilities.

The location nevertheless gives the report greater weight than an ordinary civilian sighting. EVA-5 personnel worked in air surveillance, and the station’s task depended on recognising routine traffic and transmitting radar data accurately. The unit’s official history also records the long operating life and reliability of its height-finding radar equipment. That background supports the witnesses’ general competence, although equipment reliability in normal service cannot prove that every individual radar return represented a solid airborne target.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ejercitodelaireydelespacio.defensa.gob.es/EA/ejercitodelaire/es/Unidades/Unidad/edd28d67-7a2d-11ee-99f3-005056bf91c5/?path=%2Fsites%2Finternet.es%2F.content%2Funidad%2Funidad_00004.xml&amp;resourceId=edd28d67-7a2d-11ee-99f3-005056bf91c5" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ejercitodelaireydelespacio.defensa.gob.es">[Ejército del Aire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ejercitodelaireydelespacio.defensa.gob.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Unidades - Unidad...</span></span></span>

The strongest defensible formulation is therefore that a radar anomaly and a multi-witness visual observation were reported within the same early-morning episode. Calling it a fully correlated radar-visual track goes beyond the surviving evidence.

## The missing contemporary case file

The history of the paperwork is one of the most important—and least understood—parts of the Aitana case. Spain’s Air Force began assembling and declassifying its UFO records in the early 1990s. The resulting Ministry of Defence collection contains 80 files and about 1,900 pages covering incidents in which Air Force personnel or equipment were involved between 1962 and 1995. Individual files may contain summaries, witness interviews, operational reports and meteorological information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es/BVMDefensa/exp_ovni/es/micrositios/inicio.do" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es">[Biblioteca Virtual Defensa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gob.es.</span></span></span>

The 1975 Aitana incident did not enter that archive as a normal, self-contained case file. Researcher Vicente-Juan Ballester Olmos, who assisted closely with the declassification process, later wrote that the central Air Force records contained no reference to the incident when searches were made in the early 1990s. A 1992 request to the relevant regional command produced no recorded response, while EVA-5 stated in 1994 that it held no information from 1975 or 1979.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.academia.edu/31802317/EVA_5_Los_informes_perdidos_de_1975" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academia.edu">[Academia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">EVA 5 Los informes perdidos de 1975</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Academia(PDF) EVA-5: Los informes perdidos de 1975...</span></span></span>

This absence initially encouraged suspicions that significant material had been withheld. A more mundane archival explanation eventually emerged. According to correspondence described by Ballester Olmos, historical documents concerning Aitana sightings had been published by the Ministry of Defence in the 2001 institutional history *Kansas/Alcoy 1957–2000*. “Kansas” was the military code name associated with the radar installation. The records had therefore survived in a unit history or local documentary channel, but had not been forwarded into the central UFO archive during the declassification searches.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.academia.edu/31802317/EVA_5_Los_informes_perdidos_de_1975" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academia.edu">[Academia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">EVA 5 Los informes perdidos de 1975</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Academia(PDF) EVA-5: Los informes perdidos de 1975...</span></span></span>

That finding weakens claims of a deliberate cover-up, but it also exposes how fragmentary the record is. The absence of a central file means readers cannot inspect the kind of evidential package available in better-documented Spanish military cases. There is no easily accessible set of original witness interviews, radar logs, weather data and investigator conclusions tied together under a 1975 Aitana case number.

Ballester Olmos reported possessing a substantial private case file, including detailed questionnaires completed by witnesses. That claim suggests that more contemporary evidence may once have existed outside the official central collection. However, private investigative holdings are not equivalent to a fully catalogued public archive, particularly when readers cannot independently compare every questionnaire with the original operational records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.academia.edu/31802317/EVA_5_Los_informes_perdidos_de_1975" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academia.edu">[Academia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">EVA 5 Los informes perdidos de 1975</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Academia(PDF) EVA-5: Los informes perdidos de 1975...</span></span></span>

## What the later testimony adds

Francisco Miñano’s public account has revived interest in the case. In a 2024 radio interview, he was presented as a retired Air Force commander and a principal participant in the encounter near the antennas. This is useful because it confirms that at least one identifiable former officer continued to stand by the broad account decades later.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.orm.es/programas/elultimopeldano/testimonio-encuentro-cercano-en-la-base-de-aitana-ovnis-en-instalaciones-militares/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: orm.es">[Onda Regional de Murcia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">orm.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on orm.es.</span></span></span>

Later testimony can preserve details omitted from a brief official summary, particularly the appearance, position and behaviour of an object. It can also indicate whether a witness regarded the event as an ordinary aircraft encounter or something markedly unfamiliar. Miñano’s willingness to discuss the incident publicly therefore strengthens the case that the visual report was not simply invented by later writers.

It does not solve the main evidential problems. The interview took place nearly 49 years after the event and was broadcast within a programme devoted to mysteries and UFOs. Human memory is reconstructive: over decades, details can be influenced by retelling, discussion with other witnesses, published accounts and later interpretations. A retrospective interview should consequently be treated as supporting testimony, not as a replacement for a statement written immediately after the event.

The programme’s description also uses more dramatic language than the surviving military summary, claiming that the full event was substantially more striking than the short record suggests. That may be true, but the additional claims should be separated from the securely documented minimum: a radar echo was reported over the sea, and several non-commissioned officers reportedly saw an object pass at low altitude over EVA-5.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.orm.es/programas/elultimopeldano/testimonio-encuentro-cercano-en-la-base-de-aitana-ovnis-en-instalaciones-militares/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: orm.es">[Onda Regional de Murcia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">orm.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on orm.es.</span></span></span>

Later reporting has therefore strengthened the human side of the story by identifying a witness and keeping the event open to scrutiny. It has not strengthened the technical evidence, because no radar plots, photographs or newly authenticated operational logs have accompanied the later testimony.

## Ordinary explanations and their limits

Several conventional possibilities must be considered, even though none has been demonstrated conclusively.

**An aircraft or helicopter** is the most obvious explanation for a low-flying object over a military station. At pre-dawn hours, navigation lights, changing viewing angles and mountain terrain can distort impressions of shape, distance and speed. Yet a flight close to an air-surveillance facility should, in principle, have been more readily recognised or recorded. The available summaries do not say whether scheduled flights, military exercises or local helicopter activity were checked.

**A radar anomaly** could account for the offshore echo. Radar systems can display returns caused by anomalous atmospheric propagation, sea clutter, side-lobe effects, birds, weather or equipment behaviour. A stationary return over the sea is not automatically evidence of a hovering craft. Without information about the radar mode, persistence, range, altitude and confirmation by another station, the return cannot be assessed technically.

**Astronomical or atmospheric light** might explain a distant visual observation, especially around dawn. This becomes less persuasive if the object genuinely passed close above the radar site and was seen by several personnel from different positions. Unfortunately, the public record does not preserve enough geometry to determine whether “over the installation” was a measured location or a visual impression.

**A classified or unreported military operation** is possible in principle because EVA-5 was part of Spain’s air-defence network. This explanation is sometimes invoked too easily: the absence of public identification is not itself evidence of secret technology. No known documentation presently links the incident to a specific exercise, aircraft or foreign operation.

These explanations remain possibilities rather than solutions because the investigation record is incomplete. A convincing conventional identification would need to fit both the time and location of the radar echo and the object described by the witnesses. No such identification has emerged in the publicly accessible material.

## What evidence would settle the case

The Aitana incident remains unresolved largely because the evidence needed to test it is missing rather than because the surviving data demonstrate extraordinary performance. Several records would materially change the assessment:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* the original operations log for the night of 27–28 July 1975;
* separate, dated statements from each witness;
* radar plots or records showing range, altitude and movement;
* confirmation from another radar station or air-traffic control centre;
* local weather and atmospheric-propagation data;
* lists of military and civilian flights near Aitana;
* a precise reconstruction of where each witness stood and what direction each person faced.

</div>

If these materials showed a continuous radar track moving from the sea to the station, independent confirmation by another sensor and consistent witness descriptions of a structured object, the case would become substantially stronger. If they showed that the radar return was intermittent, geographically unrelated or typical of known propagation effects, the connection between the two observations would weaken sharply.

Modern investigation standards emphasise simultaneous measurements from several independent instruments precisely because a single sensor can mislead. Cameras, calibrated radar data, environmental readings and triangulation allow investigators to distinguish a physical target from optical or electronic artefacts. The Aitana case has the outline of such a multi-sensor event, but not the preserved measurements needed to verify one.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18566" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Scientific Investigation of Unidentified Aerial Phenomena (UAP) Using Multimodal Ground-Based ObservatoriesMay 29, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 29, 2023</span></span></span>

## Was Alicante’s strongest case truly unexplained?

Yes, in the limited sense that no publicly demonstrated explanation accounts for the entire reported episode. The date, approximate time, military setting, radar anomaly and multiple visual witnesses are supported by overlapping institutional, journalistic and testimonial sources. The event also appears in a documentary trail connected to the Air Force, even though bureaucratic failures kept it outside the normal central file sequence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.academia.edu/31802317/EVA_5_Los_informes_perdidos_de_1975" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academia.edu">[Academia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">EVA 5 Los informes perdidos de 1975</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Academia(PDF) EVA-5: Los informes perdidos de 1975...</span></span></span>

But “unexplained” should not be confused with “inexplicable”. The evidence does not establish the object’s dimensions, altitude, speed, composition or origin. Nor does it prove that the radar return and visual object were the same target. The lack of an ordinary identification may reflect the disappearance of key records as much as the unusual nature of the event itself.

Within Alicante’s UFO history, the 1975 Aitana incident deserves its leading position because it arose at an operational radar station and involved several military witnesses. Its enduring value is as a case study in both promising evidence and archival weakness. It is stronger than an unsupported local legend, weaker than a fully documented radar-visual interception, and best classified as unresolved because the surviving record is too incomplete for either a confident debunking or an extraordinary conclusion.
