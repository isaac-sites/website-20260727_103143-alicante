## Introduction

The strongest conventional reading of the Manises incident is not that one ordinary light explains every report. It is that several unrelated lights were combined into a single apparent pursuit: distant industrial flames seen from the airliner, bright astronomical objects noticed from Manises and by the Mirage pilot, and severe night-time errors in judging distance and movement. This interpretation matters to [Albacete]({{ 'why-albacete-became-central-to-spains/' | relative_url }}) because the fighter launched from [Los Llanos]({{ 'los-llanos/' | relative_url }}) Air Base, yet the dramatic “chase” may have developed from visual targets that were never physically close to the aircraft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/reports/791111_ejufoas_manises.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[nicap.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Juan Antonio Fernández Peris, from Valenc</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Source details in endnotes.</span></span></span>

That explanation is plausible rather than proven. The surviving record does not contain calibrated photographs, continuous radar tracking or precise sightline measurements for every phase. The witnesses also rejected parts of the sceptical reconstruction. Even so, ordinary-light hypotheses fit several awkward features of the case better than the idea of one solid vehicle travelling from the Balearic area to Valencia and then responding intelligently to an interceptor.

## Industrial flames near Escombreras

The leading explanation for the two red lights first seen from the passenger aircraft points towards the industrial complex at Escombreras, near Cartagena. Juan Antonio Fernández Peris, who studied the case over many years and made extensive use of the declassified Air Force file, argued that the aircraft’s position and reported viewing direction aligned closely with the refinery area. Its flare towers burned waste gases and could produce powerful orange-red flames visible from a considerable distance over the dark Mediterranean.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/reports/791111_ejufoas_manises.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[nicap.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Juan Antonio Fernández Peris, from Valenc</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Source details in endnotes.</span></span></span>

The proposed range was far greater than the crew believed. This is not automatically fatal to the explanation. Over water at night, a bright source may have no visible ground reference beneath it, no recognisable skyline around it and no obvious clue to its true scale. A pair of flames separated within an industrial site can therefore appear as two lights suspended in the sky. Changes in flame height and brightness may also give them a pulsing or unstable appearance rather than the steady look expected from fixed ground lighting.

The reconstruction becomes more persuasive when the aircraft’s movement is considered. A distant source changes its bearing only slowly as an aircraft travels, particularly when both are separated by well over 100 kilometres. The crew may therefore have continued to see the lights in roughly the same sector while altering course and altitude. Without a reliable distance estimate, that persistence can be interpreted as the lights maintaining formation with the aircraft rather than remaining fixed on the distant coast. Fernández Peris treated this geometrical match as the starting point of the incident rather than as an explanation invented solely from the colour of the lights.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/reports/791111_ejufoas_manises.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[nicap.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Juan Antonio Fernández Peris, from Valenc</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Source details in endnotes.</span></span></span>

Atmospheric conditions could have strengthened the effect. The sceptical reconstruction refers to a marked temperature inversion, in which layers of air at different temperatures bend light unusually near the horizon. Such refraction can increase the apparent visibility of distant sources, distort their shape and make their brightness fluctuate. It need not lift a refinery into a sharply defined image; it only has to help isolated flames remain conspicuous across an unexpectedly long line of sight.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.academia.edu/44150004/LAS_BALIZAS_DEL_11_DE_NOVIEMBRE_DE_1979" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academia.edu">[Academia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">LAS BALIZAS DEL 11 DE NOVIEMBRE DE 1979</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ballester Olmos, J. ### The main objections

The Escombreras theory is not a complete demonstration. Critics have argued that terrain near the refinery may have blocked or reduced the relevant sightline, depending on the aircraft’s exact position and altitude. They also note that the crew described lights apparently approaching from the left and believed they changed in response to the aircraft. Small errors in reconstructing the route, viewing angle or time can therefore make a large difference to whether Escombreras truly lay where the witnesses were looking.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.academia.edu/103909911/Expediente_Manises_El_terreno_alrededor_de_Escombreras" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academia.edu">[Academia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PDF) Expediente Manises: El terreno alrededor de Escombreras</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Academia(PDF) Expediente Manises: El terreno alrededor de Escombreras...</span></span></span>

Experience is another objection. Pilots involved in the case said they were familiar with coastal industrial installations and did not recognise the lights as refinery flames. That testimony deserves weight, but familiarity does not guarantee identification when a known source is seen from an unusual bearing, through distorted air and without the surrounding landscape that normally reveals what it is. The relevant question is not whether the crew knew Escombreras existed, but whether the visual information available that night was sufficient to identify it.

The industrial-flame hypothesis is therefore strongest as an explanation of the initial red pair, not as an all-purpose answer to every later light. Attempts to make the same flames account for observations from Manises, the fighter’s changing headings and every reported manoeuvre create unnecessary strain. The more defensible sceptical position is that the original airliner sighting and the later interception phase involved different sources.

## Stars, planets and atmospheric distortion

After the passenger aircraft landed, people at Manises and the Mirage pilot reported further bright lights. Sceptical investigators have proposed that at least some of these were stars or planets seen under heightened expectation. Vega, one of the brightest stars in the northern sky, has featured prominently in reconstructions of the fighter phase. Other accounts have suggested that Jupiter or distant industrial lighting on the North African coast may have contributed at different points in the flight.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.informeovni.net/articulos/elexpedientemanises.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: informeovni.net">[informeovni.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">informeovni.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">El Expediente Manises&quot;: un análisis crítico</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">El Expediente Manises&quot;: un análisis crítico</span></span></span>

This sounds implausible only if a star is imagined as an obviously fixed point on a labelled sky map. From a fast aircraft at night, the observer sees a bright light through a moving canopy, while turning, climbing and changing heading. The horizon may be indistinct or absent. A star’s position relative to the cockpit frame can then alter markedly even though its position relative to the landscape does not. If the pilot turns towards it, it remains ahead; if he climbs or banks, it appears to shift vertically or laterally.

Atmospheric scintillation adds further confusion. A bright star close to the horizon passes through a much greater thickness of turbulent air than one high overhead. Its light may flicker, change colour and appear to jump slightly. These effects are particularly noticeable for brilliant isolated objects and can make them look more active than ordinary stars. Refraction can also displace their apparent position, although it cannot make a star carry out the large physical manoeuvres sometimes attributed to the Manises lights.

Night-flying guidance treats such misperceptions as an operational hazard rather than a sign of poor training. The US Federal Aviation Administration describes autokinesis as the illusion in which a stationary light against a featureless dark background appears to move, sometimes towards the aircraft. It specifically lists stars, planets and aircraft lights as possible triggers and notes that the absence of fixed visual references makes the light’s position difficult for the brain to judge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.faasafety.gov/files/events/SO/SO15/2024/SO15134204/YourSensesInTheShadows.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faasafety.gov">[faasafety.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faasafety.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">FAA Safety</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FAA Safety</span></span></span>

That mechanism is highly relevant to the Mirage sortie from Albacete. Captain Fernando Cámara had been launched to find an unidentified target after an airliner diversion and reports from Manises. Once airborne over a dark Mediterranean landscape, any unusually bright light in the expected search area carried immediate significance. The hypothesis does not require panic or incompetence. It requires only a skilled pilot applying attention to a difficult visual target in conditions known to produce false motion.

Astronomical reconstructions remain disputed because the reported directions and times are not always consistent enough to identify a specific object beyond doubt. Some critics have calculated that certain planets were already low or below the relevant horizon during parts of the incident. This weakens loose claims that “the planets” explain everything, but it does not eliminate bright stars from other bearings or at later points in the fighter’s route. The correct test must be target by target and minute by minute, rather than a general appeal to astronomy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://expmanises.blogspot.com/p/caso-aeropuerto-de-manises.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: expmanises.blogspot.com">[Expediente Manises]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">expmanises.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">CASO AEROPUERTO de MANISES. ## Why distance errors create apparent pursuit

A point of light has almost no visible scale. Unless its true size is known, brightness alone cannot reveal whether it is a small nearby lamp or a powerful source far away. Two lights with the same apparent brightness can differ enormously in both size and distance. This is why witnesses can describe a light as hundreds of metres away even when no independent measurement supports that estimate.

The Manises accounts often convert apparent angular changes directly into speed. If a light seems to move across part of the windscreen in a few seconds and is assumed to be nearby, the implied speed may be modest. If the same light is assumed to be kilometres away, the calculated speed becomes enormous. But the calculation has no firm foundation until range is known. Night observations of isolated lights rarely provide that range.

Parallax is central to the illusion. Nearby objects appear to shift substantially against a distant background when the observer moves; very distant sources barely shift. In darkness, however, the background may be invisible. The observer cannot easily tell whether a small angular change comes from movement of the light, movement of the aircraft or a turn of the observer’s head. A distant flare or star can consequently appear to follow, retreat or match course.

Course changes may reinforce the impression. When an aircraft turns towards a distant light, the light stays near the nose because the pilot is continually correcting towards it. If the distance does not seem to close, the natural interpretation may be that the target is moving away at comparable speed. In reality, a star is unreachable and a refinery flame may be far beyond the expected search zone. The “pursuit” is created by the observer’s changing heading rather than by the source fleeing.

This helps explain one of the central puzzles of the Mirage flight: the pilot reportedly approached lights several times but could not reach them. A genuinely nearby object would normally show increasing angular size, changing detail or measurable closure. A remote light can remain bright yet indefinitely distant. Running low on fuel after repeated attempts is therefore compatible with chasing successive fixed or extremely distant sources.

The same reasoning applies to the airliner crew’s sense that the red lights approached and then followed them. If the crew first noticed fluctuating lights without knowing their range, changes in brightness could be read as changes in distance. A flare that brightened might seem to advance; one that dimmed might seem to fall back. An alteration in the aircraft’s orientation could move the lights across the cockpit view and create the appearance of a response.

## One object or several mistaken lights?

Popular retellings tend to join the entire sequence into one coherent encounter: lights approach the passenger aircraft, accompany it to Valencia, appear over the airport and then evade the Mirage launched from Albacete. The conventional reconstruction challenges that continuity. It treats the case as a chain of observations connected by expectation and narrative, not necessarily by one physical object.

The sequence can be divided into three broad groups:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **The initial red pair:** potentially flare towers or other industrial flames in the Escombreras area.
* **Lights seen from Manises:** possibly bright stars, planets, conventional aircraft or distant ground sources noticed after the airport had been alerted.
* **Targets pursued by the Mirage:** potentially more than one astronomical or terrestrial light acquired on different headings during a long night sortie.

</div>

Fernández Peris’s published summary explicitly presented the event as a complex combination of separate misidentifications. A later study by several Spanish investigators likewise argued that the red lights observed by the airliner probably came from Escombreras, while subsequent airport and fighter observations were more likely astronomical.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/reports/791111_ejufoas_manises.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[nicap.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Juan Antonio Fernández Peris, from Valenc</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Source details in endnotes.</span></span></span>

This “several lights” model has an important advantage: it does not have to explain how one object occupied incompatible directions or remained visible to observers separated by changing geography. It also fits the way incidents grow operationally. The first crew reports an unexplained light; controllers and airport staff begin searching; the interceptor is told where unusual activity is expected; ordinary lights that would otherwise be ignored acquire significance.

Expectation does not mean fabrication. Each witness may have reported honestly what appeared unusual from their own position. The mistake can occur later, when separate sightings are woven into a single continuous track. Once that narrative is established, discrepancies are often interpreted as extraordinary manoeuvres rather than as evidence that observers were looking at different things.

## What the conventional account still struggles to explain

The sceptical interpretation weakens the case for an exotic vehicle, but it does not settle every detail. The strongest unresolved issue is the precision of the original sightlines. The published reconstructions rely on times, bearings and route information that are not always as exact as their diagrams suggest. A small change in the aircraft’s location or viewing direction may affect whether the Escombreras alignment remains convincing.

The reports of radar contacts also require caution. Popular versions describe large objects tracked independently by radar, but the available case material does not provide a clean, continuous radar plot linking the airliner sighting, Manises and the Mirage. Intermittent or ambiguous returns may arise from weather, clutter, aircraft or processing effects. Without preserved technical data, they cannot securely confirm either a physical unknown craft or a particular conventional light source.

The Mirage pilot also reported an onboard warning associated with radar illumination or electronic interference. That episode is adjacent to, but not identical with, the question of what the visible lights were. A warning-system activation would not prove that the light being chased caused it. Conversely, explaining the visible target as Vega or a distant industrial source does not by itself explain the cockpit indication. The events may have been coincidental, misinterpreted or linked to another military emitter, but the surviving public evidence is insufficient to choose confidently between those possibilities.

Witness disagreement must also remain visible. Captain Cámara rejected the idea that he was simply chasing Escombreras and emphasised that at least one light appeared in a different direction. That objection actually supports the more careful sceptical model: the fighter need not have been following the same industrial source seen by the passenger crew. ## The best-supported assessment

Conventional explanations account for much of the reported behaviour without requiring a craft capable of extreme speed or intelligent evasion. Distant industrial flames provide a plausible source for the original red lights; bright stars and perhaps other distant ground lights offer plausible targets during the airport and fighter phases; and well-documented night-vision effects explain why stationary or remote sources can appear to approach, follow or flee.

The evidence is strongest at the level of mechanism and weakest at the level of exact identification. It is well established that pilots can misjudge isolated lights at night, including stars, planets and ground lights. It is reasonably argued that Escombreras lay close to the relevant visual line from the airliner. It is less securely demonstrated that every reported light can be assigned to a named source at a precise minute.

For Albacete’s UFO history, this distinction is decisive. The Los Llanos scramble was real, and the pilot’s prolonged search was real. Neither fact proves that an extraordinary vehicle was present. The most economical reading is that the Air Force responded properly to a genuine uncertainty, then encountered the same limitations that had affected the civilian crew: darkness, distant lights, uncertain range and an expectation that one elusive target was somewhere ahead.

The Manises mystery therefore survives mainly because the record is incomplete, not because conventional explanations have failed in principle. Later analysis has weakened the image of one object continuously pursuing an airliner and outmanoeuvring a Mirage. What remains is a complicated aviation incident in which several ordinary lights may have been misidentified under unusually difficult conditions, with some technical details still unresolved.
