## Introduction

[Girona]({{ 'gironas-ufo-cases-solved-weak-and/' | relative_url }})’s declassified UFO files can prove that unusual aerial reports were made, recorded and sometimes assessed by the Spanish Air Force. They can show what witnesses said, which checks were attempted, what information reached military authorities and whether officials found a conventional explanation. They cannot, by themselves, prove that an extraordinary craft existed, still less that it came from another world.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Girona_1912ef_girona_declassified_1bb91c-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Official Files" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

That distinction matters because three labels are often confused. **Declassified** means a document no longer requires restricted handling. **Unidentified** means the available information did not establish an object’s identity. **Unexplained** usually means investigators did not reach a satisfactory explanation. None of these terms is a scientific finding about extraterrestrial origin. Girona’s records are most valuable when read as an uneven administrative archive: one airport report was identified as balloons, one elaborate civilian account was barely investigated, and one military-base sighting remained unresolved despite the location’s surveillance role.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es/BVMDefensa/exp_ovni/es/micrositios/inicio.do" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es">[gob.es]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gob.es.</span></span></span>

## Why the Air Force collected unusual reports

The Spanish Air Force did not create UFO files because it had accepted that alien vehicles were visiting Spain. From 1968, its instructions encouraged members of the public to report apparent unidentified flying objects to air or local authorities, while centralising the resulting information within the Air Staff. The purpose was to conduct appropriate enquiries, gather reports in one place and distinguish potentially relevant airspace incidents from sensational press stories or ordinary misidentifications.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/SpanishUFOFiles/normativa_ovni_%2808-11-1996%29_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">normativa ovni (08 11 1996) djvu.txt</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet ArchiveFull text of &quot;Spanish UFO Files&quot;de 1996 EL OFICIAL DE INTELIGENCIA DEL M.O.A., DESCLASIFICADO REFERENCIA SESPA 0001 ANEXO...</span></span></span>

That mission was practical. An unexplained light near an airport, flight route or military installation could represent several things of legitimate concern: an aircraft whose identity was not yet known, a balloon, an astronomical object, an atmospheric effect, an inaccurate report or, in principle, an intrusion into controlled airspace. Authorities did not need to believe an extraordinary explanation before opening a file. They needed only enough uncertainty to justify recording or checking the event.

The files therefore prove institutional attention, not institutional endorsement. A witness statement could enter the system even when it contained no photograph, radar return or physical trace. Some files consist mainly of correspondence and descriptions supplied by the observer. Others include statements from military personnel or enquiries through operational channels. Their presence in the archive reflects a reporting procedure whose evidential quality varied greatly from case to case.

Spain’s secrecy law reinforces this point. Material could be classified when unauthorised disclosure might endanger national security or defence. Classification was therefore concerned with sensitivity, access and protection—not with whether every claim contained in a document was true. Removing that protection later says that secrecy is no longer considered necessary; it does not retroactively validate the report’s contents.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.boe.es/buscar/pdf/1968/BOE-A-1968-444-consolidado.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: boe.es">[BOE]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">boe.es</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Este princ</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ley 9/1968, de 5 de abril, sobre secretos oficiales.declarado en nuestras Leyes Fundamentales, la publicidad de la actividad de los Ór...</span></span></span>

## Declassified does not mean confirmed

The Spanish programme that opened the UFO files was an archival and administrative process. A physical copy of the collection was deposited in the Air Force Central Library in 1992, and the digitised material is now available through the Ministry of Defence’s virtual library. The surviving collection covers reports assembled over several decades and released in stages during the 1990s.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es/BVMDefensa/exp_ovni/es/micrositios/inicio.do" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es">[gob.es]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gob.es.</span></span></span>

The wording used in individual files is revealing. Declassification recommendations commonly state that officials found no reason to maintain the material as classified. In the 1982 Blanes file, for example, the recommendation concerns whether the dossier should remain protected, not whether the witness had accurately identified a structured flying machine. The same file explicitly notes that no subsequent investigation or continuing correspondence with the witness was recorded.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/SpanishUFOFiles/1982-08-19_avistamiento_en_blanes_%28gerona%29_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

This creates a crucial separation between two questions:

1. **Can the public now inspect the file?**
2. **Does the file contain enough reliable evidence to establish what happened?**

Declassification answers the first. It may leave the second completely open.

The release process nevertheless improved UFO research in a meaningful way. It allowed readers to compare later retellings with the documents actually held by the Air Force. Dates, witness descriptions, official summaries and investigative gaps became checkable. Claims that “the military confirmed” a particular incident can therefore be tested against the file rather than repeated from memory or sensational reporting.

## Three Girona files, three different outcomes

Girona’s best-known official reports are useful because they do not all end with the same label. Taken together, they show why “UFO file” is a category of records, not a category of conclusions.

**Girona airport, 1969: identified.** Airport personnel reported two bright objects at high altitude but concluded that they were French meteorological balloons. This is evidence that the reporting system could record a puzzling observation and still reach an ordinary explanation. It also demonstrates how professional aviation experience can help without making witnesses infallible: the objects were initially notable enough to report, yet familiar enough in appearance and movement to identify.

The case weakens the popular assumption that declassified files preserve only mysteries. In fact, an identified report may be more evidentially useful than a dramatic unresolved one because it shows how an apparent anomaly can emerge from distance, lighting and limited visual cues.

**Blanes, 1982: detailed but poorly tested.** A resident described a large rotating, disc-like object moving along the coast and said seven other people were called outside to see it. His written submission included drawings, estimated dimensions and extensive speculation about the object’s structure and technology. The official summary preserved this material but recorded no subsequent investigation and apparently no further correspondence with the witness.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/stream/SpanishUFOFiles/1982-08-19_avistamiento_en_blanes_%28gerona%29_djvu.txt" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

The file proves that the Air Force received a detailed report. It does not prove the witness’s estimates of size, distance or construction. Without interviews with the other observers, aircraft and balloon checks, astronomical comparisons, meteorological data or instrument readings, later researchers cannot reliably reconstruct the object’s position or speed. The elaborate description may make the case memorable, but descriptive richness is not the same as independent corroboration.

**Roses, 1991: military witnesses, no decisive identification.** At the EVA-4 air-surveillance station near Roses, a security commander reported seeing a luminous spherical object circling above the main building in the early hours of 13 September. Other personnel were involved in the observation, and the case remained unidentified in the surviving record. The file was declassified in February 1996.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufotransparency.com/files/intl-es-expediente-eva-4-rosas-1991-1991-09-13-avistamiento-en-eva-4-rosas-gerona" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufotransparency.com">[UFO Transparency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufotransparency.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ufotransparency.com.</span></span></span>

The military setting increases the case’s interest because the witnesses were at a facility associated with watching the airspace. It does not automatically increase the quality of every claimed measurement. Visual estimates of an object’s radius, height and flight path remain estimates unless supported by triangulation, calibrated imagery or sensor data. Most importantly, the absence of a confirming radar return prevents the location itself from functioning as instrument-based proof.

The Roses file can therefore establish that personnel at a radar station reported something they could not identify visually. It cannot establish that the station’s radar detected a solid craft performing the described movements.

## Identified, unresolved and poorly investigated

Readers often treat “unresolved” as the strongest possible official category. In practice, it may conceal very different evidential situations.

An **identified case** is one in which investigators or informed witnesses found a plausible source that fitted the observation. Girona’s 1969 balloon report belongs here. Identification does not mean every visual detail was measured perfectly; it means the available evidence supported a sufficiently convincing ordinary explanation.

A genuinely **unresolved case** has survived meaningful checks without a secure identification. The strongest version would include timely witness interviews, precise geometry, multiple independent viewpoints, photographs or video with metadata, weather and astronomical comparisons, aircraft records and corroborating sensors. Even then, “unresolved” would mean only that the cause remained undetermined.

A **poorly investigated case** may also remain unidentified, but for a weaker reason: essential checks were never made, were not documented or can no longer be reconstructed. Blanes is the clearest Girona example. Its unexplained status reflects missing investigation as much as resistance to explanation.

This difference prevents a common statistical mistake. A collection of unresolved files cannot be treated as a collection of equally strong anomalies. One case may lack information; another may contain contradictory testimony; another may have good witnesses but no instrument confirmation; a fourth may have been carefully checked and still resist explanation. Counting all four under one label hides the evidential distinctions that matter most.

## What the archive preserves—and what it loses

Official files are strongest when used to establish documentary facts. They can normally tell the reader:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* when and where a report was logged;
* who submitted it or which authority forwarded it;
* how the object was described at the time;
* which sketches, letters or statements entered the dossier;
* what enquiries were recorded;
* whether officials proposed identification, closure or declassification.

</div>

They become much weaker when used to prove an object’s physical properties. Witnesses commonly estimate height, distance, size and speed without a known reference point. At night, a bright object may appear closer or larger than it is. An angular movement across the sky cannot be converted into a reliable speed unless distance is independently known. A drawing records how the witness remembered or interpreted an appearance; it is not equivalent to a photograph.

Time also degrades what can be checked. Weather records, aircraft movements and astronomical positions may sometimes be reconstructed, but missing interview notes, unnamed corroborating witnesses and undocumented enquiries cannot simply be recreated decades later. Declassification improves access to whatever survived; it cannot restore information that investigators never collected.

Modern proposals for serious study emphasise precisely the measurements missing from most historical files: synchronised cameras, triangulation, radar-derived range and motion, multispectral observations, acoustic measurements and environmental sensors. Such methods are designed to separate genuine physical anomalies from optical effects, sensor artefacts and mistaken distance estimates. By those standards, the Girona records are primarily historical case documents rather than complete scientific datasets.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18566" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on arxiv.org.</span></span></span>

## The strongest claims the files support

Girona’s declassified material supports several firm but limited conclusions.

First, people in the province—including airport staff, civilians and military personnel—reported aerial phenomena they initially found unusual. Those reports were not all invented later by newspapers or UFO writers; documentary records show that some reached official channels.

Second, the Air Force’s conclusions were not uniform. At least one Girona report was given a conventional identification, while others remained unresolved or insufficiently investigated. The archive therefore does not present a single official narrative of extraordinary craft.

Third, witness status matters less than the quality of corroboration. Aviation or military experience may make a report more worthy of examination, but it cannot substitute for reliable range, imagery or sensor confirmation. The Roses case illustrates this especially clearly: a sighting at a radar installation is not the same as a radar sighting.

Fourth, administrative labels describe the state of a file, not the nature of the object. “Declassified” concerns public access. “Unidentified” concerns the limits of identification. Neither term means “alien”, “advanced technology” or “confirmed vehicle”.

Finally, the files reveal weaknesses in the historical investigation system itself. The Blanes dossier preserves a striking story but also documents the absence of the follow-up needed to test it. That omission is evidence—not evidence for an extraordinary object, but evidence that some potentially important reports were archived without being adequately examined.

## A careful verdict on Girona’s official files

The declassified records make Girona’s UFO history more transparent, but not more extraterrestrial. They prove that reports were collected and that authorities sometimes took them seriously enough to document, circulate or review. They also show that ordinary objects could trigger official attention and that some unresolved cases remained unresolved because the evidence was incomplete.

The airport balloon identification is the clearest positive conclusion in the provincial record. Blanes shows how a detailed narrative can outrun the investigation supporting it. Roses provides a more credible institutional setting and multiple witnesses, yet still lacks the kind of instrument confirmation needed to demonstrate a solid craft with extraordinary performance.

The fairest description is therefore not that Girona’s files disclose hidden proof, nor that every witness was mistaken. They disclose the limits of the surviving evidence. Some events can be plausibly identified, some cannot now be reconstructed, and a small residue remains genuinely uncertain. That uncertainty is historically interesting and worthy of careful study, but it is not proof of an extraterrestrial presence.

    return Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]'))
      .map(function (button) { return button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'); })
      .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
  }
  function applyMarket(section, marketId, persist) {
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    if (available.indexOf(marketId)< 0) marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-localized-link]')).forEach(function (link) {
      var query = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-query') || '';
      var reference = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-reference') || '';
      link.setAttribute('href', buildUrl(query, reference, marketId));
    });
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select && available.indexOf(marketId) >= 0) select.value = marketId;
    var selectedText = marketId;
    if (select && select.selectedIndex >= 0) selectedText = select.options[select.selectedIndex].text;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
