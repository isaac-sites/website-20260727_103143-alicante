## Introduction

The Motril sighting of 17 November 1979 was not filed by the Spanish Air Force as an isolated [Granada]({{ 'granadas-ufo-history-beyond-the-motril/' | relative_url }}) incident. It was grouped with separate reports from Valencia on 11 November and Madrid on 28 November under a single case reference covering three dates and three locations. That administrative link is the strongest basis for describing a wider November sighting cluster, but it does not prove that the same object or cause was responsible each time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/download/SpanishUFOFiles/1979-11-11-17-28_avistamiento_en_valencia-motril-madrid-ii.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Granada_3f7db9_november_1979_cluste_ccecb1-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for 1979 Cluster" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

What connects the cases is a short time window, military involvement and repeated difficulty matching unusual lights or radar indications to an identifiable aircraft. What separates them is equally important. Valencia began with a commercial airliner and an emergency diversion; Motril centred on a fighter pilot’s triangular formation of lights and a peculiar radio transmission; Madrid produced public reports, intermittent radar returns and an F-4 interception without a visual confirmation. The cluster therefore matters less as evidence of one travelling craft than as a revealing series of different air-defence anomalies recorded during the same month.

## The linked reports and their chronology

The official file is numbered for the three dates—11, 17 and 28 November 1979—and names Valencia, Motril and Madrid together. The surviving documentation is partly retrospective: some material was recovered in the 1990s when the Air Force sought information that remained in unit archives after UFO records had supposedly been centralised. One memorandum openly noted that journalists had already publicised military incidents, encouraging accusations that the Air Force was withholding information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/download/SpanishUFOFiles/1979-11-11-17-28_avistamiento_en_valencia-motril-madrid-ii.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive</span></span></span>

**11 November: Valencia and Manises.** A TAE passenger aircraft travelling from Mallorca towards the Canary Islands reported red lights apparently approaching or accompanying it over the western Mediterranean. The crew diverted to Manises Airport near Valencia as a precaution, after which a Mirage F1 was sent from Los Llanos Air Base to investigate. Contemporary reporting shows that the incident quickly became a national story and prompted an official aviation inquiry.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://elpais.com/diario/1979/11/14/espana/311382029_850215.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: elpais.com">[El País]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">elpais.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">* Ufología</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">El PaísEncuesta oficial sobre el aterrizaje forzoso de Manises &#124; España &#124; EL PAÍSNovember 14, 1979 — 14 nov 1979 - 00:00CET (Central Euro...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 14, 1979</span></span></span>

The Valencia episode supplied the dramatic opening to the sequence: a civilian aircraft carrying 109 people changed course, ground controllers were involved and a military interceptor was launched. Later accounts merged several reported lights, radar indications and electronic warnings into a single pursuit narrative. Critical researchers have argued instead that the episode may combine unrelated observations, including distant refinery flames, bright astronomical objects, atmospheric distortion and ambiguous electronic indications. That explanation remains disputed, but it weakens the assumption that every reported feature came from one extraordinary airborne object.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://expmanises.blogspot.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: expmanises.blogspot.com">[blogspot.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">expmanises.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Expediente Manises</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Estudio realizado partiendo de las condiciones atmosféricas presentes durante el plan de vuelo JK-297 del Supercaravelle de la Compañía A...</span></span></span>

**17 November: Motril and the Granada coast.** Six days later, another military interception was associated with the area around Motril and the Alboran Sea. According to the account reproduced from the official file, a fighter pilot saw three intense lights of similar colour and brightness arranged in an apparent isosceles triangle. The pilot flew towards the centre of the formation for roughly ten minutes but reported no apparent reduction in distance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.granadahoy.com/vivir/ovni-visito-motril-1979-tres_0_2002289169.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: granadahoy.com">[Granada Hoy]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">granadahoy.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ovni visito motril 1979 tres 0 2002289169</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ovni visito motril 1979 tres 0 2002289169</span></span></span>

During the return flight, the pilot reported hearing children laughing and repeating a brief greeting over a military ultra-high-frequency channel. The transmission lasted about 30 seconds and apparently remained audible while the pilot checked the radio configuration. The aircraft subsequently returned to Los Llanos and landed normally. The concise official account does not support later embellishments involving a long conversation, damage to the fighter or physical effects on the pilot.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.espaciomisterio.com/ovnis-y-vida-extraterrestre/expediente-ovni-desconcerto-ejercito-aire_59400" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: espaciomisterio.com">[Espacio Misterio]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">espaciomisterio.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Espacio Misterio El expediente ovni que desconcertó al Ejército del Aire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Espacio Misterio El expediente ovni que desconcertó al Ejército del Aire</span></span></span>

**27–28 November: Madrid.** Late on 27 November and into the early hours of the 28th, intermittent lights were widely reported over Madrid. Contemporary newspapers described crowds gathering in several parts of the city while radio stations broadcast updates. One report estimated that about 400 people assembled near the university district, illustrating how quickly a sky observation could become a collective media event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://elpais.com/diario/1979/11/28/madrid/312639862_850215.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: elpais.com">[El País]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">elpais.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on elpais.com.</span></span></span>

The military record says that Pegaso, the air-defence control centre, was informed at 02:30 Zulu time that two unidentified objects were above Madrid. An alert F-4 Phantom was scrambled from Torrejón at 03:30 and climbed to 35,000 feet. The crew saw no object, but reported several fleeting radar contacts and two occasions when its warning equipment indicated illumination or tracking by pulsed radar.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/download/SpanishUFOFiles/1979-11-11-17-28_avistamiento_en_valencia-motril-madrid-ii.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive</span></span></span>

The radar returns were unstable rather than continuous. A single echo disappeared on the next sweep and later appeared as three elongated, diffuse returns separated by roughly one to one-and-a-half nautical miles. The fighter repeatedly crossed the area indicated near Barajas without obtaining visual confirmation. The crew report also stated that radio communications were not disrupted and visibility was very good.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/download/SpanishUFOFiles/1979-11-11-17-28_avistamiento_en_valencia-motril-madrid-ii.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive</span></span></span>

## What the cases shared and where they differed

The three reports are sometimes presented as stages in one nationwide “flap”, meaning a concentrated period of unusual sightings. The chronology supports the limited claim that Spanish aviation and defence personnel dealt with several puzzling incidents in less than three weeks. It does not establish a single physical phenomenon moving from Valencia to Granada and then Madrid.

Several features made the grouping understandable:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Each report involved lights or sensor indications that could not be identified immediately.
* All three drew in military command, controllers, interceptors or Air Force records.
* Clear or favourable visibility was noted in the Motril and Madrid documentation, reducing—but not eliminating—the range of simple weather explanations.
* Each case contained a mismatch between what one source reported and what another could confirm: visual lights without secure range at Motril, complex mixed testimony at Valencia, and radar indications without a visual object over Madrid.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/download/SpanishUFOFiles/1979-11-11-17-28_avistamiento_en_valencia-motril-madrid-ii.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[archive.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive</span></span></span>

</div>

The differences are more substantial than the similarities. Valencia began with a civilian crew’s perceived collision risk. Motril was primarily a visual military observation followed by unexplained radio audio. Madrid combined a mass public sighting with intermittent military radar returns, yet the interceptor crew saw nothing. The reported shapes also differed: red or brilliant lights around Valencia, a three-light triangle near Motril, and intermittent luminous points over Madrid. These variations argue against treating the file’s administrative grouping as proof of a common craft.

The role of radar also needs careful qualification. Radar can provide stronger evidence than unaided observation when it supplies a stable track, range, altitude and movement confirmed across systems. The Madrid contacts did not meet that standard. They were fleeting, diffuse and absent on successive sweeps. The F-4’s warning indications showed that some radio-frequency energy may have been detected, but they did not identify its source or demonstrate that it came from the same thing producing the radar echoes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/download/SpanishUFOFiles/1979-11-11-17-28_avistamiento_en_valencia-motril-madrid-ii.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive</span></span></span>

Motril presents the reverse problem. The pilot’s report is detailed and came from an official mission, but the three lights apparently remained at an unchanging angular distance. Without reliable range information, that behaviour can be produced by very distant lights, astronomical objects or optical effects as well as by an object deliberately maintaining separation. The file reportedly considered atmospheric [refraction]({{ 'refraction/' | relative_url }}), while stopping short of a conclusive identification.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.espaciomisterio.com/ovnis-y-vida-extraterrestre/expediente-ovni-desconcerto-ejercito-aire_59400" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: espaciomisterio.com">[Espacio Misterio]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">espaciomisterio.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Espacio Misterio El expediente ovni que desconcertó al Ejército del Aire</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Espacio Misterio El expediente ovni que desconcertó al Ejército del Aire</span></span></span>

The children’s voices are the cluster’s most unusual feature, but also one of its least independently testable. No publicly available recording has emerged, and the surviving account does not identify the transmitter. Accidental cross-channel reception, deliberate interference, equipment effects or an unauthorised transmission are all less dramatic possibilities. None is proven, but the absence of an audio recording or supporting communications log means the episode cannot carry the evidential weight sometimes placed upon it.

## Did investigators find one explanation?

There is no persuasive evidence that investigators established one solution for all three dates. The official grouping reflects record management and comparative interest, not a formal conclusion that Valencia, Motril and Madrid involved the same cause. Indeed, the cases contain different combinations of witnesses, equipment and observable effects.

## Why the cluster matters to Granada’s UFO history

The November sequence gives Motril a wider national setting without making Granada merely an appendage to the famous Manises case. It shows that the Granada coast entered Air Force records during a period when controllers and fighter units were already alert to unusual reports. That context may have increased the likelihood of a military response, careful reporting and later inclusion in a combined file.

It also illustrates a recurring problem in historical UFO research: official documentation can confirm that an incident was taken seriously without confirming the extraordinary interpretation attached to it. The file establishes dates, military involvement, reported observations and operational responses. It does not establish a solid triangular craft near Motril, a shared object travelling across Spain or an extraterrestrial source.

Later disclosure strengthened the historical reality of the cluster because it demonstrated that the three reports genuinely entered Spanish military channels. At the same time, it weakened some popular retellings by exposing the fragmented nature of the evidence. The Madrid fighter never saw an object; its radar contacts were intermittent. Motril produced no known photograph, recording or measured distance. Valencia may involve several ordinary stimuli combined into one memorable narrative.

For Granada, the cluster is therefore important not because it proves a nationwide incursion, but because it places the Motril encounter inside one of Spain’s best-documented months of UFO reporting. It remains a notable military aviation case, yet the wider chronology cautions against linking separate incidents simply because they occurred close together. The most balanced conclusion is that November 1979 produced a real cluster of reports and responses, but not clear evidence of a single cause.
