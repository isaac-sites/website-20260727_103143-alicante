## Introduction

The *Cadarso* encounter is one of the most intriguing stories attached to [Melilla]({{ 'how-strong-is-melillas-ufo-evidence/' | relative_url }})’s UFO history, but also one of the least verifiable. The claim is that, during a Spanish Navy patrol between Melilla and Ceuta in 1983, the ship’s radar detected a surface target approaching on a collision course. At the calculated moment of impact, the echo supposedly disappeared, although nothing struck the vessel and no object was seen.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Melilla_c01d8f_cadarso_radar_claim_66602b-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Cadarso Radar" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

That outline is dramatic, yet almost every detail needed to assess it is missing. No precise date, position, radar plot, bridge log, weather report or named crew testimony has been made publicly accessible. The story therefore cannot presently be treated as a documented naval encounter. It is better classified as naval folklore built around a real warship, a plausible patrol route and an unexplained radar anecdote whose original evidential trail remains unknown.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://revistavocesdelmisterio.wordpress.com/2021/06/16/ovnis-en-la-historia-de-melilla/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: revistavocesdelmisterio.wordpress.com">[La esencia misma del Misterio]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">revistavocesdelmisterio.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wordpress.com.</span></span></span>

## The collision-course radar story

The fullest readily accessible version occupies only a few sentences in a later compilation of Melilla sightings. It says that the Spanish Navy patrol vessel *Cadarso* was operating between Melilla and Ceuta on an unspecified day in 1983 when its radar acquired a surface echo on a collision course. The echo allegedly vanished when the ship and the supposed target should have met. The account adds that the second-in-command was on watch and that personnel on the bridge were frightened.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://revistavocesdelmisterio.wordpress.com/2021/06/16/ovnis-en-la-historia-de-melilla/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: revistavocesdelmisterio.wordpress.com">[La esencia misma del Misterio]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">revistavocesdelmisterio.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wordpress.com.</span></span></span>

The vessel itself was certainly real and in active Spanish service during 1983. *Cadarso*, hull number P-03, was a Lazaga-class patrol ship commissioned in July 1976 and withdrawn from Spanish service in 1993. Published naval histories describe it as a fast vessel of roughly 58 metres, with a maximum speed of about 28 knots and a complement of approximately 39. Its known electronics reportedly included a surface-search or fire-control radar and a separate Raytheon navigation radar, making radar tracking during a patrol entirely credible in principle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://foro.todoavante.es/viewtopic.php?f=81&amp;t=13310" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: foro.todoavante.es">[foro.todoavante.es]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">foro.todoavante.es</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Historia Naval de España y Países de habla española</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ver Tema - Patrullero Cadarso-P03...</span></span></span>

Naval-history material records exercises in northern Spanish waters in 1982 and its use as a test platform for the Meroka close-in weapon system in 1984. ## Why the story sounds stronger than the evidence

Radar cases often seem more persuasive than ordinary sightings because an instrument appears to have detected something objective. In practice, a radar echo is not automatically a solid object. It is a return signal that still has to be interpreted using its strength, shape, persistence, movement and relationship to other information.

The phrase “collision course” is particularly important. To establish one properly, the bridge team would normally need repeated measurements showing that the target’s bearing remained steady while its range closed. A single unexplained mark, or an intermittently plotted return, would not be enough. The published story does not state how long the echo was followed, how many radar sweeps showed it, whether its range and bearing were recorded, or whether the supposed collision time was calculated manually or by an automated plotting aid.

Nor does the narrative explain what bridge personnel did in response. A genuine collision threat would normally prompt attempts to identify the contact visually, sound warnings, alter course or speed, check navigation lights, compare other sensors and record the incident. None of these actions appears in the accessible retelling. The absence of such information does not prove that nothing happened; it means that the published account is too compressed to distinguish a serious navigational event from a brief anomalous display.

The reference to fear among the bridge crew gives the story human force, but it is also a common feature of oral maritime narratives. Emotional reactions can survive repeated retelling even after technical details have disappeared. Over time, a confusing radar return can become “an object”, a projected closest approach can become “the moment of collision”, and the loss of the echo can become an apparently impossible disappearance.

## What records would be needed to verify it

A credible investigation would begin with records produced close to the event, rather than with later summaries. The most valuable item would be the ship’s navigation or bridge log for the relevant watch. That could establish the exact date, time, course, speed, position, sea conditions, visibility and any manoeuvres ordered by the officer of the watch.

Other useful material would include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the radar operator’s plot or written notes, showing successive bearings and ranges;
* equipment fault and maintenance records;
* a report to the ship’s commanding officer or naval authorities;
* communications with coastal stations or other vessels;
* meteorological and sea-state information;
* names and separate statements from the personnel present;
* confirmation that both available radars showed the same contact;
* evidence that observers searched visually and found no vessel, buoy, land feature or weather phenomenon.

Agreement between independent systems would be especially important. A target shown on two differently configured radars, tracked continuously and supported by visual or radio observations would be much harder to dismiss than an echo seen on one display. Conversely, a contact that changed or vanished when gain, range scale or sea-clutter controls were adjusted would point towards a radar artefact.

No such package has surfaced in accessible reporting. The commonly cited account supplies neither a document reference nor a traceable witness interview. A naval-history chronology for the ship lists several deployments and notable activities but does not mention a 1983 collision-course anomaly. That silence is not conclusive, because informal incidents may never enter published ship histories, but it provides no independent support for the UFO version.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://revistavocesdelmisterio.wordpress.com/2021/06/16/ovnis-en-la-historia-de-melilla/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: revistavocesdelmisterio.wordpress.com">[La esencia misma del Misterio]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">revistavocesdelmisterio.wordpress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wordpress.com.</span></span></span>

Spain’s well-known declassified UFO collection does not resolve the question. The Ministry of Defence’s online archive was created from files assembled by the Spanish Air Force, with a physical copy deposited in the Air Force’s central library in 1992 before later digitisation. Its existence is evidence of an official aviation-focused investigation and declassification programme, not a complete index of every unusual event experienced by every branch of the armed forces. The absence of an obvious *Cadarso* file there therefore cannot establish that the Navy held no record; it only means that the familiar public Air Force collection does not presently authenticate the story.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es/BVMDefensa/exp_ovni/es/micrositios/inicio.do" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es">[Biblioteca Virtual Defensa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gob.es.</span></span></span>

## Radar effects and ordinary alternatives

Several recognised radar effects could produce a contact that looked solid for a time and then disappeared. Modern equipment is better at filtering unwanted returns, but the underlying problems are longstanding.

**Sea clutter** is caused by radar energy reflecting from wave crests and disturbed water. It is strongest close to a ship and varies with sea state, wind, antenna height and radar settings. Performance standards for marine radar explicitly recognise that waves, rain and other clutter reduce detection reliability and require adjustable anti-clutter controls. A patch of sea clutter can strengthen, move irregularly or disappear as the vessel changes heading or as the operator adjusts gain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.imorules.com/GUID-2141E6DF-030D-414F-B752-6466EA3A3B9C.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: imorules.com">[ImoRules]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">imorules.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on imorules.com.</span></span></span>

**Multiple and ghost echoes** arise when radar energy follows an indirect path, perhaps reflecting between ships or from structures on the vessel. Manufacturer guidance notes that these false targets can appear at misleading positions and may behave enough like genuine contacts to confuse an operator. Some disappear when the geometry changes or when gain and sea-clutter settings are reduced.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://docs.raymarine.com/87402/en-US/latest/FalseEchoes-4A646026.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: docs.raymarine.com">[docs.raymarine.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">docs.raymarine.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">False echoes</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">False echoes</span></span></span>

**Side-lobe returns** are produced by weaker radiation outside the radar antenna’s main beam. Large or nearby targets can consequently create arcs or duplicate marks around their true position. These echoes are most noticeable at short range—the very stage at which the *Cadarso* story says the target vanished.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://docs.raymarine.com/87402/en-US/latest/FalseEchoes-4A646026.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: docs.raymarine.com">[docs.raymarine.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">docs.raymarine.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">False echoes</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">False echoes</span></span></span>

**Atmospheric propagation** can also bend radar energy in unusual ways. Temperature and humidity layers above the sea may extend or distort normal radar coverage, allowing distant ships or land features to appear where they are not expected. The waters around Melilla lie beside a dry continental coast and beneath an atmosphere frequently shaped by strong temperature contrasts, making propagation effects a relevant possibility, although no specific weather data survive in the published account.

A real vessel also remains possible. A small boat viewed bow-on may present a weak radar return, particularly in rough water. It might turn away, become masked by sea clutter, pass through a radar blind sector or simply fall below the detection threshold at close range. Without the ranges, bearings and display settings, “vanished” could mean anything from an abrupt electronic loss to an operator no longer being able to separate a weak echo from surrounding clutter.

Equipment error cannot be excluded either. A temporary fault, poor tuning, interference from another radar or incorrect interpretation of the range scale could create a threatening-looking track. None of these explanations can be selected confidently because the case lacks the technical information needed to test them.

## Why it became naval folklore

The story has several qualities that help maritime legends endure. It involves a named military vessel, an operationally sensitive stretch of sea and a threat detected by technology rather than by an anonymous observer. It also ends at the most dramatic possible moment: the target disappears exactly when collision appears unavoidable.

Its setting adds credibility without providing evidence. The route between Ceuta and Melilla crosses the Alboran Sea, a strategically important and heavily travelled region connecting the western Mediterranean with the Strait of Gibraltar. Patrol vessels, merchant traffic, fishing boats, coastal radar, military activity and variable sea conditions make it a place where unusual contacts are plausible—but also where ordinary radar confusion is unsurprising.

Later writers have linked the episode to a supposed “Alboran triangle” or a broader sequence of maritime mysteries. That framing weakens rather than strengthens the case. Grouping unrelated aircraft losses, disappearances and radar anecdotes beneath a geographical mystery label can make coincidence look like a coherent pattern. The *Cadarso* report needs to stand or fall on its own records, not on the atmosphere created by other stories.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.andaluciainformacion.es/opinion/author/hay-un-triangulo-de-las-bermudas-de-andalucia/20230220114059925699.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: andaluciainformacion.es">[Andalucía Información]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">andaluciainformacion.es</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Andalucía Información¿Hay un &#x27;Triángulo de las Bermudas&#x27; de Andalucía?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">March 4, 2023 — Lo mismo sucedió al patrullero &quot;Cadarso&quot;, en 1983, cuando detectó un eco en rumbo de colisión en la travesía Ceuta-Melil...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 4, 2023</span></span></span>

The story has also been repeated with little visible development. Later versions largely preserve the same collision-course formula rather than adding documents, named witnesses or technical reconstruction. Repetition shows that the anecdote has circulated; it does not provide independent corroboration when the versions may derive from the same unidentified source.

## The fairest assessment

The *Cadarso* episode should not be described as a confirmed unidentified craft, an underwater object or evidence of an extraordinary presence near Melilla. The published material establishes only that a story exists about a radar echo aboard a real Spanish patrol vessel in 1983.

The claim is not inherently impossible. Marine radars do acquire unexplained contacts, and bridge teams sometimes encounter returns that cannot be identified in the moment. A contemporary log and consistent testimony could turn the anecdote into a worthwhile historical radar case. At present, however, there is no accessible evidence that allows readers to reconstruct the track, evaluate the equipment or establish that the story came directly from personnel aboard the ship.

Within Melilla’s UFO history, its value is therefore cautionary. It shows how a short military anecdote can acquire authority through the prestige of a naval setting while remaining less securely documented than many civilian sightings. The most responsible classification is **unverified naval folklore with plausible conventional explanations**, not a solved incident but not a robust unresolved case either. Until operational records or identifiable first-hand testimony emerge, the disappearing target remains a story about a radar screen rather than evidence of an extraordinary object in the Alboran Sea.
