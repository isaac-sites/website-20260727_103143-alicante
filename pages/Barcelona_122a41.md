## Introduction

That distinction matters. Barcelona has a rich UFO culture, but the evidence varies sharply from case to case. Some reports remain unidentified because too little information survives. Others have plausible explanations involving balloons, meteors, astronomical objects, aircraft or scientific instruments. “Unidentified” in this history usually means that a report was not conclusively resolved from the available material, not that an alien craft was established.

## The 1978 Barcelona file

The clearest official Barcelona case began with a letter rather than an emergency military alert. According to Air Force file 780704, the secretary of the Barcelona Parapsychology and Ufology Group wrote to the authorities after hearing that an unidentified object had crossed Barcelona, L’Hospitalet and Sabadell on 4 July 1978. The group also reported that the object had supposedly been detected by radar at Barcelona’s El Prat airport and asked the Air Force for further details.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/download/SpanishUFOFiles/1978-07-04_avistamiento_en_barcelona-hospitalet-sabadell.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet ArchiveAVISTAMIENTO DE FENOMENOS EXTRAÑOS (EXPEDIENTES OVNI)AVISTAMIENTO DE FENOMENOS EXTRAÑOS (EXPEDIENTES OVNI) AVISTAMIENTO D...</span></span></span>

The claim then moved through several layers of military administration. Air Force offices requested information from the relevant regional command and from the Barcelona airport control centre. On 1 September 1978, the head of the Catalonia air sector reported that his office had received no information indicating that an unidentified object had flown over the three cities. The airport control centre likewise had no record of an unidentified radar return on the date concerned.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/download/SpanishUFOFiles/1978-07-04_avistamiento_en_barcelona-hospitalet-sabadell.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet ArchiveAVISTAMIENTO DE FENOMENOS EXTRAÑOS (EXPEDIENTES OVNI)AVISTAMIENTO DE FENOMENOS EXTRAÑOS (EXPEDIENTES OVNI) AVISTAMIENTO D...</span></span></span>

## Montserrat became the province’s UFO landmark

Barcelona’s best-known UFO location is not the city or its airport but Montserrat, the mountain massif and monastery area north-west of the capital. From 1977, Luis José Grifol organised public observation gatherings there on the eleventh night of each month. The meetings continued for decades and attracted committed believers, curious visitors, journalists and people who simply wanted to watch the night sky from a dark elevated location.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.barcelonaesmoltmes.cat/en/-/montserrat-a-magical-mountain" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: barcelonaesmoltmes.cat">[barcelonaesmoltmes.cat]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">barcelonaesmoltmes.cat</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Parc Natural de Montserrat</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Barcelona és molt mésMontserrat, a magical mountainSince 1977, Montserrat has also been a “UFO sanctuary” thanks to Luis José Grifol, who...</span></span></span>

Grifol said his interest began after seeing a fluorescent fireball-like object near Montgat in 1977. At Montserrat he developed a more spiritual interpretation, presenting some lights as possible responses from non-human intelligences rather than merely distant objects awaiting identification. This moved the gatherings beyond conventional witness investigation and towards organised contact belief.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rtve.es/play/videos/tenemos-que-hablar/tenemos-hablar-luis-jose-avisto-primer-ovni-montgat-barcelona/1845034/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rtve.es">[RTVE]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rtve.es</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Tenemos que hablar</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Tenemos que hablar</span></span></span>

Montserrat matters because it became a durable public institution within Spanish UFO culture. Its regular timetable created repeated opportunities for observation, storytelling and media coverage. Reports from the gatherings often concerned distant points of light, apparent changes in direction or objects that seemed to brighten after observers called attention to them. Such experiences can feel striking in person, especially when shared by a group, but most lack the positional data, calibrated imagery and independent tracking required for reliable identification.

The setting also increases the number of ordinary objects likely to be noticed. Montserrat provides broad horizons and darker skies than central Barcelona, while remaining beneath or near busy regional airspace. Aircraft seen head-on can appear stationary before suddenly moving sideways; satellites may brighten and fade as their angle to the Sun changes; meteors can flare abruptly; and atmospheric distortion can make bright stars shimmer or change colour. Modern satellite groups add another recognisable source of unusual light formations. Even accounts sympathetic to the Montserrat tradition acknowledge aircraft and satellites as explanations for at least some recent observations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://canrobira.com/es/misterios-monasterio/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canrobira.com">[Masia Can Robira]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canrobira.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Masia Can Robira Misterios del monasterio de Montserrat</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Masia Can Robira Misterios del monasterio de Montserrat</span></span></span>

No single evidential standard applies to every Montserrat report, but the broad pattern is clear: the gatherings generated abundant testimony and cultural influence without producing a publicly verified case supported by multiple calibrated instruments. Montserrat is therefore a landmark in Barcelona’s UFO history primarily because people repeatedly went there expecting to see something, not because one decisive incident settled what the lights were.

## Barcelona helped build Spanish UFO research

The province’s importance extends beyond sightings. Barcelona became an early centre of organised Spanish UFO study through the Centre for Interplanetary Studies, founded in 1958. The organisation traces its origins to the surge of public interest created by the 1957 International Astronautical Congress in Barcelona and the launch of Sputnik, the first artificial satellite. Those events made spaceflight newly tangible and helped create an audience for reports of unexplained objects in the sky.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.el-cei.org/2024/12/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: el-cei.org">[el-cei.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">el-cei.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on el-cei.org.</span></span></span>

Writer and translator Antonio Ribera was one of the centre’s founders and became a leading populariser of the subject in Spain. He published extensively on UFOs, participated in international conferences and helped connect Spanish cases with wider European and American UFO literature. Barcelona also hosted Spain’s first national ufology congress in 1977, reflecting the city’s role as an organisational and publishing hub rather than merely a location where sightings occurred.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonio_Ribera" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Antonio Ribera</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Antonio Ribera</span></span></span>

This local network had both strengths and weaknesses. [Researchers]({{ 'researchers/' | relative_url }}) preserved press cuttings, correspondence, witness reports and case discussions that might otherwise have disappeared. The continuing work of the Barcelona-based centre offers valuable evidence about how reports circulated and how interpretations changed. Its more recent publications have revisited older Catalan cases, compared original letters with later retellings and recovered material from private archives.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.el-cei.org/2024/12/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: el-cei.org">[el-cei.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">el-cei.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on el-cei.org.</span></span></span>

At the same time, early UFO organisations generally worked without the tools now considered essential for investigating aerial events. Precise times were often missing, witness interviews were not always recorded verbatim, and investigators rarely had immediate access to flight paths, satellite predictions, meteor networks or complete meteorological data. Some researchers also began from the assumption that unusual reports might represent visiting craft, which could influence the questions asked and the alternatives explored.

Barcelona’s archival legacy is consequently valuable but uneven. It documents what people reported, believed and investigated; it does not automatically validate every conclusion reached by those investigators.

## What Spain’s declassified files establish

Spain began reviewing classified records of “strange phenomena sightings” in 1991. The Ministry of Defence’s digital collection now contains 80 files comprising about 1,900 pages and covering incidents from 1962 to 1995 in which Air Force personnel or equipment were involved in some way. A physical copy was deposited with the Air Force’s central library before the material was digitised for public consultation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es/BVMDefensa/exp_ovni/es/micrositios/inicio.do" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es">[Biblioteca Virtual Defensa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Se trata de un total de 80 expedientes, 1.900 páginas de avistamientos de fenómenos extraños dentro del espacio aéreo español, en los que...</span></span></span>

These records are important because they let readers separate an official investigation from an official endorsement. A file may exist because a civilian group wrote to the military, because a pilot reported a light, or because radar personnel were asked to check their records. Classification could reflect military communications, procedures or operational information rather than the extraordinary nature of the object.

The Barcelona file illustrates this perfectly. Its presence in the archive confirms that the Air Force processed an enquiry about a reported sighting. It does not confirm that military observers saw the object, that radar tracked it or that investigators concluded it displayed exceptional performance. Indeed, the response preserved in the file contradicts its most evidentially important claim: El Prat’s control centre found no record of an unidentified detection on the stated date.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/download/SpanishUFOFiles/1978-07-04_avistamiento_en_barcelona-hospitalet-sabadell.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet ArchiveAVISTAMIENTO DE FENOMENOS EXTRAÑOS (EXPEDIENTES OVNI)AVISTAMIENTO DE FENOMENOS EXTRAÑOS (EXPEDIENTES OVNI) AVISTAMIENTO D...</span></span></span>

Declassification nevertheless improved the history. Before the papers became readily accessible, later writers could repeat compressed versions of a case without showing how each claim entered the story. The documents reveal the chain of information: an association reported what it had heard, administrators requested checks, and the relevant air authorities replied that they could not confirm it. That chain is less dramatic than a secret radar encounter, but far more useful for judging the evidence.

## Aviation adds credibility and confusion

Barcelona’s airport and air-traffic infrastructure inevitably recur in regional UFO stories. Reports involving pilots, controllers or radar operators deserve attention because these witnesses understand aircraft and may have access to information unavailable to an ordinary observer. Yet professional status does not make every distant light identifiable, nor does a reference to radar mean that a stable unexplained track was recorded.

Radar claims require specific supporting details: which sensor detected the target, whether the return persisted, whether another radar confirmed it, its measured altitude and speed, and whether weather, equipment effects or known traffic were excluded. None of that supporting material appears in the surviving 1978 Barcelona dossier. The airport’s response was instead that no unidentified detection was recorded.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/download/SpanishUFOFiles/1978-07-04_avistamiento_en_barcelona-hospitalet-sabadell.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet ArchiveAVISTAMIENTO DE FENOMENOS EXTRAÑOS (EXPEDIENTES OVNI)AVISTAMIENTO DE FENOMENOS EXTRAÑOS (EXPEDIENTES OVNI) AVISTAMIENTO D...</span></span></span>

Barcelona air-traffic control has also appeared in accounts of incidents occurring well outside the province, most famously the 1979 Manises case near Valencia. Because Barcelona controllers communicated with the aircraft, the case is sometimes drawn into Barcelona UFO histories. Its central events, however, belonged to the air route, Manises Airport and the subsequent military interception over eastern Spain, not to a sighting over Barcelona province. It is best treated as a related national aviation case rather than a Barcelona incident.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manises_UFO_Incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Manises UFO incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Manises UFO incident</span></span></span>

This boundary is important. Associating every aircraft that contacted Barcelona control with the province can create a misleading impression of a large local cluster. Barcelona’s genuine contribution lies more in its control infrastructure, investigative organisations and archives than in a concentration of well-supported airborne encounters over the province itself.

## Why ordinary objects produce strong reports

The Barcelona record repeatedly demonstrates how an observation can be sincere yet misidentified. Witness credibility and object identification are separate questions. A person may accurately report seeing an unusual light while being unable to judge its distance, size or speed.

Several explanations deserve priority when assessing local reports:

* **Balloons and scientific probes:** High-altitude balloons can drift slowly, reflect sunlight and appear almost motionless. The 1978 Air Force correspondence specifically mentioned balloon launches from Sicily. In May 2014, another object noticed over L’Hospitalet was later identified as an atmospheric research probe associated with Spain’s National Research Council.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/download/SpanishUFOFiles/1978-07-04_avistamiento_en_barcelona-hospitalet-sabadell.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet ArchiveAVISTAMIENTO DE FENOMENOS EXTRAÑOS (EXPEDIENTES OVNI)AVISTAMIENTO DE FENOMENOS EXTRAÑOS (EXPEDIENTES OVNI) AVISTAMIENTO D...</span></span></span>
* **Meteors and fireballs:** Bright meteors may change brightness, fragment or cross a large part of the sky in seconds. Spanish research networks reconstruct such events using observations from multiple stations, showing why isolated witnesses can mistake their trajectory, distance or apparent destination.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/1604.08380" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on arxiv.org.</span></span></span>
* **Aircraft:** Landing lights can make an approaching aeroplane resemble a stationary orb. When it turns, the light may appear to accelerate or vanish suddenly.
* **Satellites:** Individual satellites can flare, while groups of recently launched satellites may form striking lines or clusters. These explanations have become increasingly relevant to reports from Montserrat and other dark-sky observation sites.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://canrobira.com/es/misterios-monasterio/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: canrobira.com">[Masia Can Robira]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">canrobira.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Masia Can Robira Misterios del monasterio de Montserrat</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Masia Can Robira Misterios del monasterio de Montserrat</span></span></span>
* **Stars and planets:** Atmospheric turbulence near the horizon can make a bright astronomical object pulse, change colour or appear to move. Without a fixed reference point, small movements of the observer’s eyes can make a stationary light seem mobile.

An explanation should not be forced merely because it is familiar. Matching a report properly requires time, direction, duration, weather, angular movement and independent records. But when those details are absent, the correct result is usually “insufficient information”, not “extraordinary object”.

## How strong are Barcelona’s principal claims?

Barcelona’s UFO history can be divided into three evidence levels.

**Historically important but evidentially weak:** The 4 July 1978 Barcelona–L’Hospitalet–Sabadell report is preserved in an official archive and is therefore significant. Its central radar claim was not confirmed, however, and the available file lacks the witness material needed for a fresh identification.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/download/SpanishUFOFiles/1978-07-04_avistamiento_en_barcelona-hospitalet-sabadell.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet ArchiveAVISTAMIENTO DE FENOMENOS EXTRAÑOS (EXPEDIENTES OVNI)AVISTAMIENTO DE FENOMENOS EXTRAÑOS (EXPEDIENTES OVNI) AVISTAMIENTO D...</span></span></span>

## The province’s real place in UFO history

Barcelona occupies an unusual position in Spanish UFO history. Its most important legacy is not one spectacular encounter but an ecosystem: investigators such as Antonio Ribera, organisations such as the Centre for Interplanetary Studies, local publications and archives, the airport’s role in checking aviation claims, and the long-running Montserrat gatherings. Together, they helped determine how UFO stories were collected, circulated and remembered across Spain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonio_Ribera" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Antonio Ribera</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Antonio Ribera</span></span></span>

The strongest lesson from that history is methodological. Official paperwork must be read closely, because a documented allegation can later be mistaken for a documented detection. Repeated testimony can establish that people saw lights, but not necessarily what those lights were. A case may remain unresolved because crucial information was never collected, while another may become less mysterious when radar records, balloon launches or scientific instruments are checked.

Barcelona therefore offers a compelling UFO history without requiring an extraordinary conclusion. It shows how unexplained observations arise where busy airspace, astronomy, public fascination and organised investigation meet — and how later archival work can make a famous story clearer even when it cannot identify the original light.
