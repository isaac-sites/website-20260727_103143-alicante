## Introduction

Montserrat became [Barcelona]({{ 'how-strong-is-barcelonas-ufo-evidence/' | relative_url }}) province’s best-known UFO landmark because people repeatedly went there expecting to see something. From the late 1970s onwards, Luis José Grifol turned night-time observation into a monthly public ritual, usually associated with the eleventh day of each month. Believers, sceptics, journalists and curious visitors gathered near El Bruc, ate together, drove up the mountain and watched points of light cross a dark, dramatic horizon. At its height, the gathering could attract hundreds of people.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rtve.es/play/noticias/20111026/cronicas-expediente-ovni/471225.shtml" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rtve.es">[rtve.es]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rtve.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Expediente OVNI&quot;October 26, 2011 — El hombre que los cita, Luis José Grifol, asegura que los dias 11 tienen un significado especial...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 26, 2011</span></span></span>

The tradition is historically important, but its evidential value is limited. Numerous attendees reported lights, flashes or objects that seemed to change course, respond to questions or appear at meaningful moments. Yet the published accounts rarely preserve exact times, viewing directions, angular measurements, original photographs or independent tracking data. Most sightings therefore remain unidentified only in the narrow sense that they cannot now be reconstructed. Aircraft, satellites, meteors, bright celestial objects and atmospheric effects offer plausible explanations for much of what was described, while a small residue remains too poorly documented for a firm conclusion.

## Luis José Grifol and the monthly gatherings

Grifol said his involvement began in 1977 after he observed unusual lights from his home in Montgat, on the Barcelona coast. He interpreted their movements as responsive and later described further visual and telepathic contacts. By the early 1980s, his claims had received national press coverage, and he argued that their authenticity was supported by the large number of people who had accompanied him and seen lights at Montserrat.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://elpais.com/diario/1983/08/29/ultima/430956006_850215.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: elpais.com">[elpais.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">elpais.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Lo que avala la autenticidad de mis contactos es que conmigo han visto ovnis en Montserrat cientos de personas de toda clase y condición&quot;...</span></span></span>

The crucial change was from a personal experience to a repeatable social event. Grifol began taking friends and acquaintances to a viewing area on the Montserrat massif. Word spread, and the outings developed into regular gatherings on the eleventh night of the month. Accounts differ over the precise year in which the formal monthly schedule began: some trace the activity directly to 1977, while others place the organised eleventh-day tradition around 1979 or 1980. The discrepancy is a warning against treating later retellings as a precise contemporary record.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rtve.es/play/videos/tenemos-que-hablar/tenemos-hablar-luis-jose-avisto-primer-ovni-montgat-barcelona/1845034/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rtve.es">[rtve.es]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rtve.es</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Tenemos que hablar</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Tenemos que hablar - Programa de testimonios en RTVE PlayMay 29, 2013 — Luis José Grifol organiza excursiones a Montserrat (BCN) todo...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 29, 2013</span></span></span>

Grifol attached symbolic importance to the number 11. Later local reporting said he linked the date partly to aviation-related UFO incidents, particularly the Manises case of 11 November 1979, in which a commercial aircraft diverted to Valencia after its crew reported unexplained lights. Montserrat was presented as a kind of landmark or beacon for the intelligences he believed were behind the appearances. These were Grifol’s interpretations, not findings independently established by astronomy, aviation authorities or the Spanish military.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://veuanoia.cat/mor-luis-jose-grifol-impulsor-trobades-ovni-montserrat-dies-11/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: veuanoia.cat">[Veuanoia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">veuanoia.cat</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mor Luís José Grífol, l&#x27;impulsor de les trobades ovni de Montserrat els dies 11</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mor Luís José Grífol, l&#x27;impulsor de les trobades ovni de Montserrat els dies 11 - La Veu de l&#x27;AnoiaMor Luís José Grífol, l&#x27;impuls...</span></span></span>

The usual evening was as much a social routine as an investigation. Participants met at the Hotel Bruc below the mountain, talked over food and then travelled to the viewing area, which became popularly known as the UFO clearing. Television footage from 2011 showed that the gathering could still attract roughly 300 people on a major night, even while an important football match competed for local attention. A long-term attendee later recalled that more ordinary meetings varied between about 20 and 60 participants, ranging from committed regulars to visitors who had come for amusement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rtve.es/play/noticias/20111026/cronicas-expediente-ovni/471225.shtml" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rtve.es">[rtve.es]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rtve.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Expediente OVNI&quot;October 26, 2011 — El hombre que los cita, Luis José Grifol, asegura que los dias 11 tienen un significado especial...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 26, 2011</span></span></span>

That mixed audience helps explain the gathering’s endurance. Some visitors accepted Grifol’s contact claims; others were interested in UFO history without sharing his beliefs. Journalists came because the event offered witnesses, atmosphere and a ready-made story. Teenagers and tourists came to experience an unusual Barcelona-area tradition. By 2025, participants interviewed at the site described the UFO theme as partly an excuse for a monthly reunion, while newcomers were increasingly drawn by social media and television drama rather than by long-standing ufological commitment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cronicaglobal.cat/vida/20251026/masificacion-noches-montserrat-veteranos-venimos-jovenes-porros/1003742701072_0.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cronicaglobal.cat">[Crónica Global]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cronicaglobal.cat</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on cronicaglobal.cat.</span></span></span>

Grifol’s personal presence nevertheless gave the meetings their structure. He addressed the crowd, interpreted lights and connected observations with his wider beliefs about non-human intelligences. Reports consistently describe his ability to hold the attention of people who did not necessarily accept his conclusions. He stopped attending regularly in his later years, and the gatherings continued in a looser form without the single authoritative voice that had shaped them for decades. Grifol died on 31 December 2025, aged 81, leaving behind a cultural institution more securely documented than any particular extraordinary sighting associated with it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://veuanoia.cat/mor-luis-jose-grifol-impulsor-trobades-ovni-montserrat-dies-11/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: veuanoia.cat">[veuanoia.cat]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">veuanoia.cat</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mor Luís José Grífol, l&#x27;impulsor de les trobades ovni de Montserrat els dies 11</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mor Luís José Grífol, l&#x27;impulsor de les trobades ovni de Montserrat els dies 11 - La Veu de l&#x27;AnoiaMor Luís José Grífol, l&#x27;impuls...</span></span></span>

## What observers reported from the mountain

Most Montserrat reports concern lights rather than clearly observed structured craft. Witnesses have described luminous points crossing the sky, brightening, fading, changing colour, appearing to stop or seeming to move after Grifol addressed them. Other accounts speak of larger glowing forms or intense lights passing close to the mountain. The surviving descriptions are vivid, but they usually lack the basic observational information needed to distinguish an aircraft from an astronomical or atmospheric phenomenon.

One of the most frequently retold episodes involved the future author Javier Sierra and investigator Manuel Carballal during a visit to Grifol in July 1987. Sierra has said that, while still a teenager, he went to interview Grifol and witnessed a powerful light that profoundly affected him. Later programmes and articles present the event as a formative experience for both young observers. It is significant within Spanish UFO culture because two later media figures repeatedly testified that they found the sight difficult to explain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://omny.fm/shows/o-v-n-i/e04-montserrat" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: omny.fm">[omny.fm]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">omny.fm</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Studio E04</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Studio E04</span></span></span>

The episode is not, however, a strong scientific case simply because the witnesses remained impressed by it. Public retellings do not supply a complete observation log, simultaneous photographs from known positions, radar records or measurements of distance and speed. Even an audio recording of reactions, where mentioned, would show that the witnesses experienced surprise; it would not by itself establish what produced the light. The case therefore preserves sincere testimony and cultural influence, but not enough physical information to eliminate ordinary causes confidently.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.larazon.es/opinion/dia-que-acabo-adolescencia_2026011269643291ea66eb73532d22f7.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: larazon.es">[La Razón]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">larazon.es</span><span class="citation-popover-title">La Razón El día que se acabó mi adolescencia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">La Razón El día que se acabó mi adolescencia</span></span></span>

Montserrat’s reputation also absorbed reports that did not arise from the monthly gatherings. A notable example occurred on 29 November 1985, when observers around Montserrat and Barcelona province reported a bright point with a conspicuous trail descending rapidly. Contemporary or later summaries refer to airport personnel, police, religious figures and dozens of witnesses. A broad geographical distribution and short, fast, luminous descent are characteristics that investigators would normally compare with a bright meteor or re-entering material before considering a controlled craft. The report illustrates how a dramatic but potentially natural sky event can reinforce an existing hotspot’s legend.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://elpais.com/ccaa/2016/11/01/catalunya/1478022885_488700.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: elpais.com">[El País]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">elpais.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on elpais.com.</span></span></span>

Other reported lights are difficult even to date. Montserrat accounts have circulated through television appearances, radio discussions, books, podcasts and personal recollections, often years after the event. Details can become compressed into the recurring formula that UFOs appeared on the eleventh night. The formula obscures an essential distinction: a gathering may have produced numerous moments of uncertainty without producing numerous well-investigated anomalies.

The crowd itself could influence interpretation. When dozens or hundreds of people scan the same sky and someone calls attention to a light, the observation becomes socially guided. Participants may agree that they see the same point while differing unconsciously about its direction, distance or behaviour. A normal aircraft turn can be interpreted as a response; a satellite flare can appear at the emotional high point of a discussion; an object temporarily hidden by haze can seem to disappear instantly. None of this requires dishonesty. It reflects the difficulty of judging isolated lights at night without stable visual references.

Expectation also affects which events are remembered. A gathering lasting several hours may contain many identifiable aircraft, stars and satellites, but the one light that appears unusual becomes the story repeated afterwards. Nights on which nothing remarkable happened receive less coverage. This selection effect can create the impression of a recurring phenomenon even when the reported events differ in appearance, duration and likely cause.

## Aircraft, satellites, meteors and atmospheric effects

Montserrat is an impressive observing location, but it is not an isolated laboratory. It lies within a densely populated and heavily travelled region, with Barcelona’s metropolitan airspace to the south-east and regular aviation routes visible over Catalonia. A distant aircraft approaching almost head-on may appear motionless before turning sharply. Landing lights can dominate the view while red, green or flashing navigation lights remain invisible; when the aircraft changes angle, the bright light may fade suddenly. The result can resemble a luminous object that hovers, reacts and vanishes.

This explanation becomes especially relevant when witnesses report slow lights lasting several minutes. Modern official guidance identifies conventional aircraft as a frequent source of unidentified reports, particularly when viewed from unusual angles or in low visibility. A convincing aircraft identification would require the exact time and viewing direction to be compared with flight records. Most published Montserrat stories do not preserve that information, so aircraft remain a plausible category rather than a proven explanation for every light.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/FAQ/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on aaro.mil.</span></span></span>

Satellites provide another strong candidate. They generally move silently and steadily across the stars, sometimes fading as they enter the Earth’s shadow. Rotating spacecraft or debris can produce repeated glints, while reflective surfaces can brighten abruptly and then disappear. To an observer unfamiliar with orbital motion, a satellite may seem extraordinarily high, fast or purposeful. Modern satellite trains are particularly conspicuous, although they cannot explain reports made before such constellations existed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/FAQ/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on aaro.mil.</span></span></span>

A bright meteor fits reports of a rapid streak, flare or descending light lasting seconds. Meteors can fragment, change brightness and leave glowing trails that persist briefly after the main object has vanished. Because observers have no direct distance cue, a meteor tens of kilometres above the ground may appear to fall behind a nearby ridge or into the mountain. The apparent connection with Montserrat can therefore result from perspective rather than from an object reaching the massif.

The date of the gatherings also guaranteed occasional overlap with meteor activity. The eleventh night of any month is not astronomically privileged, but repeated all-night observations across many years make encounters with sporadic meteors inevitable. Summer gatherings could also fall close to active shower periods. A dramatic meteor seen by a primed crowd is likely to be remembered as a meaningful appearance, especially if it occurs immediately after a request for a sign.

Bright planets and stars can produce slower misunderstandings. Venus, Jupiter and Sirius are common examples in UFO investigations because atmospheric turbulence makes them flicker, change colour and appear to shift slightly. The effect is stronger when a light is low above the horizon, where its rays travel through more atmosphere. Thin cloud can make it swell, dim, split or disappear. Small involuntary eye movements can also make a lone point seem to wander when there is no nearby reference against which to judge it. AARO lists planets, stars and related perceptual effects among common causes of apparently hovering or manoeuvring objects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/FAQ/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on aaro.mil.</span></span></span>

Montserrat’s terrain can intensify these ambiguities. Jagged silhouettes provide a powerful foreground but an unreliable distance scale. Valleys and ridges hide lower parts of the sky, so ordinary objects may appear suddenly from behind rock, cloud or vegetation. Local haze over the Barcelona plain can diffuse lights from aircraft or settlements. Temperature differences and layers of moist air can alter brightness and apparent position, although “atmospheric effects” should not be used as a vague answer without weather data for the particular night.

Balloons and lantern-like objects are less predictable but also relevant. A windborne light can drift, pause relative to a ridge, brighten as it turns and then vanish when its flame fails or it enters cloud. Scientific and commercial balloons can reflect sunlight at high altitude after the ground below has become dark. Official investigators classify balloons and other airborne clutter as frequent sources of anomalous-looking reports. Again, a specific match requires wind, launch and timing data that the historical Montserrat reports seldom contain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/FAQ/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on aaro.mil.</span></span></span>

## Why the sightings were rarely resolved

The principal weakness was not that the witnesses were necessarily unreliable. It was that the gatherings were organised to watch and interpret, not to measure. Reports commonly lack several pieces of information that are indispensable for identification:

* the exact time to the nearest minute;

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* the observer’s precise position;
* the compass direction and elevation of the light;
* its duration and path against known stars;
* whether it flashed regularly or produced sound;
* original, uncropped photographs or video;
* weather, visibility and wind data;
* comparison with air traffic and satellite passes;
* observations from a second location suitable for triangulation.

</div>

Without these details, descriptions such as “very fast”, “enormous” or “close to the mountain” cannot be converted into reliable physical estimates. A light of unknown distance has no known size or speed. Apparent acceleration may result from a turn, a change in brightness or movement of the observer’s eyes rather than from rapid propulsion.

The structure of the gatherings created another problem: Grifol was both organiser and chief interpreter. When a prominent figure identifies a light for a crowd, later witness accounts are no longer fully independent. People may remember their observations through the explanation offered at the time. A strong case would separate individual statements before witnesses discuss the event and would preserve disagreements rather than smoothing them into a single narrative.

Modern scientific proposals for ground-based UAP research demonstrate how different a rigorous observation programme would look. [Researchers]({{ 'researchers/' | relative_url }}) advocate synchronised wide- and narrow-field cameras, triangulation, environmental sensors, radio receivers and acoustic instruments so that an apparent anomaly can be measured and independently corroborated. NASA likewise stresses that the shortage of high-quality observations prevents firm scientific conclusions about many reports. Montserrat’s traditional gatherings produced sustained attention, but not this kind of controlled dataset.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18566" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on arxiv.org.</span></span></span>

This is why “unresolved” must be used carefully. In many Montserrat cases, it does not mean that ordinary explanations were tested and failed. It means that the report reached the public without enough information for the tests to be performed. A light cannot be positively identified as an aircraft years later if nobody recorded its time and direction, but that evidential failure does not make an extraordinary craft more likely.

## A landmark of UFO culture, not proof of repeated visitation

Montserrat matters within Barcelona’s UFO history because it created continuity. Individual sightings elsewhere in the province appeared briefly in newspapers or official correspondence and then faded. The mountain offered a fixed place, a fixed date and a recognisable host. It allowed people to participate directly in the UFO story rather than merely reading about one. Television programmes could return repeatedly, and journalists could be confident of finding both believers and sceptics in the same setting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rtve.es/play/noticias/20111026/cronicas-expediente-ovni/471225.shtml" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rtve.es">[rtve.es]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rtve.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Expediente OVNI&quot;October 26, 2011 — El hombre que los cita, Luis José Grifol, asegura que los dias 11 tienen un significado especial...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 26, 2011</span></span></span>

The location also carried a pre-existing symbolic weight. Montserrat’s monastery, unusual geology and long tradition of religious and legendary narratives made the mountain an unusually persuasive setting for claims of contact. Its physical appearance encourages the idea of a separate, mysterious landscape, even though the viewing area remains connected to busy roads, settlements and regional air traffic. The setting strengthened the emotional force of sightings without strengthening their technical documentation.

Later media attention has sustained the tradition after the peak of Spanish UFO enthusiasm. Documentaries revisited Grifol and interviewed veteran participants; the television series *La Mesías* used Montserrat’s UFO-meeting imagery and renewed public curiosity about the Hotel Bruc and the observation site. Recent reports describe the clearing as an established visitor destination, with social gatherings continuing even among people who do not expect to see extraterrestrial craft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://elpais.com/espana/catalunya/2024-01-28/en-las-entranas-del-bruc-el-hotel-con-el-que-la-mesias-abduce-a-millones-de-espectadores.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: elpais.com">[elpais.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">elpais.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Jordi Gili (Igualada, 56 años) acompañó a Grifol a “mirar el cielo” de Montserrat, desde finales de los 80 y hasta 2010: “El grupo era ir...</span></span></span>

This later history neither proves nor completely invalidates what earlier witnesses believed they saw. It does, however, weaken the idea that Montserrat should be treated as a site of regularly demonstrated anomalous activity. After decades of observation, the public record still lacks a securely documented event combining calibrated images, multiple independent positions, reliable timing and supporting aviation or sensor evidence.

The most balanced verdict is therefore that Montserrat is a **major cultural hotspot with a large body of sincere but generally weakly recorded sightings**. Some reports may never be identified because the necessary details were not collected. A few may have involved genuinely unusual appearances. Yet the recurring lights described at the gatherings fit a night sky already populated by aircraft, satellites, meteors, planets, balloons and changing atmospheric conditions. The tradition tells us a great deal about Barcelona’s UFO community, the power of repeated expectation and the social life of unexplained experiences. It tells us much less about the physical origin of any one light seen above the mountain.
