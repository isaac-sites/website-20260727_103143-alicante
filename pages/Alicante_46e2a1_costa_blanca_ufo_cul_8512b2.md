## Introduction

Costa Blanca UFO culture grew less from one conclusive encounter than from a feedback loop between civilian reports, newspaper coverage and people deliberately gathering to watch the sky. Benidorm and Villajoyosa became its most memorable centres during Spain’s wider UFO boom of the 1970s, when unidentified lights were treated not merely as isolated curiosities but as public events. A 1973 report described hundreds of people watching luminous spheres over Benidorm’s Levante beach, while press material from Villajoyosa preserved claims by municipal workers who believed they had seen an unidentified object during their working day.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cadenaser.com/comunitat-valenciana/2025/10/29/del-bosque-a-las-estrellas-el-boca-del-calvari-revive-los-anos-del-festival-ovni-que-benidorm-celebro-en-la-playa-de-levante-radio-benidorm/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cadenaser.com">[Cadena SER]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cadenaser.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Curada por Antonio Cervera, la muestra estará abierta hasta mediados de enero. &quot;Spanish Files&quot; investiga el fenómeno OVNI en España, espe...</span></span></span>

The surviving record is culturally revealing but evidentially uneven. Contemporary newspaper stories are not yet readily searchable online in full, witness details are often incomplete, and later retellings sometimes compress separate reports into a single period of “UFO fever”. Even so, the Costa Blanca material matters within [Alicante]({{ 'why-alicantes-ufo-files-still-invite/' | relative_url }})’s UFO history because it shows how unexplained sightings moved beyond military files and into beaches, terraces, workplaces and collective memory.

## Civilian reports beyond the military files

Official records give one unusually clear example of how an ordinary Alicante resident tried to report an extraordinary sighting. On 25 June 1981, a private citizen wrote to the Chief of Staff of the Spanish Air Force after observing an object above his Alicante flat while standing on the terrace. The report entered the Ministry of Defence archive as a sighting of strange phenomena in Alicante. That shape makes the case more distinctive than a report of a distant point of light. A boomerang or flying-wing description suggests that the witness believed he could see an outline rather than merely brightness or motion. Yet the surviving catalogue information does not supply the measurements needed for a strong reconstruction: there is no publicly summarised duration, angular size, direction of travel, weather record, independent second witness or confirmed comparison with aircraft movements.

The file therefore establishes something narrower but still useful. It confirms that a civilian report was received, retained and eventually declassified. It does not show that military investigators verified an unknown craft. The Defence archive explains that each case file normally contains a summary of the location, date, events, considerations and classification decision, but the depth of investigation varies greatly between reports. A document’s presence in the archive means that an observation was recorded, not that an exotic interpretation was accepted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es/BVMDefensa/exp_ovni/es/micrositios/inicio.do" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es">[Biblioteca Virtual Defensa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cada expediente consta de unas páginas de resumen donde figura el lugar del avistamiento, la fecha, el resumen de los hechos, las conside...</span></span></span>

This difference is important when comparing civilian cases with the better-known Aitana reports. Military sightings might involve radar operators, air-surveillance procedures or several service witnesses. The terrace report depended principally on one person’s observation and drawing. Its evidential strength is therefore modest, even though its official preservation gives it a firmer documentary footing than an unsupported story repeated only in later UFO literature.

The case also captures a broader change in reporting culture. A resident who encountered something puzzling did not simply tell friends or contact a newspaper; he wrote directly to the Air Force. During the height of UFO publicity, civilians increasingly understood unexplained lights as matters that could be reported to military or aviation authorities. The official archive and popular culture were not separate worlds. Public fascination helped generate reports, and the existence of official files in turn made the subject appear more credible.

## Benidorm and Villajoyosa in the press

Benidorm’s most striking contribution was communal rather than forensic. Research presented in the photographic project *Spanish Files* recovered a 1973 report in the Alicante newspaper *Información* describing what later accounts call a UFO festival on Levante beach. Hundreds of residents were said to have watched luminous spheres moving in the sky. The episode transformed observation into spectacle: people were not merely accidental witnesses but members of a crowd assembled around the expectation that something might appear.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cadenaser.com/comunitat-valenciana/2025/10/29/del-bosque-a-las-estrellas-el-boca-del-calvari-revive-los-anos-del-festival-ovni-que-benidorm-celebro-en-la-playa-de-levante-radio-benidorm/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cadenaser.com">[Cadena SER]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cadenaser.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Curada por Antonio Cervera, la muestra estará abierta hasta mediados de enero. &quot;Spanish Files&quot; investiga el fenómeno OVNI en España, espe...</span></span></span>

The word “festival” should be treated carefully. It may describe an organised watch, a newspaper label or a later shorthand for a large public gathering. The currently accessible secondary accounts do not provide the original article’s complete wording, precise date, photographs or a minute-by-minute chronology. Nor do they identify how many observers supplied independent statements rather than responding to the reactions of people around them. The claim of “hundreds” is therefore evidence of reported public participation, not a scientifically controlled count of witnesses.

Even with those limitations, the setting helps explain the story’s power. Levante beach offered a broad horizon and space for a large evening crowd. Benidorm was also one of Spain’s most visible symbols of tourism, nightlife and modern leisure. In such surroundings, sky-watching could become part curiosity, part entertainment and part shared search for the unknown. A distant aircraft, planet, meteor, lantern, flare or atmospheric effect could be seen by many people simultaneously, but collective attention could also encourage observers to interpret an ambiguous light in the same way.

Villajoyosa supplied a different kind of civilian witness story. Material gathered for *Spanish Files* refers to a report in which several municipal workers said they saw an unidentified flying object during their working day. The occupational setting matters because it suggests witnesses who were together for an ordinary reason, rather than people who had assembled specifically in the hope of seeing a UFO.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cadenaser.com/comunitat-valenciana/2025/10/29/del-bosque-a-las-estrellas-el-boca-del-calvari-revive-los-anos-del-festival-ovni-que-benidorm-celebro-en-la-playa-de-levante-radio-benidorm/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cadenaser.com">[Cadena SER]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cadenaser.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Curada por Antonio Cervera, la muestra estará abierta hasta mediados de enero. &quot;Spanish Files&quot; investiga el fenómeno OVNI en España, espe...</span></span></span>

However, the online retellings do not presently provide enough detail to test the report properly. They do not name the workers, give the exact date, reproduce their original descriptions or establish whether the observers reported independently. Without those elements, the Villajoyosa incident remains a press-preserved witness cluster rather than a well-documented case. Several witnesses can strengthen an observation when their accounts are recorded separately and agree on specific details. A group statement published after discussion among the witnesses is less powerful because perception and memory can converge.

Benidorm and Villajoyosa therefore represent two mechanisms by which UFO stories gained local authority. In Benidorm, the number of watchers made the event feel significant. In Villajoyosa, the everyday status of workers gave the report a sense of ordinary credibility. Neither mechanism removes the need for basic evidence such as times, directions, duration, photographs and comparison with known objects in the sky.

## How newspapers helped create a sighting wave

Spain’s UFO boom was inseparable from the mass media. A retrospective produced by the EFE news agency describes the 1970s as a period when sightings and supposed contacts received enormous public attention through newspapers, television, books and cinema. The director of the Andalusian Centre of Photography, discussing the *Spanish Files* exhibition, argued that the media did more than report the phenomenon: they supplied images and expectations through which people interpreted unfamiliar lights.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://efe.com/andalucia/2024-10-07/las-fotos-que-recuerdan-como-vivio-el-fenomeno-ovni-la-espana-de-los-70/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: efe.com">[EFE Noticias]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">efe.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">las fotos que recuerdan como vivio el fenomeno ovni la espana de los 70</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">EFE NoticiasLas fotos que recuerdan cómo se vivió el fenómeno OVNI en los 70October 7, 2024 — Las fotos que recuerdan cómo se vivió el fe...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 7, 2024</span></span></span>

On the Costa Blanca, this process could become self-reinforcing:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* A newspaper publicised an unusual observation.
* More residents began watching the sky or discussing earlier experiences.
* Ordinary celestial or aerial objects received closer attention.
* New reports gave editors material for follow-up stories.
* Repetition made the area appear to be an established hotspot.

This does not mean witnesses invented their experiences. Many probably saw something genuinely unfamiliar to them. The difficulty lies in moving from “unidentified by the observer” to “inherently inexplicable”. An object can remain unidentified because the account lacks sufficient data, not because all conventional possibilities have been eliminated.

Communal observation adds another complication. A crowd can confirm that a visible stimulus was present, but it does not automatically confirm its distance, size or nature. People standing together may take cues from one another about where to look and what they are seeing. Once someone calls a light a sphere, craft or formation, others may adopt the same vocabulary even when the object itself is only a bright point. A crowd report can therefore be socially strong while remaining technically weak.

The period’s visual culture also shaped expectations. Flying saucers, glowing spheres and mysterious formations were already familiar from international UFO reporting and popular entertainment. The EFE account of *Spanish Files* notes that supposed UFO imagery often repeated older religious and mystical visual patterns, while also serving as a form of imaginative escape in a rapidly changing Spain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://efe.com/andalucia/2024-10-07/las-fotos-que-recuerdan-como-vivio-el-fenomeno-ovni-la-espana-de-los-70/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: efe.com">[EFE Noticias]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">efe.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">las fotos que recuerdan como vivio el fenomeno ovni la espana de los 70</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">EFE NoticiasLas fotos que recuerdan cómo se vivió el fenómeno OVNI en los 70October 7, 2024 — Las fotos que recuerdan cómo se vivió el fe...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 7, 2024</span></span></span>

Benidorm amplified that futuristic atmosphere in unusually visible ways. The city’s CAP 3000 nightclub, opened around the turn of the 1970s, was built in the form of a flying saucer. Although a leisure venue is not evidence of a sighting, its design shows how space-age imagery had entered the Costa Blanca’s commercial landscape. UFO symbolism could be mysterious, fashionable and entertaining at the same time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://elmiradordebenidorm.es/que-ver-en-benidorm/cap-3000-discoteca-ovni-ku-benidorm/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: elmiradordebenidorm.es">[El Mirador de Benidorm]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">elmiradordebenidorm.es</span><span class="citation-popover-title">cap 3000 discoteca ovni ku benidorm</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">cap 3000 discoteca ovni ku benidorm</span></span></span>

## The best evidence and the largest gaps

The strongest evidence for Costa Blanca UFO culture is documentary rather than physical. There is an official catalogue entry for the 1981 Alicante civilian report, a preserved administrative trail showing that the Air Force received it, and later reporting that describes the witness’s boomerang drawing. There are also identifiable press traditions concerning the 1973 Benidorm gathering and the Villajoyosa workers, now revisited through local radio and a museum exhibition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es/BVMDefensa/exp_ovni/en/consulta/indice_campo.do?campo=idtitulo" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es">[gob.es]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Avistamiento de fenómenos extraños en EVA-5, Aitana (Alicante): 24 y 26 de Abril y 26 de Julio de 1986 (1994) (1)...</span></span></span>

What is missing is equally important. No publicly accessible source examined here provides a clear photograph of the boomerang-shaped object, calibrated radar data linked to it or a set of independent contemporary statements. The Benidorm episode lacks a readily available complete reproduction of the original *Información* report, while the Villajoyosa account lacks the witness-level detail needed to compare descriptions. Later cultural projects preserve valuable memory, but they are reconstructions assembled decades after the events.

There is also no single explanation that can responsibly be applied to all the reports. Different observations may have had different causes. Plausible recurring possibilities include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* aircraft seen at unfamiliar angles, especially during approach or departure;
* bright planets or stars appearing to move because of cloud, observer movement or the autokinetic effect;
* meteors and fireballs, which can appear dramatic and much closer than they are;
* balloons, flares or illuminated objects;
* unusual clouds or atmospheric optical effects;
* inaccurate estimates of size, distance and speed;
* incomplete reporting that makes an ordinary stimulus impossible to identify later.

</div>

Modern examples show how easily striking natural forms can acquire UFO language. In December 2025, photographs of a lenticular cloud over parts of Alicante spread through social media because the smooth, apparently solid shape resembled a classic flying saucer. Meteorological reporting identified it as a known cloud formation produced by airflow over terrain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.eldebate.com/espana/comunidad-valenciana/20251206/nubes-forma-ovni-sorprenden-alicante-espectacular-formacion-lenticular_362954.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eldebate.com">[El Debate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eldebate.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on eldebate.com.</span></span></span>

That recent example does not explain any particular 1970s report. It illustrates the more general problem: an object can look structured, stationary and extraordinary while having a well-understood cause. Conversely, the absence of a confirmed explanation decades later may simply reflect a shortage of usable observations.

## How public fascination shaped later memory

The Costa Blanca stories survived because they became part of local culture, not because later investigation produced decisive new technical evidence. In 2025, Benidorm’s Boca del Calvari Museum hosted work from José Luis Carrillo’s *Spanish Files*, bringing together press cuttings, official documents, testimony and contemporary photography. Local coverage used the 1973 Levante beach gathering and the Villajoyosa report to explore how residents remembered a period when people went outdoors together hoping to see mysterious lights.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cadenaser.com/comunitat-valenciana/2025/10/29/del-bosque-a-las-estrellas-el-boca-del-calvari-revive-los-anos-del-festival-ovni-que-benidorm-celebro-en-la-playa-de-levante-radio-benidorm/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cadenaser.com">[Cadena SER]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cadenaser.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Curada por Antonio Cervera, la muestra estará abierta hasta mediados de enero. &quot;Spanish Files&quot; investiga el fenómeno OVNI en España, espe...</span></span></span>

This museum treatment shifts the central question. Rather than asking only whether extraterrestrial craft appeared, it asks why the reports mattered so much to the people who made, read and remembered them. The UFO became a container for several emotions: fear of the unknown, excitement about technology, disappointment with familiar institutions and hope that the universe contained something beyond everyday life.

That interpretation is supported by wider assessments of Spain’s 1970s UFO boom. The EFE account describes the subject as both a media phenomenon and an alternative cultural space during the final years of dictatorship and the political transition that followed. It also acknowledges fraud, misleading photographs and the ease with which viewers trusted images that appear far less convincing today.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://efe.com/andalucia/2024-10-07/las-fotos-que-recuerdan-como-vivio-el-fenomeno-ovni-la-espana-de-los-70/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: efe.com">[EFE Noticias]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">efe.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">las fotos que recuerdan como vivio el fenomeno ovni la espana de los 70</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">EFE NoticiasLas fotos que recuerdan cómo se vivió el fenómeno OVNI en los 70October 7, 2024 — Las fotos que recuerdan cómo se vivió el fe...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 7, 2024</span></span></span>

Later remembrance can both preserve and distort. Exhibitions and radio features recover local reports that might otherwise disappear into inaccessible newspaper archives. At the same time, memorable phrases such as “UFO festival” or “hundreds of witnesses” can become detached from the qualifications in the original coverage. A culturally important story may grow clearer in outline while becoming less precise in detail.

This is why the Costa Blanca material should not be ranked simply as either proof or nonsense. The 1981 letter is a real official document describing a civilian claim. The Benidorm and Villajoyosa stories reflect genuine press and community interest. Yet none currently carries the combination of contemporary records, independent testimony, imagery and technical analysis required to establish an extraordinary aerial object.

## Alicante’s civilian legacy

Within Alicante’s wider UFO history, the Costa Blanca reports provide a necessary civilian counterpart to the radar and military cases associated with the Sierra de Aitana. They show how a provincial UFO record is created not only by instruments and official investigations but by newspapers, social expectations, public spaces and the willingness of ordinary people to report what they could not identify.

The boomerang report illustrates individual testimony entering an official system. Villajoyosa represents the credibility attached to a group of everyday workplace witnesses. Benidorm shows the phenomenon becoming collective theatre, with the beach functioning as an open-air observation platform. Together, these episodes explain how UFO fascination could spread even when the underlying evidence remained incomplete.

Later reporting has strengthened confidence that these stories formed part of Costa Blanca public life. It has not substantially strengthened the claim that the reported objects were exotic craft. The most defensible conclusion is therefore cultural and historical: residents saw or believed they saw unusual things, the press magnified those experiences, and communal sky-watching turned uncertainty into a shared local memory. The mystery survives chiefly because the original evidence is too fragmentary to settle it, while the human story around it is unusually vivid.
