## Introduction

In the early hours of 28 November 1979, the Spanish Air Force launched an F-4 Phantom from Torrejón Air Base after radar operators reported unidentified contacts above [Madrid]({{ 'madrids-ufo-history-between-hoax-and/' | relative_url }}). The interceptor spent about 50 minutes at 35,000 feet, was directed repeatedly towards the reported position near Barajas, detected brief and unstable echoes on its own radar and twice received a warning consistent with illumination by a pulsed radar. The crew never saw an object.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Madrid_7a2191_torrejon_radar_scram_0d3e50-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Radar Scramble" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

That combination makes the Torrejón radar scramble one of Madrid’s strongest military UFO cases. It was not merely a distant light reported by civilians: an operational fighter was launched, ground and airborne systems registered anomalies, and a surviving official file preserves the crew’s account. Yet the same record contains the case’s central weakness. The contacts vanished after single radar sweeps, changed from one echo into three elongated patches and never produced a sustained track or visual confirmation. The incident is therefore well documented but not evidence of a confirmed craft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/download/SpanishUFOFiles/1979-11-11-17-28_avistamiento_en_valencia-motril-madrid-ii.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive</span></span></span>

## Why the interceptor was launched

The incident developed across the night of 27–28 November. A later Air Force summary lists the first report at 23:00 on 27 November, when lights were said to have been visible over Madrid and the EVA-2 air-surveillance unit detected unknown echoes. The operational account records that at 02:30 Zulu time on 28 November, the Pegaso control centre received a message stating that two unidentified objects were above Madrid and were being observed by many people. At 03:30, the alert aircraft at Torrejón was ordered into the air.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/download/SpanishUFOFiles/1979-11-11-17-28_avistamiento_en_valencia-motril-madrid-ii.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive</span></span></span>

The distinction between these times matters. Some retellings describe a single uninterrupted event beginning late on 27 November; the file itself appears to combine an earlier public or radar report with the formal scramble several hours later. It does not provide a complete minute-by-minute log establishing that the same targets remained continuously under observation. The safest description is therefore that reports and unknown echoes occurred during the night, followed by an interception mission in the early morning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/download/SpanishUFOFiles/1979-11-11-17-28_avistamiento_en_valencia-motril-madrid-ii.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive</span></span></span>

The fighter was an F-4 Phantom belonging to the Torrejón alert force. Ground control directed it towards a contact reported by the Villatobas radar, south-east of Madrid. The target was initially placed at approximately 80,000 feet, far above the interceptor, which climbed to 35,000 feet and orbited beneath the reported position. This was a genuine air-defence response rather than a retrospective UFO investigation: controllers had an unexplained return in controlled airspace and dispatched an available interceptor to identify it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/download/SpanishUFOFiles/1979-11-11-17-28_avistamiento_en_valencia-motril-madrid-ii.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive</span></span></span>

That response is important, but it should not be misunderstood. A scramble shows that the report was taken seriously enough to check; it does not show that controllers had established the presence of an extraordinary vehicle. Military air-defence systems are designed to investigate uncertain contacts precisely because radar information can be incomplete, erroneous or potentially important.

## What ground and airborne radar recorded

The official account is unusually specific about the interceptor’s instruments. After the crew activated its threat-warning equipment, a signal appeared in the aircraft’s rear-left sector, described as roughly the seven-thirty position and in “band I”. The crew interpreted it as apparently close and made the turns requested by the controller, but never acquired a visual target. The file also states that on two occasions the aircraft appeared to experience illumination or “lock” from a pulsed radar.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/download/SpanishUFOFiles/1979-11-11-17-28_avistamiento_en_valencia-motril-madrid-ii.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive</span></span></span>

The F-4’s own radar produced four contacts that broadly agreed with the directions supplied by Pegaso. These were not stable tracks. The first appeared as a single echo and vanished on the next sweep. Later returns appeared as three elongated, diffuse echoes, separated by roughly 1.5 nautical miles, which again disappeared by the following sweep. The crew repeatedly crossed the indicated location, corresponding approximately to the area above Barajas, without seeing anything.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/download/SpanishUFOFiles/1979-11-11-17-28_avistamiento_en_valencia-motril-madrid-ii.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive</span></span></span>

This is the strongest part of the case because the airborne radar responses were not wholly independent of the ground report, yet they were at least registered aboard the interceptor rather than existing only on a controller’s display. Their occurrence while the fighter was being vectored towards the reported area gives the incident more evidential weight than a lone fleeting echo.

It is also where caution is most necessary. A target that appears for one sweep, disappears immediately and returns with a radically altered shape is much weaker evidence of a solid aircraft than a persistent echo with a measurable course, speed and altitude. The file contains no preserved radar plot, recorded screen imagery or continuous track from which an outside analyst could reconstruct the target’s movement. What survives is a written operational report describing transitory signals.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/download/SpanishUFOFiles/1979-11-11-17-28_avistamiento_en_valencia-motril-madrid-ii.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive</span></span></span>

The claimed altitude is similarly uncertain. Ground information first placed the contact at about 80,000 feet, while later directions reportedly gave considerably lower angular or altitude indications. The aircraft radar echoes were described in relation to the fighter’s antenna angle and estimated height difference rather than through a stable transponder-derived altitude. An apparent height on primary radar is therefore not equivalent to a verified aircraft flying at that level.

## Why the lack of a visual target matters

Weather and visibility were described in the military report as excellent. That removes an easy explanation based on cloud obscuring an otherwise ordinary aircraft. It also makes the repeated failure to see anything more significant: the crew flew over the indicated area several times, looked in the directions supplied by control and found no visible object.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/download/SpanishUFOFiles/1979-11-11-17-28_avistamiento_en_valencia-motril-madrid-ii.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive</span></span></span>

Nevertheless, good visibility does not guarantee that a radar source should have been visible. A false or mislocated radar echo has no object to see. A distant aircraft may also be too small or poorly illuminated for visual acquisition, particularly when altitude information is uncertain. The absence of a sighting therefore cuts in two directions. It prevents the case from becoming a radar-visual encounter, but it is compatible with technical explanations involving propagation, clutter, interference or a real target plotted in the wrong place.

The civilian element is even less secure. The control report says that the objects were being observed by a large number of people, but the declassified Torrejón material does not include a developed body of individual witness statements, viewing positions, angular measurements or consistent descriptions that can be matched against the radar timing. Later summaries often repeat the phrase “many people” without identifying those witnesses or demonstrating that they all saw the same phenomenon.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/download/SpanishUFOFiles/1979-11-11-17-28_avistamiento_en_valencia-motril-madrid-ii.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive</span></span></span>

This evidential gap separates the Torrejón incident from cases supported by detailed independent visual testimony. There may have been widespread reports of lights over Madrid, but the available military document primarily substantiates the radar response and the fighter mission, not the number or reliability of civilian observers.

## Conventional explanations and unresolved gaps

The most obvious conventional possibility is anomalous radar propagation. Temperature and moisture layers can bend radar energy towards the ground, producing extraneous returns from terrain or distant objects. The United States Federal Aviation Administration notes that temperature inversions can create multiple unexplained blips, while technical guidance from the US National Weather Service explains that even part of a beam trapped in a refractive layer can generate strong, unexpected echoes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.weather.gov/media/wrh/online_publications/TAs/ta9318.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: weather.gov">[weather.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">weather.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on weather.gov.</span></span></span>

Such effects fit several features of the Torrejón report:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the contacts were fleeting rather than continuously tracked;
* a single echo became three elongated, diffuse returns;
* the echoes repeatedly disappeared on the following sweep;
* the crew found no object at the indicated location;
* ground estimates of altitude and position appear to have changed.

</div>

None of those characteristics proves anomalous propagation, but they resemble the unstable or false returns that radar systems can generate. Civil and military surveillance systems may also produce false targets through reflections, sidelobes, interference or replies associated with other radar systems. International Civil Aviation Organization material treats false and duplicated radar reports as recognised surveillance problems rather than exceptional phenomena.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.icao.int/sites/default/files/sp-files/SAM/Documents/2010/SURAUTOSEM/25_ICAO_RadarPerformance.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: icao.int">[ICAO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">icao.int</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on icao.int.</span></span></span>

The threat-warning indications require separate treatment. A radar-warning receiver does not identify a physical object; it detects radio-frequency energy matching programmed characteristics. The two pulsed-radar warnings could have come from another military radar, a ground installation, an aircraft outside the crew’s visual field, signal reflections or an equipment anomaly. The report does not provide the frequency, pulse pattern, duration or emitter identification needed to determine the source. Calling the warnings a “lock-on” is understandable from the crew’s perspective, but the surviving evidence cannot establish that an unknown craft deliberately targeted the F-4.

There is no strong basis for blaming a particular weather mechanism on this specific night because the file contains no detailed atmospheric profile, radar-engineering assessment or reconstruction of refractive conditions above central Spain. Historical surface data from Madrid-Barajas indicate generally cold and calm conditions around the end of November, circumstances in which nocturnal inversions can occur, but the available public data are incomplete for 28 November and cannot demonstrate that ducting produced these particular echoes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.tutiempo.net/clima/11-1979/ws-82210.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: tutiempo.net">[www.tutiempo.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">tutiempo.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ws 82210</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ws 82210</span></span></span>

Other ordinary possibilities remain open. The contacts could have involved a combination of genuine aircraft, ground clutter, electronic interference and observational confusion rather than one cause. The reported transformation from one echo to three diffuse returns particularly argues against assuming that every instrument response belonged to a single coherent target. Conversely, no conventional explanation has been demonstrated from the surviving record with enough specificity to close the case.

## How the file was recovered and declassified

The document now associated with the incident was not assembled immediately as a polished investigation. In 1994, during the Spanish Air Force’s effort to centralise and release UFO material, the Joint Chief of the Operational Air Command requested information still held within individual units. The request explicitly referred to reports already discussed publicly and to suspicions that the Air Force was withholding records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/download/SpanishUFOFiles/1979-11-11-17-28_avistamiento_en_valencia-motril-madrid-ii.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive</span></span></span>

The responding command stated that it no longer held full documentation for the event but supplied photocopies concerning the scramble. It also explained why underlying surveillance records might be missing: operational books covering routine watch, equipment status and control-room activity could be destroyed six months after the end of the year in which their final entry was made. This means that potentially decisive material—contemporaneous logs, equipment-status records and detailed radar-room activity—may have been routinely discarded long before UFO declassification began.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/download/SpanishUFOFiles/1979-11-11-17-28_avistamiento_en_valencia-motril-madrid-ii.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive</span></span></span>

That archival history is essential to interpreting the case. The absence of a final explanation is not necessarily evidence that investigators exhausted every possibility and failed. It may instead reflect the survival of a short operational narrative without the technical records needed for reconstruction. The official catalogue identifies the broader Valencia, Motril and Madrid collection as Air Force intelligence material declassified in 1994, while the shorter Torrejón supplement was later assessed as containing no information that still required classification.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es/BVMDefensa/es/consulta/registro.do?id=38287" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es">[Biblioteca Virtual Defensa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Avistamiento de fenómenos extraños: expedientes OVNI España.... Derechos: La copia digital se distribuye bajo licencia CC BY 4.0 que per...</span></span></span>

The file groups the Madrid scramble with other incidents reported in Valencia and Motril during November 1979. That arrangement has encouraged accounts presenting the events as one nationwide UFO wave. The documents establish chronological proximity, but they do not prove that the same object or mechanism caused all three episodes. The Torrejón case should therefore be assessed on its own radar and flight evidence rather than borrowing credibility or mystery from the better-known Manises incident.

## How strong is the case today?

The Torrejón scramble remains Madrid’s most substantial military UFO incident because several elements are secure: an F-4 was launched, military controllers reported unknown contacts, the interceptor registered four fleeting radar echoes, its warning receiver detected pulsed-radar energy, and the crew searched the area without identifying a target. These facts come from an official operational record rather than later folklore.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/download/SpanishUFOFiles/1979-11-11-17-28_avistamiento_en_valencia-motril-madrid-ii.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive</span></span></span>

What the record does not establish is equally important. There was no visual sighting by the interceptor crew, no persistent radar track, no recovered object, no clear measurement of extraordinary speed or manoeuvring, and no surviving technical analysis excluding radar propagation, interference or other system effects. The vaguely reported civilian observations are not documented well enough in this file to provide a reliable second line of evidence.

Later publication strengthened the historical reality of the scramble but weakened some of the more dramatic versions of the story. The declassified papers confirm that something unusual appeared on military systems, yet their precise wording shows how unstable the contacts were. Descriptions such as “very fleeting”, “elongated” and “diffuse”, together with disappearance on the next sweep, are more consistent with uncertain radar phenomena than with a clearly tracked craft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/download/SpanishUFOFiles/1979-11-11-17-28_avistamiento_en_valencia-motril-madrid-ii.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Internet Archive</span></span></span>

The fairest verdict is **unresolved but limited**. The event cannot responsibly be dismissed as an invented tale: the military response and instrument reports are documented. Nor can it be presented as compelling evidence of an exotic vehicle. The surviving file records an unsuccessful attempt to identify unstable radar contacts above Madrid. Its value lies less in proving what was there than in showing how a genuine air-defence alert can remain unexplained when transient signals, missing records and the absence of visual confirmation prevent a firm conclusion.
