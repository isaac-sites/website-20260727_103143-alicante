## Introduction

Los Llanos Air Base gave [Albacete]({{ 'why-albacete-became-central-to-spains/' | relative_url }}) its clearest place in Spanish UFO history because it housed the fighter unit able to respond quickly when an uncertain airspace report became an aviation-security problem. During the Manises incident of 11–12 November 1979, a Mirage F1 from Ala 14 was launched from the base after a passenger aircraft diverted to Valencia and further unusual lights were reported. The documented scramble matters; it shows that the report was treated seriously enough to investigate from the air. It does not show that the base had confirmed an extraordinary craft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.congreso.es/public_oficiales/L1/CONG/BOCG/E/E_378-I.PDF" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: congreso.es">[Congreso de los Diputados]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">congreso.es</span><span class="citation-popover-title">de los Diputados</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Congreso de los Diputados...</span></span></span>

That distinction is the key to understanding Albacete’s role. Los Llanos was not a secret centre for UFO research, nor was it the main location of the sighting. It was an operational fighter base with an alert aircraft, trained personnel and the infrastructure needed to launch an interceptor towards something that had not yet been identified. The province entered the story through military readiness rather than through proof of an alien or otherwise exotic vehicle.

## Ala 14 and the Mirage F1

Ala 14 was established at Los Llanos in 1974, and its first Mirage F1 fighters arrived from France on 18 June 1975. Their introduction substantially altered the base: the runway was extended to 2,700 metres and supporting infrastructure was modernised for the new aircraft. The Mirage remained the unit’s main combat type for almost four decades, accumulating more than 200,000 flying hours before its final Spanish service flight in December 2013.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ejercitodelaireydelespacio.defensa.gob.es/EA/ejercitodelaire/es/Actualidad/Noticias/Noticia/89eb371d-4c30-11f0-9e08-005056a2cb59/?path=%2Fsites%2Finternet.es%2F.content%2FnoticiaEA%2FnoticiaEA_01351.xml&amp;resourceId=89eb371d-4c30-11f0-9e08-005056a2cb59" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ejercitodelaireydelespacio.defensa.gob.es">[Ejército del Aire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ejercitodelaireydelespacio.defensa.gob.es</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ejército del Aire- Actualidad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Noticias - Noticia...</span></span></span>

This timing is important. By November 1979, the Mirage F1 was not an unfamiliar experimental aircraft at Albacete. Ala 14 had operated the type for more than four years and had been organised around the continuing tasks of surveillance, airspace control and combat readiness. An official description of the unit’s mission identifies its role as contributing to the surveillance and control of Spanish sovereign airspace while maintaining its assigned aircraft and personnel in an operational state.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://defensadigital.defensa.gob.es/defensahub/lod/oai%3Abibliotecavirtualdefensa.es%3A1048687.rdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: defensadigital.defensa.gob.es">[defensadigital.defensa.gob.es]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">defensadigital.defensa.gob.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Source details in endnotes.</span></span></span>

The Mirage F1 was therefore a logical aircraft to send towards an uncertain aerial report. It was designed as a fast interceptor and air-superiority fighter, capable of reaching a distant area far more rapidly than a transport aircraft or helicopter. Its value in the Manises episode was practical: it could arrive quickly, search visually, follow directions from ground controllers and attempt to identify whatever had prompted the alert.

Los Llanos itself also offered an advantageous inland position. Situated beside Albacete city, it could support responses towards eastern and south-eastern Spain without being located inside the busy civil-airport environment where the original disruption was unfolding. The connection between Albacete and the case was therefore organisational as much as geographical: the base had the appropriate fighter, trained crew and alert structure when assistance was requested.

## How the alert response worked

The surviving parliamentary record states that a Mirage F1 left Los Llanos “on alert” and spent roughly one hour and forty-five minutes in flight attempting to approach reported unidentified objects. The wording came from a parliamentary question submitted in September 1980, rather than from a complete operational log published at the time, so some of its dramatic detail reflects the claims being put to the government for explanation. Nevertheless, it establishes that the Albacete launch had become part of the official political record.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.congreso.es/public_oficiales/L1/CONG/BOCG/E/E_378-I.PDF" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: congreso.es">[Congreso de los Diputados]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">congreso.es</span><span class="citation-popover-title">de los Diputados</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Congreso de los Diputados...</span></span></span>

Later accounts by the pilot, Fernando Cámara, describe him as serving on the base’s alert roster when the scramble signal sounded. According to his recollection, he entered the prepared Mirage, received directions through the military control network and flew towards Valencia to investigate the lights reported after the commercial aircraft’s diversion. Local reporting has placed the alert signal at about 23:26, although times vary across retellings and should not be treated as perfectly settled without the original sequence of operational records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.eldigitaldealbacete.com/2026/06/22/el-avion-de-esta-rotonda-de-albacete-y-el-caso-ovni-mas-famoso-de-espana-la-historia-real-que-aun-desconcierta-al-piloto-protagonista/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eldigitaldealbacete.com">[El Digital de Albacete]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eldigitaldealbacete.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mirage F-1 instalado en la rotonda de la AB-20 en Albacete Pero ese avió</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mirage F-1 instalado en la rotonda de la AB-20 en Albacete Pero ese avió</span></span></span>

An alert launch is often misunderstood because popular UFO accounts treat it as though the military had already verified an extraordinary target. In reality, the logic runs in the opposite direction. An interceptor is launched because controllers or commanders do not yet have a satisfactory identification and need better information. The purpose is to investigate, establish visual contact where possible, assess whether there is a safety or defence threat and report back.

Modern Quick Reaction Alert procedures provide a useful illustration, although they should not be projected backwards as a precise description of every Spanish procedure used in 1979. Under contemporary air-policing practice, radar or civil-air-traffic information identifies an aircraft or activity of concern; a command authority decides whether to launch; an alert crew takes off within minutes; and controllers guide the interceptor towards the relevant area for identification. The sequence is built around uncertainty, not prior confirmation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ac.nato.int/missions/air-policing?pubDate=20260115" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ac.nato.int">[NATO Allied Command]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ac.nato.int</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Allied Command Allied Air Command &#124; We secure the skies</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NATO Allied CommandAllied Air Command &#124; We secure the skiesJanuary 15, 2026 — Allied Air Command &#124; We secure the skies Home / What we do...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 15, 2026</span></span></span>

A scramble can be triggered by many ordinary but potentially serious situations: an aircraft that has lost radio contact, a missing or incorrect flight plan, an unidentified radar return, an apparent airspace violation or credible reports of unsafe traffic. NATO’s current explanation of air policing explicitly describes unidentified or non-communicating aircraft as hazards requiring investigation and sometimes interception.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ac.nato.int/missions/air-policing?pubDate=20260115" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ac.nato.int">[NATO Allied Command]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ac.nato.int</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Allied Command Allied Air Command &#124; We secure the skies</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NATO Allied CommandAllied Air Command &#124; We secure the skiesJanuary 15, 2026 — Allied Air Command &#124; We secure the skies Home / What we do...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 15, 2026</span></span></span>

The Manises circumstances were unusual because the alert followed reports involving a commercial aircraft, controllers and observers at an airport rather than a clearly tracked conventional aircraft. That made the assignment difficult from the outset. The Mirage pilot was being sent to identify reported lights whose distance, altitude and physical nature were uncertain, not to intercept a target with a stable transponder return and known flight path.

## The mission from Albacete

The parliamentary question recorded that the Super Caravelle crew first reported unidentified traffic to Barcelona control shortly after 23:08 on 11 November 1979. The airliner subsequently diverted and landed at Manises Airport at about 23:50. It also reported claims that unusual lights remained visible around the airport area and that the Mirage from Los Llanos was sent after them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.congreso.es/public_oficiales/L1/CONG/BOCG/E/E_378-I.PDF" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: congreso.es">[Congreso de los Diputados]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">congreso.es</span><span class="citation-popover-title">de los Diputados</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Congreso de los Diputados...</span></span></span>

For Los Llanos, the practical task was identification. Cámara’s later testimony describes seeing luminous phenomena, attempting to close the distance and receiving changing directions from ground control. He has also said that the light was visible to him but was not acquired in the way a normal aircraft target might be by the Mirage’s own systems. These details are central to the mystery narrative, but they largely depend on the pilot’s recollections, interviews and interpretations of cockpit indications rather than a complete publicly available package of raw airborne data.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.eldigitaldealbacete.com/2026/06/22/el-avion-de-esta-rotonda-de-albacete-y-el-caso-ovni-mas-famoso-de-espana-la-historia-real-que-aun-desconcierta-al-piloto-protagonista/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eldigitaldealbacete.com">[El Digital de Albacete]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eldigitaldealbacete.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mirage F-1 instalado en la rotonda de la AB-20 en Albacete Pero ese avió</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mirage F-1 instalado en la rotonda de la AB-20 en Albacete Pero ese avió</span></span></span>

Accounts frequently state that the fighter’s radar-warning receiver activated as though the aircraft were being illuminated by a continuous-wave radar associated with weapon guidance. That would have been operationally significant to the pilot because such a warning can indicate potentially hostile targeting. However, a cockpit warning does not by itself identify its source. It establishes that the equipment detected or interpreted an electromagnetic signal, not that the reported light emitted that signal or that a non-human craft had locked onto the Mirage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.eldigitaldealbacete.com/2026/06/22/el-avion-de-esta-rotonda-de-albacete-y-el-caso-ovni-mas-famoso-de-espana-la-historia-real-que-aun-desconcierta-al-piloto-protagonista/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eldigitaldealbacete.com">[El Digital de Albacete]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eldigitaldealbacete.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mirage F-1 instalado en la rotonda de la AB-20 en Albacete Pero ese avió</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mirage F-1 instalado en la rotonda de la AB-20 en Albacete Pero ese avió</span></span></span>

Reports of radio interference raise a similar evidential problem. Interference during a demanding night flight is relevant and deserves investigation, but the surviving public narrative does not provide enough calibrated technical data to connect it securely to the object the pilot believed he was following. Possible sources could include equipment behaviour, propagation conditions, unrelated transmissions or electronic activity elsewhere in the region. Without synchronised recordings and engineering analysis, temporal coincidence is not proof of a common cause.

The mission ended without a positive identification. Popular retellings frame this as a fighter being “outflown” by an object with extraordinary performance. A more cautious description is that the pilot could not establish and maintain a sufficiently certain position relative to a distant light to identify it. At night, an observer can judge direction reasonably well while badly misjudging distance, scale, altitude and speed, especially when there is no visible background and no reliable radar range.

That limitation is especially important when discussing apparent acceleration. A light that seems stationary and then appears to move as the fighter turns, descends or changes its viewing angle may not be manoeuvring in the way the pilot perceives. Conversely, the absence of a satisfactory lock or range measurement does not prove that the light was stationary or conventional. It means that the strongest claims about speed and evasive movement rest on visual geometry that was difficult to measure.

## What the scramble proves

The Los Llanos response provides three relatively firm conclusions.

**The aviation concern was genuine.** A military fighter was not launched merely because somebody reported a strange star from the ground. The order followed an unfolding aviation event that had already involved a passenger-aircraft diversion and reports from professional personnel. The authorities had a reasonable safety and air-defence basis for seeking more information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.congreso.es/public_oficiales/L1/CONG/BOCG/E/E_378-I.PDF" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: congreso.es">[Congreso de los Diputados]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">congreso.es</span><span class="citation-popover-title">de los Diputados</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Congreso de los Diputados...</span></span></span>

**Ala 14 was operationally prepared.** The incident shows that Albacete possessed more than an airfield and parked aircraft. It had a fighter unit, an alert arrangement, a pilot available for immediate duty and links to the wider control system. That readiness is consistent with Ala 14’s long-term air-surveillance and response mission.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://defensadigital.defensa.gob.es/defensahub/lod/oai%3Abibliotecavirtualdefensa.es%3A1048687.rdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: defensadigital.defensa.gob.es">[defensadigital.defensa.gob.es]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">defensadigital.defensa.gob.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Source details in endnotes.</span></span></span>

**The interception did not resolve the report.** The pilot returned without identifying a conventional aircraft, but also without bringing back publicly available instrument evidence demonstrating a structured extraordinary vehicle. The outcome was operationally inconclusive rather than positive confirmation of a UFO in the popular sense.

The scramble does not prove that ground radar had established a single physical object performing the manoeuvres later attributed to it. The 1980 parliamentary question referred to confidential reports of radar detections, but it was a politician’s detailed statement of the case and questions to the government, not a published technical assessment validating each claim. Its language should therefore be used as evidence that allegations entered the official record, not as automatic verification of those allegations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.congreso.es/public_oficiales/L1/CONG/BOCG/E/E_378-I.PDF" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: congreso.es">[Congreso de los Diputados]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">congreso.es</span><span class="citation-popover-title">de los Diputados</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Congreso de los Diputados...</span></span></span>

Nor does the scramble establish that every light reported during the night had one common source. The airliner crew, airport observers and fighter pilot were in different positions, looking in different directions and at different times. Several [conventional lights]({{ 'explanations/' | relative_url }}) or astronomical objects could have been grouped into a single narrative after the event. Alternatively, some observations may remain unexplained even if others can be identified.

## Why a fighter could fail to identify an ordinary light

The presence of an advanced combat aircraft can make a case sound stronger than it necessarily is. A fighter is extremely capable against another aircraft whose position can be measured, tracked and predicted. It is far less effective as a scientific instrument when chasing a point of light without dependable range information.

At night, visual interception depends heavily on external references. A pilot may see an intense light but have no direct way to tell whether it is a few kilometres away, far beyond the horizon or effectively at astronomical distance. If the assumed distance is wrong, estimated size and speed will also be wrong. A distant fixed light can appear to keep pace with an aircraft because the line of sight changes very little as the aircraft moves.

Atmospheric refraction can further complicate the view. Layers of air at different temperatures can bend, distort or magnify distant light sources, particularly near the horizon. A bright industrial flame, planet or star may shimmer, change colour or appear to shift position. These effects do not automatically explain the Manises reports, but they demonstrate why visual pursuit alone cannot establish extraordinary flight performance.

One later sceptical reconstruction proposed that at least some of the reddish lights were distant flare stacks associated with the industrial complex at Escombreras near Cartagena, made unusually conspicuous by atmospheric conditions. Other conventional candidates discussed in the wider case include bright astronomical objects. These explanations remain disputed by participants, including Cámara, who has argued that the direction and behaviour he observed were inconsistent with the refinery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://academia-lab.com/encyclopedia/manises-ufo-incident/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academia-lab.com">[academia-lab.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academia-lab.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on academia-lab.com.</span></span></span>

The competing claims expose the central evidential gap. To test the flare hypothesis decisively, investigators would need a reliable reconstruction of the fighter’s position, heading and altitude at each observation, together with meteorological profiles, the operating state and brightness of the industrial flames, and accurate timings. To validate claims of extraordinary manoeuvring, they would need equally robust range, speed or multi-sensor tracking data. The public record has not provided either side with a complete dataset of that quality.

## Official records and later interpretation

Spain began declassifying military files on unusual aerial observations in 1991, placing physical copies in the Air Force’s central library in 1992 and later digitising the collection. The Ministry of Defence’s virtual archive now describes 80 case files containing approximately 1,900 pages, covering incidents from 1962 to 1995 in which Air Force personnel or equipment were involved. The files may include summaries, witness interviews, service reports and meteorological material, although their depth varies considerably.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es/BVMDefensa/exp_ovni/es/micrositios/inicio.do" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es">[Biblioteca Virtual Defensa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gob.es.</span></span></span>

Declassification is valuable because it confirms that the military recorded and reviewed such incidents. It should not be confused with official endorsement of extraordinary interpretations. Governments routinely retain reports precisely because the initial information is incomplete, operationally relevant or requires later assessment. An archived “unidentified” observation means that the available investigation did not produce a secure identification in that record; it does not create a new category of confirmed craft.

The Manises documentation is also uneven in evidential quality. Some facts are strongly grounded: the commercial diversion occurred, controllers handled the situation, the fighter launched from Albacete and the pilot completed an unsuccessful identification mission. Other elements—exact distances, multiple coordinated objects, extreme acceleration and a direct causal connection between the observed light and electronic warnings—are less firmly supported because the publicly accessible technical record is incomplete or filtered through later testimony.

Later reporting has often strengthened the story’s cultural prominence without strengthening its measurements. Anniversary articles repeatedly revisit the pilot’s account and the dramatic contrast between a sophisticated fighter and an elusive light. Such interviews preserve valuable first-person testimony, but repetition does not turn recollection into instrument-quality evidence. Recent local coverage still presents Cámara as convinced that what he encountered was not adequately explained, while also demonstrating how closely the episode has become attached to Albacete’s aviation identity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.eldigitaldealbacete.com/2026/06/22/el-avion-de-esta-rotonda-de-albacete-y-el-caso-ovni-mas-famoso-de-espana-la-historia-real-que-aun-desconcierta-al-piloto-protagonista/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eldigitaldealbacete.com">[El Digital de Albacete]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eldigitaldealbacete.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mirage F-1 instalado en la rotonda de la AB-20 en Albacete Pero ese avió</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mirage F-1 instalado en la rotonda de la AB-20 en Albacete Pero ese avió</span></span></span>

## Albacete’s real UFO role

Los Llanos made Albacete operationally central to a famous Spanish UFO case, but geographically secondary to the original sighting. The passenger aircraft was over the Mediterranean, the diversion was to Manises and much of the subsequent observation unfolded around Valencia and the eastern coast. Albacete supplied the response capability.

That role is more historically meaningful than a claim that the province merely happened to own the aircraft involved. Ala 14 embodied the system designed to act when civil aviation reports crossed into uncertainty about sovereign airspace. The Manises scramble shows the point where a witness account became an operational question: was there unidentified traffic, was it hazardous, and could a military pilot determine what it was?

The episode also provides a useful warning about how UFO legends develop. A documented military reaction can become shorthand for “military confirmation”, even though the reaction was an attempt to obtain confirmation. The inability of a fighter to identify a light can become evidence that the light possessed superior technology, even when the absence of range data makes comparison impossible. Electronic warnings can be attributed to the visible phenomenon, even when the source was never established.

The most defensible interpretation is therefore neither dismissal nor extraordinary certainty. Los Llanos responded to a legitimate and unusual aviation report. Its pilot appears to have encountered lights or indications that he could not confidently identify during the mission. The available public evidence does not demonstrate that those observations represented a single structured craft, and plausible conventional explanations remain in dispute rather than conclusively eliminated.

## The lasting connection

The Mirage F1 remains an unusually strong symbol of Albacete’s military-aviation history. In 2026, a restored example was installed as a public monument in the city, reflecting the long relationship between Albacete, Los Llanos, Ala 14 and the local air-maintenance establishment. The aircraft displayed is not evidence from the 1979 mission, but its prominent place in the city illustrates how deeply the Mirage era has entered local identity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.defensa.com/espana/albacete-inaugura-monumento-avion-combate-mirage-f-1-pertenecio" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: defensa.com">[Defensa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">defensa.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Albacete inaugura un Mirage F-1 como monumento aeronáutico de la ciudad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Albacete inaugura un Mirage F-1 como monumento aeronáutico de la ciudad</span></span></span>

Ala 14 now operates Eurofighters rather than Mirage F1s, yet its core readiness function continues. Recent official reporting describes the unit performing Quick Reaction Alert air-policing missions and taking part in scrambles intended to investigate or respond to uncertain or potentially threatening airspace activity. That continuity helps explain the 1979 episode without sensationalism: keeping fighters ready to launch is a normal defence responsibility, even when the report that triggers a launch later becomes famous.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ejercitodelaireydelespacio.defensa.gob.es/EA/ejercitodelaire/es/Actualidad/Noticias/Noticia/c0eab7e4-1e0a-11f1-b0b2-005056a2cb59/?amp%3Bpath=%2Fsites%2Finternet.es%2F.content%2FnoticiaEA%2FnoticiaEA_01462.xml&amp;resourceId=c0eab7e4-1e0a-11f1-b0b2-005056a2cb59" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ejercitodelaireydelespacio.defensa.gob.es">[Ejército del Aire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ejercitodelaireydelespacio.defensa.gob.es</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ejército del Aire- Actualidad</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ejército del Aire- Actualidad</span></span></span>

Los Llanos therefore matters to Albacete’s UFO history for a precise reason. It was the place where an unresolved civilian-air-safety report was converted into a military identification mission. The scramble establishes seriousness, readiness and uncertainty. It does not establish an extraordinary craft—and that boundary between documented response and unproven interpretation is the most important fact in the base’s UFO legacy.
