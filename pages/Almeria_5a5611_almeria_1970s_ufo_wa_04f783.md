## Introduction

Almería’s 1970s UFO wave was not a single continuous outbreak of unexplained aircraft. It was a feedback cycle in which unusual lights, newspaper coverage, radio and television programmes, organised sky-watching and local investigation encouraged one another. The cultural association Avance, led in the late 1970s by Emilio Asensio, gave that cycle a local structure: members watched the sky, collected stories and examined several of the province’s most widely discussed reports.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.juntadeandalucia.es/cultura/agendaculturaldeandalucia/evento/mesa-redonda-ovnis-en-almeria-en-los-anos-70" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: juntadeandalucia.es">[Junta de Andalucía]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">juntadeandalucia.es</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Junta de Andalucía Mesa redonda</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OVNIS en Almería en los años 70Mesa redonda. This period matters because it created the version of Almería’s UFO history that survived in public memory. Reports from July 1974, the [Aguadulce–Roquetas]({{ 'coastal-sightings/' | relative_url }}) coastal corridor and a photograph taken by the lawyer and politician Fausto Romero became memorable partly because investigators and journalists kept returning to them. Yet the surviving public evidence is fragmented. There is no accessible Avance case catalogue with consistently recorded times, bearings, photographs and witness interviews, and later retellings often provide more atmosphere than testable detail. The wave is therefore strongest as a documented episode of local observation and belief, not as proof of extraordinary craft.

## Why the 1970s changed local UFO culture

Earlier reports in Almería had generally depended on individual witnesses and occasional official correspondence. During the 1970s, UFO watching became a shared activity. People did not merely notice something unexpected and report it afterwards; some deliberately travelled to exposed coastal viewpoints, watched the sky for long periods and discussed what they saw with others who already regarded unidentified lights as a subject worth investigating.

The national media environment helped make that possible. Spanish radio and television increasingly treated UFOs, alleged encounters and paranormal claims as serious subjects for discussion. Research on the television programme *Más Allá*, broadcast from 1976, found that UFO material formed a substantial part of its output and that many people who entered Spanish ufology during or after the 1970s credited radio and television with sparking their interest. Presenter Fernando Jiménez del Oso’s sober style was particularly influential because it gave speculative material the appearance of measured scientific inquiry.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/683e/4475a42a1540c0190d1c3d3153bec3770138.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pdfs.semanticscholar.org">[Semantic Scholar]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pdfs.semanticscholar.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ibídem, 27 de marzo de 2005), licenciado en Medicina y Cirugía en la espe-cialidad de Psiquiatría. Almería absorbed this national enthusiasm but developed its own local geography. The coast west of the city offered wide horizons, relatively dark viewing areas and long lines of sight across the Mediterranean. Aguadulce, Roquetas de Mar and the road running beneath the cliffs west of Almería became recurring locations in later accounts. This did not necessarily mean that genuinely anomalous objects concentrated there. It meant that more interested people were looking from the same places, comparing observations and alerting journalists or fellow enthusiasts when they noticed lights.

The province was also becoming more connected to ordinary aviation. Almería airport had operated scheduled services since the late 1960s, and in May 1974 British Airways opened the first regular direct international route from the airport. Charter and international traffic subsequently became increasingly familiar features of the coastal sky.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.diariodealmeria.es/almeria/Cincuenta-primer-vuelo-internacional-directo-Almeria_0_1903611377.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: diariodealmeria.es">[diariodealmeria.es]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">diariodealmeria.es</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cincuenta años del primer vuelo internacional directo desde el aeropuerto de Almería</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cincuenta años del primer vuelo internacional directo desde el aeropuerto de Almería</span></span></span> That changing background is important because distant aircraft seen head-on, landing lights, navigation lamps and turning traffic can look stationary or move in apparently abrupt ways when distance and direction are unknown.

This produced a basic tension that runs through the whole wave. Almería’s open skies made sustained observation easier, but they also exposed watchers to distant aircraft, bright planets, stars near the horizon, meteors, satellites and atmospheric distortion. Without accurate bearings, duration, angular movement and weather information, even an honest and attentive witness could produce a report that later investigators could not resolve.

## Avance turned sightings into a local movement

The Avance cultural association emerged in Almería during the late 1970s as part of the wider Spanish enthusiasm for ufology and paranormal investigation. A 2024 event organised by the Andalusian regional cultural authorities identified Emilio Asensio as the person responsible for Avance at that time and described the organisation as a group whose members watched Almería’s skies and studied some of the province’s most prominent reports.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.juntadeandalucia.es/cultura/agendaculturaldeandalucia/evento/mesa-redonda-ovnis-en-almeria-en-los-anos-70" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: juntadeandalucia.es">[Junta de Andalucía]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">juntadeandalucia.es</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Junta de Andalucía Mesa redonda</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OVNIS en Almería en los años 70Mesa redonda. OVNIS en Almería en los años 70 Inicio Cursos y formación Adultos Almería AAIICC CAF Mesa re...</span></span></span>

That description is significant because it places Avance somewhere between a social club, a field-observation group and an amateur investigative body. Its members could provide witnesses with a place to report experiences, arrange collective watches and preserve stories that might otherwise have disappeared. They also helped establish the idea that Almería was experiencing a recognisable pattern rather than a series of unrelated mistakes or isolated curiosities.

Avance’s work should not, however, be confused with a formal scientific programme. The surviving public material does not show that the association routinely used calibrated cameras, astronomical reference checks, radio monitoring, triangulation from separated observation points or standardised case forms. Nor is there a readily available archive showing how many reports were rejected, explained or left unresolved. That absence makes it difficult to judge whether the group primarily tested claims or mainly documented experiences already considered potentially extraordinary.

Even so, organised observation was an advance on casual storytelling. A group could compare the recollections of several people, return to a location, check whether a light reappeared and ask witnesses about direction, colour and movement. In principle, such methods can reveal ordinary explanations. A light that returns at the same hour may be an aircraft route or an astronomical object; apparent movement seen by only one observer may result from the autokinetic effect, in which a fixed light seems to wander against a dark, featureless background.

The central limitation was that UFO investigation in the 1970s usually began after observers had already noticed something they found remarkable. The investigators were rarely working with continuous instrument records. Their evidence therefore depended heavily on memory, descriptive language and interpretation. Avance could preserve and organise reports, but it could not recover measurements that witnesses had never taken.

## The reports that gave the wave its shape

The surviving record points to a few recurring examples rather than a comprehensive list of well-documented cases. A regional cultural programme reviewing the period singled out a sighting reported during a July 1974 night and the case of Fausto Romero, who said that he had seen and photographed an unidentified object from his home.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.juntadeandalucia.es/cultura/agendaculturaldeandalucia/evento/mesa-redonda-ovnis-en-almeria-en-los-anos-70" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: juntadeandalucia.es">[Junta de Andalucía]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">juntadeandalucia.es</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Junta de Andalucía Mesa redonda</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OVNIS en Almería en los años 70Mesa redonda. OVNIS en Almería en los años 70 Inicio Cursos y formación Adultos Almería AAIICC CAF Mesa re...</span></span></span>

The July 1974 report is frequently mentioned as one of Almería’s notable sightings, but accessible modern summaries provide little of the information needed for reconstruction. Precise witness numbers, observation points, angular size, bearings and contemporary meteorological or aviation checks are not readily available. Its prominence therefore appears to come at least partly from repetition within the local UFO tradition. A memorable date and a dramatic night-time account can survive for decades even when the original documentation has become inaccessible.

Fausto Romero’s involvement carried a different kind of weight. Romero was a recognised Almería lawyer, academic, writer, photographer and later political figure rather than an anonymous claimant. Local retrospective reporting says that he observed unusual objects from a high building overlooking the city and photographed what he saw.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.lavozdealmeria.com/almeria/226896/ano-vinieron-invasores.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lavozdealmeria.com">[La Voz de Almería]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lavozdealmeria.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">La Voz de Almería El año que vinieron los invasores</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">En Almería se desataron las pasiones con los fenómenos paranormales y en medio de tanta fiebre todos conocíamos a algún amigo que asegura...</span></span></span> His social standing and experience with photography made the report more newsworthy, but neither quality guarantees that the photographed object was extraordinary.

A photograph can prove that light reached a camera; it cannot by itself establish the source’s distance, size or nature. Film artefacts, lens reflections, processing defects, camera movement and out-of-focus points of light can all create apparently structured forms. The strength of Romero’s case would therefore depend on the original negative, exposure details, lens and camera information, the sequence of frames, independent witnesses and a clear account of where and when the picture was taken. Those materials are not available in the public sources used for modern retellings. The case remains culturally important but evidentially weak.

Later popular accounts also associate Avance with reports over Roquetas de Mar and Aguadulce on 23 March 1977. One retelling states that the association made calls that night concerning a UFO and links the sightings to an actor’s alleged episode of missing time during a drive towards Almería.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://euroweeklynews.com/2023/11/12/aliens-in-almeria/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: euroweeklynews.com">[Euro Weekly News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">euroweeklynews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Euro Weekly News Aliens In Almeria « Euro Weekly News</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Euro Weekly News Aliens In Almeria « Euro Weekly News</span></span></span> This is a much weaker source than an original case file or contemporary newspaper report. The missing-time story contains no publicly documented journey measurements, medical assessment or independent record sufficient to distinguish an anomalous experience from faulty recollection, fatigue or later embellishment. It illustrates how a report of lights could acquire a more dramatic human narrative as it circulated.

These examples show how the wave formed around cases with different kinds of appeal:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* **A shared night-time report** suggested a wider event rather than an individual mistake.
* **A photograph by a prominent local figure** appeared to offer physical evidence.
* **Repeated coastal sightings** created the impression of a geographic hotspot.
* **Stories of missing time or contact** moved the subject beyond lights in the sky into personal mystery.

</div>

None of these features, however, substitutes for measurements or original documentation. They made the cases memorable, not necessarily stronger.

## How publicity amplified the sightings

Media attention did more than report the Almería wave after it happened. It helped determine which experiences people noticed, how they described them and whether they considered them worth reporting. National programmes supplied a ready-made vocabulary of discs, hovering lights, formations and contact. Local newspapers then made provincial examples visible, confirming to readers that the phenomenon was supposedly happening nearby rather than only in distant countries.

This process can create a reporting wave without any rise in genuinely unusual objects. Once the subject becomes prominent, more people look upwards, ambiguous observations acquire greater significance and witnesses who previously would have remained silent come forward. Each new report then acts as publicity for the next one. The result is not necessarily mass dishonesty. It can arise from sincere observation shaped by expectation and social reinforcement.

The Spanish UFO boom of the period was substantial. A retrospective report connected with the 2024 *Spanish Files* photography exhibition cited hundreds of recorded Spanish sightings in several peak years and stressed the importance of newspapers, cinema, books and broadcast media in spreading the phenomenon. The exhibition’s organisers treated the photographs not simply as evidence of objects but as material showing how visual culture, belief and media authority interacted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://efe.com/andalucia/2024-10-07/las-fotos-que-recuerdan-como-vivio-el-fenomeno-ovni-la-espana-de-los-70/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: efe.com">[EFE Noticias]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">efe.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">las fotos que recuerdan como vivio el fenomeno ovni la espana de los 70</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">las fotos que recuerdan como vivio el fenomeno ovni la espana de los 70</span></span></span>

Almería provides a clear provincial example of this mechanism. A report reached Avance or the press; members organised further observation; more people became alert to unusual lights; and later stories were interpreted against an already established local reputation. By the end of the decade, the claim that Almería possessed especially active UFO skies could itself influence what observers expected to see.

Photography intensified the effect because pictures appeared more objective than testimony. Yet 1970s UFO photographs were often distant, blurred and poorly documented. The director of the Andalusian Centre of Photography has argued that many images from the wider Spanish wave now look unconvincing and that visual deception or fraud formed part of the period’s UFO culture.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://efe.com/andalucia/2024-10-07/las-fotos-que-recuerdan-como-vivio-el-fenomeno-ovni-la-espana-de-los-70/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: efe.com">[EFE Noticias]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">efe.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">las fotos que recuerdan como vivio el fenomeno ovni la espana de los 70</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">las fotos que recuerdan como vivio el fenomeno ovni la espana de los 70</span></span></span> That does not show that Romero’s image or every Almería photograph was fraudulent. It does show why photographs should be assessed from original negatives and technical context rather than accepted because they resemble the era’s familiar flying-saucer imagery.

Publicity also blurred the line between investigation and promotion. Groups needed media attention to locate witnesses and attract members, while newspapers needed vivid stories. Both benefited when a case remained open and intriguing. A mundane identification might be accurate but generate little continued interest. An unresolved light, by contrast, could sustain articles, night watches and public discussion.

## What Avance achieved — and what it could not prove

Avance’s clearest achievement was cultural and archival. It brought together people who took local reports seriously, established observation as a group activity and helped preserve the names, places and stories that define Almería’s 1970s UFO period. Emilio Asensio’s participation in a public discussion at the Andalusian Centre of Photography in 2024 shows that the association remains part of the recognised history of the province’s engagement with the subject.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.juntadeandalucia.es/cultura/agendaculturaldeandalucia/evento/mesa-redonda-ovnis-en-almeria-en-los-anos-70" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: juntadeandalucia.es">[Junta de Andalucía]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">juntadeandalucia.es</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Junta de Andalucía Mesa redonda</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OVNIS en Almería en los años 70Mesa redonda. The group also provided a form of local ownership. Almería’s reports were not interpreted only by nationally known writers visiting from elsewhere. Residents developed their own networks, chose observation sites and debated incidents within the province. That helps explain why the 1970s wave left a deeper impression than the isolated 1968 military notification: it produced a community as well as case stories.

What Avance did not leave, at least in an accessible public form, is an evidence base strong enough to establish extraordinary vehicles. The most important missing elements are:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* complete contemporary witness statements rather than later summaries;
* observation times accurate enough to check aircraft and astronomical objects;
* bearings and elevations from more than one location;
* original photographic negatives with exposure and camera data;
* records of ordinary cases that investigators successfully explained;
* a surviving catalogue showing how conclusions were reached.

</div>

These gaps matter because an investigation cannot be assessed solely from its most mysterious conclusions. A reliable case archive should reveal the full process: what was reported, what checks were made, which possibilities were eliminated and why a case remained unresolved. Without that trail, later readers cannot distinguish a genuinely resistant anomaly from a report that simply lacked enough information.

## The strongest explanation for the wave

No single explanation is likely to cover every report. Some witnesses may have seen aircraft, others bright planets, meteors, satellites or unusual atmospheric effects. A small number may remain unidentified because their records are too incomplete, not because ordinary explanations were ruled out.

The most convincing explanation for the wave as a whole is therefore social and observational rather than extraterrestrial. Several forces acted together:

**More people were watching.** Organised sky watches greatly increased the amount of time interested observers spent looking at dark horizons.

**Expectation shaped interpretation.** National radio and television had taught audiences to treat unusual lights as possible craft and to describe them through the imagery of contemporary ufology.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/683e/4475a42a1540c0190d1c3d3153bec3770138.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pdfs.semanticscholar.org">[Semantic Scholar]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pdfs.semanticscholar.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ibídem, 27 de marzo de 2005), licenciado en Medicina y Cirugía en la espe-cialidad de Psiquiatría. **Reports generated further reports.** Local publicity encouraged witnesses to reinterpret earlier experiences or come forward with new ones.

**Weak data preserved uncertainty.** Most observations lacked the measurements needed for a secure identification, allowing unresolved cases to remain in circulation.

**Recognisable local figures increased credibility.** The involvement of people such as Fausto Romero and Emilio Asensio made the subject socially visible and harder to dismiss as a fringe pastime.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.juntadeandalucia.es/cultura/agendaculturaldeandalucia/evento/mesa-redonda-ovnis-en-almeria-en-los-anos-70" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: juntadeandalucia.es">[Junta de Andalucía]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">juntadeandalucia.es</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Junta de Andalucía Mesa redonda</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">OVNIS en Almería en los años 70Mesa redonda. This interpretation does not require witnesses to have lied. It recognises that observation is shaped by distance, darkness, expectation and memory. It also explains why the most durable result of the 1970s was not a verified object but a provincial UFO culture.

## How the period should be judged

Almería’s 1970s wave deserves a place in the province’s history because it changed who watched the sky, how sightings were reported and how ambiguous experiences entered local memory. Avance and Emilio Asensio were central to that change. They turned scattered claims into an organised subject and helped establish the coastal viewing tradition associated with Aguadulce, Roquetas de Mar and the approaches to Almería city.

The evidence supports three conclusions. First, Almería residents genuinely reported unusual lights, and some reports involved organised observers or publicly known witnesses. Second, Avance played a real role in collecting and sustaining interest in those reports. Third, the available material does not demonstrate that the objects were extraterrestrial or technologically extraordinary.

Later reporting has strengthened the historical picture of the movement by confirming Avance’s existence, Asensio’s role and the importance attached to the 1974 and Fausto Romero cases. It has not substantially strengthened the physical evidence. The original measurements, negatives and systematic investigation records needed to re-evaluate the main sightings remain absent from the accessible record.

The fairest assessment is therefore that the wave was **historically significant but scientifically inconclusive**. Its defining mechanism was the interaction of observation, organisation and publicity. Avance did not prove that extraordinary craft were present over Almería, but it helped create the network through which uncertain lights became the province’s best-known UFO era.
