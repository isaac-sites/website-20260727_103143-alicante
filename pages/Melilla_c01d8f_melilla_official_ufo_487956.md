## Introduction

Spain’s declassified UFO archive contains 80 Air Force case files covering reports from 1962 to 1995, but [Melilla]({{ 'how-strong-is-melillas-ufo-evidence/' | relative_url }}) is not named in the catalogue of publicly accessible sighting files. That is the clearest official finding: familiar Melilla stories, including the bright events reported in 1976 and 1977 and the later naval radar account, should not be described as confirmed or investigated by the Ministry of Defence unless a specific document can be produced.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es/BVMDefensa/exp_ovni/es/micrositios/inicio.do" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es">[Biblioteca Virtual Defensa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gob.es.</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Melilla_c01d8f_melilla_official_ufo_487956-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Official Files" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The gap is important, but easy to overinterpret. Spain’s collection was never a complete register of every unusual light reported by civilians, police officers, sailors or local newspapers. It consists of incidents in which Air Force personnel or equipment became involved in some way. A Melilla report might therefore have gone unrecorded, remained in another institution’s papers, been treated as an ordinary meteorological or astronomical event, or simply never reached the military reporting system. Archival absence weakens claims of official endorsement; it does not prove that witnesses saw nothing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es/BVMDefensa/exp_ovni/es/micrositios/inicio.do" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es">[Biblioteca Virtual Defensa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gob.es.</span></span></span>

## What Spain’s declassified archive actually contains

The Spanish Ministry of Defence says that the declassification process began in 1991 in response to public demand for access to documents concerning “strange phenomena”. A physical copy of the material was deposited in the Air Force’s central library in 1992 and was later digitised for the Defence Virtual Library. The resulting collection contains about 1,900 pages in 80 files, beginning with reports at San Javier in Murcia in August 1962 and ending with an incident at Morón in Seville in February 1995.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es/BVMDefensa/exp_ovni/es/micrositios/inicio.do" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es">[Biblioteca Virtual Defensa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gob.es.</span></span></span>

These are not merely lists of rumours. Depending on the case, a file can contain a summary, the date and location, witness interviews, operational logs, weather information, maps, technical considerations and a recommendation on classification or declassification. Some files run to only a few pages; others contain dozens or even hundreds. Names of witnesses and reporting officers may be removed for privacy, but the structure usually allows readers to see what information investigators possessed and how they reached their assessment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es/BVMDefensa/exp_ovni/es/micrositios/inicio.do" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es">[Biblioteca Virtual Defensa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gob.es.</span></span></span>

The archive also shows that “officially documented” does not mean “officially accepted as extraordinary”. Its files range from brief, inconclusive reports to complex incidents involving pilots, air-defence units, radar stations and multiple observation points. Some remained unidentified because the available evidence was inadequate. Others were linked to conventional causes, including astronomical objects, aircraft activity or rocket debris. One multi-location event on 23 February 1971, for example, was ultimately attributed to the atmospheric re-entry of a French research rocket.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufotransparency.com/international/files/es" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufotransparency.com">[UFO Transparency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufotransparency.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UFO Transparency Spain files · 01 Disclosures · UFO Transparency</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO Transparency Spain files · 01 Disclosures · UFO Transparency</span></span></span>

That distinction provides a useful standard for evaluating Melilla. In the strongest Spanish cases, readers can inspect a chain of paperwork: who received the report, what operational checks were made, whether radar or aviation records were consulted, which explanations were considered and what conclusion was entered. Melilla’s best-known accounts, as currently available to the public, do not offer that same documentary trail.

## Melilla is missing from the published case catalogue

The Defence Virtual Library’s title index lists the geographical subjects of the released cases. It includes incidents from the Canary Islands, Madrid, Barcelona, Murcia, Valencia, the Balearic Islands, Galicia, Navarra, Seville and numerous military air-surveillance stations. It also contains reports spanning several locations and one case described broadly as occurring over the Atlantic and the Rif. None of the 80 sighting-file titles identifies Melilla.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es/BVMDefensa/exp_ovni/es/consulta/indice_campo.do?campo=idtitulo" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es">[Biblioteca Virtual Defensa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gob.es.</span></span></span>

The catalogue contains 83 records in total, but three are supporting or contextual items rather than additional local sightings: a chronological case list, an interview concerning the declassification programme and a collection of military regulations on UFO reporting. The underlying official collection remains 80 case files. Melilla does not appear as a dedicated case title in either half of that published list.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es/BVMDefensa/exp_ovni/es/consulta/indice_campo.do?campo=idtitulo&amp;posicion=81" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es">[Biblioteca Virtual Defensa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gob.es.</span></span></span>

This is more meaningful than a failed keyword search on an unofficial database. It is a direct comparison with the Ministry of Defence’s own public catalogue. Nevertheless, the conclusion must be phrased carefully: there is **no clearly dedicated Melilla file in the published Air Force collection**, not necessarily no mention of Melilla anywhere on every scanned page. Multi-location files can contain observations beyond the place names chosen for their titles, and incomplete optical character recognition can make internal references difficult to find.

One potentially relevant title concerns an observation over the “Atlantic Ocean–Rif” on 8 December 1980. The Rif is the northern Moroccan region bordering the wider area around Melilla, but the catalogue entry does not identify the city itself. Without evidence placing the event over Melilla or linking it to a known local account, it should not be quietly converted into a Melilla case.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es/BVMDefensa/exp_ovni/es/consulta/indice_campo.do?campo=idtitulo" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es">[Biblioteca Virtual Defensa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gob.es.</span></span></span>

## Official files look different from local retellings

The contrast is clearest when Melilla’s recurring stories are set beside the normal contents of a Defence file. Local summaries commonly describe a brilliant object on 29 December 1976, a copper-coloured disc reportedly seen on 22 August 1977 and a naval radar contact associated with the waters between Melilla and Ceuta in 1983. Yet the publicly available versions generally lack a file number, issuing unit, signed statement, operational log, radar plot, weather appendix or formal conclusion.

That does not automatically make the accounts false. It places them in a different evidential category: reported local incidents preserved mainly through later retellings rather than demonstrably through the Air Force investigation system. The distinction matters because a witness’s public position, a military setting or a reference to radar can make a story sound official even when no official document has been shown.

A genuinely Defence-backed claim should normally answer several basic questions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* **Which service or command recorded it?** The Air Force archive identifies the responsible military bodies and often preserves the administrative route by which a report was handled.
* **What is the file number and date?** Strong cases can be tied to a specific record rather than a later narrative.
* **What evidence survives?** Contemporary testimony, operational logs, meteorological data and instrument records are more valuable than a summary written decades later.
* **What did investigators conclude?** Inclusion in an archive may show only that a report was received, not that its extraordinary interpretation was accepted.
* **Is the released document complete enough to check?** Redacted names are normal, but missing times, directions, durations and technical records sharply limit independent analysis.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es/BVMDefensa/exp_ovni/es/micrositios/inicio.do" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es">[Biblioteca Virtual Defensa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gob.es.</span></span></span>

</div>

By those standards, Melilla’s familiar cases remain locally reported rather than Defence-confirmed. That wording is not dismissive; it simply reflects the present public record.

## Why a real sighting might never enter this collection

The archive’s scope explains part of Melilla’s weak official footprint. The Ministry describes the files as reports within Spanish airspace in which Air Force personnel or equipment were involved in some fashion. It was therefore an institutional collection, not a universal reporting centre for every civilian observation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es/BVMDefensa/exp_ovni/es/micrositios/inicio.do" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es">[Biblioteca Virtual Defensa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gob.es.</span></span></span>

A report could fall outside the released series for several reasons. A civilian might tell only a newspaper or local police officer. A bright meteor could be recognised quickly and never treated as an air-defence concern. A maritime incident might remain in naval rather than Air Force records. Local military paperwork could be retained in an Army archive, while aviation documents might be filed under a control centre, flight route or regional command rather than under Melilla.

This is particularly relevant to the alleged 1983 *Cadarso* radar episode. If the report originated aboard a naval patrol vessel, its natural documentary trail would not necessarily sit in an Air Force UFO collection. Yet that possibility cannot be used as evidence that a naval file exists. Until a ship’s log, incident report, radar record or archival reference is identified, the story remains an unverified account of an alleged military instrument contact.

Melilla also has its own intermediate military archive, administered by the Spanish Army. It holds extensive non-digitised material and accepts research and reproduction requests, but its existence should not be mistaken for proof that UFO records are preserved there. It merely shows that the Defence Virtual Library is not the whole universe of military documentation connected with the city.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ejercito.defensa.gob.es/unidades/Madrid/ihycm/Archivos/archivos-intermedios/melilla-archivo-intermedio.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ejercito.defensa.gob.es">[Ejército de Tierra]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ejercito.defensa.gob.es</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ejército de Tierra Archivo Intermedio Militar de Melilla</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ejército de TierraArchivo Intermedio Militar de Melilla - Ejército de tierraArchivo Intermedio Militar de Melilla - Ejército de tierra In...</span></span></span>

## What the absence proves — and what it does not

The missing Melilla title supports three firm conclusions.

First, there is no basis for presenting the city’s best-known sightings as cases publicly authenticated by Spain’s declassified Air Force archive. The Ministry’s catalogue is detailed enough to name many smaller towns, individual flights, radar units and multi-location events, yet it does not list a Melilla case.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es/BVMDefensa/exp_ovni/es/consulta/indice_campo.do?campo=idtitulo" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es">[Biblioteca Virtual Defensa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gob.es.</span></span></span>

Second, Melilla’s published UFO history depends more heavily on secondary retellings than the better-documented Spanish military cases. That lowers confidence in precise details, especially descriptions of altitude, speed, shape, witness numbers and official reactions that cannot be checked against contemporary records.

Third, the gap makes ordinary explanations harder to test. A proper file might contain weather observations, aircraft movements, radar status or astronomical checks. Without those materials, an unusual light may remain “unresolved” only because the information needed to identify it was never preserved.

The archival absence does **not** prove that no unusual observations occurred. It also does not prove that records were destroyed, suppressed or kept secret. Both claims go beyond the evidence. The released collection has defined institutional boundaries, covers only 1962–1995 and omits personal details even within disclosed cases. It is therefore substantial but not exhaustive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es/BVMDefensa/exp_ovni/es/micrositios/inicio.do" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es">[Biblioteca Virtual Defensa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gob.es.</span></span></span>

Claims of concealment require positive evidence: a reference to a missing file, a declassification schedule that excludes a known document, testimony from a records custodian, or correspondence confirming that a particular report remains classified. A blank space in a catalogue is not enough.

## A more accurate status for Melilla’s cases

The most defensible classification is not “officially confirmed” or “officially disproved”, but **poorly documented in the accessible official record**. Each incident can then be judged on its own evidence.

The December 1976 luminous event is weakened by its resemblance to a large, widely visible fireball: a brilliant passage, rapid movement, flashes, illumination of the ground and a lingering trail are all compatible with a meteor. An Air Force file might have provided useful corroboration, but its apparent absence does not make an exotic interpretation more likely.

The August [1977 disc]({{ '1977-disc/' | relative_url }}) report is more difficult to assess because its striking shape and low apparent altitude come chiefly from narrative testimony. Without original statements, viewing geometry, duration, weather data or independent documentation, it is unresolved in the everyday sense that its cause cannot now be established—not in the stronger sense of having resisted a thorough official investigation.

The alleged 1983 naval radar contact potentially deserves separate archival research because instrument reports can be more valuable than unaided visual sightings. Yet radar echoes can also arise from equipment behaviour, propagation effects, sea clutter, transponder confusion or ordinary targets. The crucial evidence would be the contemporary operational record, not the later use of the word “radar”.

## The gap is the central finding

Melilla’s near-absence from Spain’s declassified UFO files is not a hidden confirmation waiting to be decoded. It is evidence that the city’s best-known stories have not yet been connected publicly to the kind of official documentation available for cases elsewhere in Spain.

That changes how the province’s UFO history should be written. Witness reports may still be described, and their local importance can be acknowledged, but official status must be reserved for incidents backed by identifiable records. Until such records emerge, the strongest accurate formulation is that Melilla has a small body of reported sightings, some intriguing and some plausibly explained, but no clearly dedicated case in the familiar public Air Force archive.

The gap also points towards the most useful future evidence: contemporary newspaper pages, municipal or police logs, airport records, ship documentation and properly referenced files from the relevant military service. Any one of those could strengthen, weaken or resolve a local story. In their absence, caution is not scepticism for its own sake. It is the lesson taught by Spain’s own archive: a dramatic account becomes an official case only when the paperwork can be found and examined.
