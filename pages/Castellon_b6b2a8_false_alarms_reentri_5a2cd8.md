## Introduction

Castellón’s most convincing “false UFO alarms” are not stories in which nothing happened. Witnesses often saw a real, spectacular event: a bright meteor, a shallow-skimming fireball, an orbital re-entry or a luminous trail distorted by upper-atmospheric winds. The mistake came later, when distance, speed and apparent manoeuvres were inferred from a view that offered almost no reliable sense of scale.

The province is particularly well placed for such confusion. Its coast provides long, unobstructed horizons over the Mediterranean, while high-altitude events can remain visible across several Spanish regions at once. A light apparently hanging offshore may actually be more than 100 kilometres above the Earth and hundreds of kilometres from Castellón. Modern camera networks now reconstruct these displays from several viewpoints, turning what once remained an enduring UFO report into a measurable atmospheric trajectory. The clearest local example is the Mediterranean fireball of March 2024, whose interpretation changed repeatedly before specialists identified it as a natural, shallow-entry meteoroid.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.csic.es/es/actualidad-del-csic/la-red-de-investigacion-de-bolidos-y-meteoritos-apunta-que-el-bolido-detectado-sobre-el-este-peninsular-fue-producido-por-la-reentrada-de-un-objeto-artificial-en-orbita-terrestre" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: csic.es">[csic.es]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">csic.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">un objeto artificial en órbita terrestre &#124; Consejo Superior de Investigaciones CientíficasMarch 30, 2024 — La Red de Investigación de Ból...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 30, 2024</span></span></span>

## Meteor and orbital re-entry signatures

A meteor begins when a natural fragment of rock or cometary material enters the atmosphere at very high speed. Friction is not quite the right description of the heating process: the object compresses the air ahead of it so violently that the gas and the meteoroid’s surface become incandescent. Most meteors last only a fraction of a second, but larger bodies can create brilliant fireballs, fragment into several lights or leave glowing trains that remain visible after the solid material has disappeared.

Orbital debris enters differently. A discarded rocket stage or satellite usually begins re-entry at roughly orbital velocity, far slower than the fastest natural meteoroids. It may therefore cross a much larger span of sky over tens of seconds or even minutes. As its structure breaks apart, several incandescent fragments can travel together, producing the familiar “formation of lights” that witnesses sometimes interpret as windows, escort craft or a single enormous vehicle. Research using Spanish meteor-camera observations has shown that these networks can reconstruct slow, curved re-entry paths and compare them with tracked spacecraft or rocket objects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.01004" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Using fireball networks to track more frequent reentries: Falcon 9 upper stage orbit determination from video recordingsSeptember 2...</span></span></span>

The distinction is often visible in recordings, although no single clue is infallible:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Natural meteors** are generally faster, shorter-lived and more likely to show an abrupt flash or terminal fragmentation.
* **Re-entering hardware** usually moves more slowly, remains visible for longer and separates into numerous similarly paced fragments.
* **Earth-grazing meteors** can imitate re-entries because they enter at a shallow angle and travel hundreds of kilometres through the upper atmosphere.
* **Persistent trains** can twist after the meteor has gone, creating apparent curves or spirals that were not part of the object’s actual flight.
* **Lens reflections** can create a second, fainter “object” moving in a different or opposite direction inside a camera image.

</div>

These categories matter to Castellón because a witness on the coast may see only a distant portion of the event. The horizon conceals much of the geometry, while haze can hide the faint leading body and leave only its broad illuminated wake. A long-lasting train may then seem to be the exhaust of an unidentified craft rather than material and ionised gas left at high altitude.

## The 2024 Mediterranean fireball reconstruction

At 11.59 pm on 29 March 2024, a brilliant object crossed the north-eastern Iberian sky and travelled towards the Mediterranean. It was recorded from numerous locations, including Sant Mateu, Benicàssim and the Pic Bartolo area in Castellón. Reports and videos arrived quickly from a much wider region, demonstrating that the phenomenon was not local to one town or one witness.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.csic.es/es/actualidad-del-csic/la-red-de-investigacion-de-bolidos-y-meteoritos-apunta-que-el-bolido-detectado-sobre-el-este-peninsular-fue-producido-por-la-reentrada-de-un-objeto-artificial-en-orbita-terrestre" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: csic.es">[csic.es]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">csic.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">un objeto artificial en órbita terrestre &#124; Consejo Superior de Investigaciones CientíficasMarch 30, 2024 — La Red de Investigación de Ból...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 30, 2024</span></span></span>

The first interpretations were confused. The event received the provisional identifier **SPMN290324ART**, with the suffix reflecting an early suspicion that it might be artificial. A ballistic missile was briefly considered, partly because of the object’s long, shallow path and the tense international context in which an unexplained luminous track could easily be read as military activity. The Spanish Fireball and Meteorite Network then moved towards an orbital re-entry explanation after examining preliminary images and measurements. The CSIC publicly ruled out a ballistic missile and stated that satellite or rocket debris appeared more likely at that stage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.csic.es/es/actualidad-del-csic/la-red-de-investigacion-de-bolidos-y-meteoritos-apunta-que-el-bolido-detectado-sobre-el-este-peninsular-fue-producido-por-la-reentrada-de-un-objeto-artificial-en-orbita-terrestre" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: csic.es">[csic.es]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">csic.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">un objeto artificial en órbita terrestre &#124; Consejo Superior de Investigaciones CientíficasMarch 30, 2024 — La Red de Investigación de Ból...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 30, 2024</span></span></span>

That was not the final conclusion. Further trajectory calculations by several specialist groups indicated that the object was a natural Earth-grazing fireball: a fragment of an asteroid entering the atmosphere at a very shallow angle. It became visible near the vertical of Perpignan, passed over or close to north-eastern Spain and continued towards the Balearic region, where it completely disintegrated. Later reporting stated that neither a missile nor a Starlink-related re-entry fitted the reconstructed motion. Spain’s Space Surveillance Operations Centre also confirmed the meteoroid interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cdn.agenciasinc.es/Noticias/El-objeto-que-sobrevolo-el-Mediterraneo-fue-un-trozo-de-asteroide" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdn.agenciasinc.es">[Sinc]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdn.agenciasinc.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">SincEl objeto que sobrevoló el Mediterráneo fue un trozo de asteroideEl objeto que sobrevoló el Mediterráneo fue un trozo de asteroide As...</span></span></span>

The case is valuable precisely because the scientific interpretation changed. That sequence was not evidence of a cover-up or official indecision about an extraordinary craft. It showed how classification works when analysts receive incomplete observations in real time. A long luminous path suggested a missile or orbital object; measurements from more cameras then supplied the speed and geometry needed to favour a natural body.

The Castellón recordings were especially useful because they provided separate sight lines from the eastern side of the peninsula. Multi-station analysis allows researchers to triangulate the luminous path rather than treating a single witness’s apparent direction as a true trajectory. Dedicated fireball networks routinely derive altitude, speed, radiant direction and possible Solar System orbit from this kind of data.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.11186" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Data on 824 fireballs observed by the digital cameras of the European Fireball Network in 2017-2018. The March 2024 event also explains why coastal observations can be so deceptive. An Earth-grazing meteoroid follows a nearly horizontal luminous path through very thin upper air. To an observer beneath or to one side of that path, it may appear to fly level, change altitude slowly or head deliberately towards the sea. Its unusual duration makes it feel controlled, although the underlying motion is ballistic.

## Why trails can appear to turn or spiral

Witness reports often describe the most puzzling part of a fireball after the object itself has vanished. A bright meteor can leave a persistent train made of ionised gas, vapour and fine ablation products. At heights where these trains form, winds do not necessarily blow in the same direction or at the same speed at every altitude. Different sections of the trail are displaced unevenly, bending an originally straight line into waves, hooks or coils.

Perspective magnifies the effect. A trail viewed lengthways can look compressed into a loop; a broad curve seen from below may resemble a spiral. As the train expands and fades, its brighter sections seem to detach or reverse direction. None of these apparent movements requires the original body to have turned. They are changes in the wake, not manoeuvres by the object.

Rocket-related displays can produce genuinely spiral-shaped clouds by a different mechanism. A rotating upper stage may vent fuel or gas, and the expanding material can reflect sunlight after local sunset. This can create a large luminous whirlpool that remains visible far longer than a meteor. Such displays are increasingly familiar because launch and re-entry activity has grown, but their scale is difficult to judge without a known reference.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.livescience.com/space/space-exploration/ghostly-spiral-photobombs-perseid-meteors-over-several-us-states-and-experts-are-unsure-what-caused-it" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: livescience.com">[Live Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">livescience.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Live Science Ghostly &#x27;spiral&#x27; photobombs Perseid meteors over several US states</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Live Science Ghostly &#x27;spiral&#x27; photobombs Perseid meteors over several US states</span></span></span>

Cameras introduce further false motion. In January 2025, a greenish superbolide visible from Castellón, Alicante, Murcia and Albacete produced an internal lens reflection described locally as a small “ghost”. The reflection appeared to move in the opposite direction from the real fireball. To someone viewing only a compressed clip, it could resemble a second object reacting to the first. Specialists instead identified it as an optical artefact generated by the fireball’s extreme brightness.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cadenaser.com/comunitat-valenciana/2025/01/24/un-asteroide-ha-cruzado-este-lunes-el-cielo-de-castellon-radio-castellon/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cadenaser.com">[Cadena SER]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cadenaser.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cadena SERUn asteroide ha cruzado este viernes el cielo de Castellón</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cadena SERUn asteroide ha cruzado este viernes el cielo de Castellón</span></span></span>

This is an important warning for modern UFO evidence. Video is more useful than memory only when the recording is examined technically. Lens flare, automatic exposure, digital stabilisation and rolling-shutter distortion can add apparent objects or movements that were not present in the sky.

## The Vinaròs trail of 1983

The same mechanisms help reinterpret Castellón’s best-known official UFO case. On 12 July 1983, a French visitor in Vinaròs reported watching an unusual high-altitude phenomenon for more than a minute through binoculars. He could not distinguish a solid object, but he saw a broad luminous trail forming rising and descending curves and what appeared to be spiral movements. His wife and daughter were present, and the report was submitted to the municipal police the following day. It eventually entered the Spanish Air Force’s official files.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://planetabenitez.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/1983-07-12_avistamiento_en_vinaroz_castellon.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: planetabenitez.com">[Planeta Benitez]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">planetabenitez.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on planetabenitez.com.</span></span></span>

The sighting coincided with reports from military aircraft, air-traffic personnel and civilian crews elsewhere in Spain. This wider observation establishes that a large luminous event probably occurred. It does not establish that a structured craft performed the reported movements. The central visible feature was the trail, while the witness could not identify a body even with binoculars. That combination is consistent with a distant high-altitude event whose wake was brighter and easier to see than its source.

Investigators considered a meteor, re-entering debris and related conventional explanations. They lacked the modern tools that resolved the March 2024 fireball: synchronised digital recordings, precise timestamps from multiple stations, calibrated camera positions and readily accessible orbital catalogues. The principal family had also left Spain before detailed interviews could be completed. As a result, the case could not be reconstructed with enough precision to choose confidently between a natural fireball and an artificial re-entry.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://planetabenitez.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/1983-07-12_avistamiento_en_vinaroz_castellon.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: planetabenitez.com">[Planeta Benitez]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">planetabenitez.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on planetabenitez.com.</span></span></span>

The most cautious verdict is therefore **plausibly explained but not conclusively identified**. The apparently spiralling flight should not be treated as firm evidence of intelligent control, because the report may describe deformation of a luminous train rather than the path of a solid object. The 2024 Mediterranean reconstruction strengthens that ordinary interpretation by demonstrating, in the same regional sky, how a shallow natural fireball can first be classified as a possible missile, then as artificial debris, and finally as a meteoroid once sufficient measurements are available.

## Why Castellón produces convincing false alarms

Castellón does not need an unusual concentration of aerial craft to generate memorable UFO reports. Its geography and observing conditions are enough to make high-altitude events unusually striking.

The Mediterranean provides a dark, open background with few buildings or mountains to indicate distance. Objects moving beyond the coast may appear lower and nearer than they really are. A fireball hundreds of kilometres away can seem to descend into the sea, even when its luminous flight ends tens of kilometres above it. The province also lies within the viewing range of events over Catalonia, Valencia, the Balearic Sea and the western Mediterranean, so a phenomenon need not pass directly over Castellón to dominate its sky.

Modern networks change the evidential balance. The May 2024 Iberian superbolide, although its main path lay over western Iberia and the Atlantic rather than Castellón, illustrates what can now be measured from coordinated observations. Researchers combined recordings from twelve Spanish Meteor Network stations, an ESA camera and space-based sensors to determine that a roughly metre-scale carbonaceous body entered at about 40 kilometres per second, began glowing at roughly 130 kilometres altitude and followed a luminous path more than 500 kilometres long.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ieec.cat/en/the-18-may-giant-superbolide-possibly-originated-in-a-jupiter-family-comet/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ieec.cat">[IEEC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ieec.cat</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The 18 May giant superbolide possibly originated in a Jupiter-family comet</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The 18 May giant superbolide possibly originated in a Jupiter-family comet</span></span></span>

That level of reconstruction exposes the weakness of older UFO estimates. A witness may honestly report impossible acceleration, a reversal or a spiral, yet possess no independent measurement of range, altitude or speed. Once several calibrated cameras observe the same event, an apparently erratic display can become a straightforward trajectory plus fragmentation, wind-distorted wake or optical artefact.

## From unidentified to explained

Castellón’s meteor and re-entry cases suggest a practical hierarchy for assessing future alarms. A report is strongest when it contains an exact time, an unedited recording, a fixed horizon, a known camera location and independent observations from widely separated points. Colour and apparent size are much less reliable because cameras process bright lights differently and witnesses lack a stable scale in a dark sky.

A dramatic description alone is not enough to distinguish a meteor from orbital debris. Duration, fragmentation and measured velocity are more useful. Nor should an initial specialist statement be mistaken for a final identification. The March 2024 case passed through several hypotheses because the first public assessment preceded full reconstruction.

Within Castellón’s UFO history, these events matter because they show how unresolved reports are created. A rare-looking display is observed accurately in broad outline but interpreted through uncertain distance and motion. When no adequate follow-up is possible, the “UFO” label survives. When multiple cameras, trajectory calculations and orbital checks are available, the same kind of event usually moves from unidentified to ordinary—without becoming any less spectacular.
