## Introduction

One of the earliest flying-saucer stories associated with [Ourense]({{ 'ourense-ufo-history-between-legend-and/' | relative_url }}) appeared in the local press on 26 March 1950. Francisco Castelao and his driver, Artemio Gómez, said that while travelling towards the city before dawn they watched an exceptionally bright object moving above the Alto do Rodicio mountain road. They first took it for a star, but decided that its apparent movement, size and brilliance did not fit that explanation. The object remained visible until the road began to descend and the surrounding terrain blocked their view.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.laregion.es/provincia/historia-4-tiempos-dia-hoy_1_20250326-3463597.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: laregion.es">[La Región]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">laregion.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on laregion.es.</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Ourense_35c272_alto_rodicio_early_r_136643-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Early Reports" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The report matters less as evidence of an exotic aircraft than as a marker of cultural change. Within three years of the phrase “flying saucer” entering international journalism, a puzzling light over an inland Galician road was already being interpreted through the same new vocabulary. Yet the surviving account is extremely short. It contains no precise time, direction, duration, weather record or independent investigation, leaving several ordinary explanations possible and no reliable way to select between them.

## The Alto do Rodicio observation

The incident took place while Castelao and Gómez were climbing the Alto do Rodicio on their way to Ourense. According to the later reproduction of the contemporary report, the two men saw an object “at great height” that was very bright and moved at a regular speed. They initially thought it was a star but rejected that possibility because of its apparent dimensions and extraordinary brilliance. They watched it for some time before losing sight of it as they started down the other side of the mountain pass.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.laregion.es/provincia/historia-4-tiempos-dia-hoy_1_20250326-3463597.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: laregion.es">[La Región]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">laregion.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on laregion.es.</span></span></span>

That is almost the entire evidential record. There was no claimed landing, close approach, unusual sound, physical effect or encounter with occupants. The witnesses did not describe a metallic disc or provide a detailed shape. Strictly speaking, the report concerns a moving light that the newspaper placed within the developing flying-saucer story.

The setting may have made the experience particularly striking. Alto do Rodicio stands roughly 950–980 metres above sea level on the elevated eastern edge of the Maceda basin. The pass overlooks a pronounced escarpment, with a substantial drop towards the lower country to the west. Roads through such terrain repeatedly open and close views of the sky as vehicles round bends, cross ridges and begin descents.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://info.igme.es/ielig/LIGInfo.aspx?codigo=CIs024" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: info.igme.es">[IGME Information Server]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">info.igme.es</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ALLARIZ &#124; 226 &#124; 17</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">IGME Information ServerIELIG - CIs024: Escarpe de falla de RodicioFebruary 20, 2018 — El Alto del Rodicio (978 m) el borde oriental eleva...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 20, 2018</span></span></span>

This helps explain why the observation ended without the object necessarily disappearing. The account says that the men lost sight of it when they began descending. That wording is compatible with the light being hidden by the landscape, the vehicle’s changing orientation or the road itself rather than accelerating away or vanishing abruptly. It is a modest but important distinction: the report records an interrupted line of sight, not a demonstrably extraordinary departure.

The presence of two named witnesses gives the story slightly more weight than an anonymous rumour. Both apparently saw enough to discuss and reject their first interpretation. Nevertheless, they were travelling together in the same vehicle and viewing the same scene from almost the same position, so their testimony does not supply independent geographical triangulation. Without observations from another location, the object’s distance, altitude and true speed cannot be calculated.

## Ourense joins the saucer age

The newspaper’s framing is as historically revealing as the sighting itself. It described a worldwide “fever” or “psychosis” concerning flying saucers and presented the Rodicio report as evidence that Ourense was no exception.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.laregion.es/provincia/historia-4-tiempos-dia-hoy_1_20250326-3463597.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: laregion.es">[La Región]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">laregion.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on laregion.es.</span></span></span> That language shows that the local story was understood from the beginning as part of an international media phenomenon, rather than as an isolated astronomical observation.

The expression “flying saucer” had spread after American pilot Kenneth Arnold reported nine unusual objects near Mount Rainier in June 1947. Arnold compared their movement to saucers skipping over water; newspaper coverage rapidly transformed that analogy into a description of saucer-shaped craft. Hundreds of reports followed in the United States, establishing a recognisable template through which unfamiliar lights and aerial objects could be interpreted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://time.com/3930602/first-reported-ufo/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: time.com">[Time]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">time.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">This Is Why People Think UFOs Look Like &#x27;Flying Saucers</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The fascination grew to include an incident on July 7, when a New Mexico rancher found what was initially thought to be a crashed flying...</span></span></span>

By 1950 the subject had moved beyond news reports into popular publishing and entertainment. Spanish flying-saucer books and pamphlets began appearing that year, mixing speculation, fiction and commentary on reports already circulating through the press. This does not show that Castelao or Gómez invented what they saw. It does show that an observer and a journalist in 1950 possessed a newly fashionable category into which an otherwise unidentified light could be placed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://marcianitosverdes.haaan.com/2020/09/los-platillos-voladores-por-j-m-dez-gmez-el-primer-libro-de-ufos-en-espaa-1950/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: marcianitosverdes.haaan.com">[Marcianitos Verdes]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">marcianitosverdes.haaan.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Marcianitos Verdes“Los platillos voladores”, por J</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">M. Díez Gómez, el primer libro de ufos en España (1950) &#124; Marcianitos Verdes“Los platillos voladores”, por J. M. Díez Gómez, el primer li...</span></span></span>

The timing was also favourable to Cold War speculation. Reports of unidentified aircraft were frequently discussed as possible secret weapons, rockets or advanced machines rather than exclusively as extraterrestrial visitors. Intelligence organisations collected foreign newspaper stories partly because public reports might contain information about unconventional aircraft, even when the material was unverified. A declassified American compilation from 1952, for example, simply translated and collated Spanish and North African press accounts, explicitly labelling them unevaluated information rather than confirmed incidents.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/document/0005516147" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">FLYING SAUCERS IN SPAIN AND NORTH AFRICA &#124; CIA FOIA (foia.cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FLYING SAUCERS IN SPAIN AND NORTH AFRICA &#124; CIA FOIA (foia.cia.gov)January 31, 2011 — FLYING SAUCERS IN SPAIN AND NORTH AFRICA &#124; CIA FO...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 31, 2011</span></span></span>

Alto do Rodicio therefore belongs to the first stage of Ourense’s UFO history: a period when the saucer idea had arrived, but local reporting had not yet developed the elaborate occupants, landed craft and dialogue found in some later provincial stories. It is valuable as a historical threshold—an ordinary journey recast through a new international mystery.

## Why the object cannot be identified today

Several conventional possibilities remain open, but the evidence is too incomplete to endorse any one of them confidently.

A celestial object is one possibility. Mars was unusually prominent in late March 1950, reaching opposition on 23 March and its closest point to Earth on 26 March. Around opposition it was visible for much of the night and was near its brightest for that apparition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://in-the-sky.org/news.php?id=19500323" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: in-the-sky.org">[In-The-Sky.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">in-the-sky.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mars at opposition</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mars at opposition</span></span></span> Saturn had also reached opposition earlier that month.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://in-the-sky.org/news.php?id=19500307" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: in-the-sky.org">[In-The-Sky.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">in-the-sky.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Saturn at opposition</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Saturn at opposition</span></span></span>

That coincidence makes an astronomical misidentification worth considering, particularly because the witnesses themselves first thought they were looking at a star. Apparent motion can be produced when a bright planet is viewed from a moving car against foreground trees, ridges or clouds. A road that changes direction can make a nearly fixed light seem to travel across the sky or follow the vehicle.

However, this remains a hypothesis rather than a solution. The report does not give the observation’s exact clock time, viewing direction or position along the road. It is therefore impossible to determine whether Mars, Saturn, another bright object or the Moon would have occupied the relevant part of the sky. The men’s reference to regular movement might describe genuine travel, apparent motion caused by the car, or simply a newspaper summary of a more ambiguous impression.

An aircraft is another plausible candidate. A steady light seen at night provides few reliable clues to distance, and an approaching aircraft can appear almost stationary before seeming to move sideways as its angle changes. Yet there is no surviving information about local flight activity, engine noise, navigation lights or the direction of travel. Calling it an aircraft would consequently be as speculative as calling it a planet.

A meteor is less well matched if the object truly remained visible “for a while” and moved at a regular pace. Most meteors cross the visible sky in seconds, although a distant fireball or persistent luminous trail can produce more complicated impressions. Again, the absence of a measured duration prevents a firm exclusion.

Atmospheric effects, including bright stars or planets distorted by thin cloud, haze or temperature layers, cannot be assessed because no weather description survives. Mountain viewpoints can offer unusually clear horizons, but they can also produce variable mist, low cloud and abrupt changes in visibility.

The central weaknesses are simple and decisive:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* no exact observation time;
* no compass direction or angular elevation;
* no reliable duration;
* no description of colour or detailed shape;
* no weather or visibility record;
* no known observation from a second location;
* no photograph, physical trace or instrument detection;
* no surviving police, aviation or military inquiry.

</div>

Without those details, investigators cannot reconstruct the sky, compare possible aircraft movements or test the reported motion against the route. Later repetition has preserved the story but has not added new evidence. The modern account in *La Región* is valuable because it reproduces the names and wording of the early report; it does not transform the brief newspaper item into a thoroughly investigated case.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.laregion.es/provincia/historia-4-tiempos-dia-hoy_1_20250326-3463597.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: laregion.es">[La Región]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">laregion.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on laregion.es.</span></span></span>

## A historical case, not a strong unknown

The Alto do Rodicio sighting deserves a place in the history of UFO reporting in Ourense because of its date and cultural setting. It shows that by March 1950 the flying-saucer narrative had reached provincial Galicia and could shape the reporting of an unexplained light seen during a routine road journey.

Its evidential status is much weaker. The witnesses probably did see a conspicuous light, and the surviving account gives no particular reason to accuse them of fabrication. But sincerity does not establish the nature of a distant object. Their initial comparison with a star, the absence of a described craft and the loss of visibility during the descent all leave room for an astronomical, atmospheric or aviation explanation.

The fairest classification is therefore **unidentified because insufficiently documented**, not unexplained after rigorous investigation. The case remains interesting precisely because it captures the moment Ourense entered the flying-saucer era, while also demonstrating how little information many early saucer reports preserved for later researchers.
