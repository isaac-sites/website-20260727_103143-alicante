## Introduction

The Ranadoiro UFO flap was a short-lived wave of night-time light reports in western [Asturias]({{ 'asturias-ufos-between-mystery-and/' | relative_url }}) in February 1989. Residents around the Sierra de Ranadoiro described red lights hovering over hills, following vehicles and apparently changing shape. The most dramatic accounts included lights crossing a car’s path, a brilliant object travelling beside a tractor and a semicircular “apparatus” seen near farm buildings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mas.lne.es/aquella-asturias/media/pdf/A1-22980494-1.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mas.lne.es">[mas.lne.es]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mas.lne.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Salvador Martínez, a la izquierda, con su nieto en brazos, se encontró con «el sob&gt; una noche, conduciendo su... quienes decían haber vi...</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Asturias_60aedd_ranadoiro_1989_ufo_f_7e7c01-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Ranadoiro" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

Something evidently disturbed several local witnesses, but the surviving evidence is too weak to establish what each person actually saw. There are no known instrument readings, radar records, physical traces or authenticated photographs tied to the episode. Much of the detailed story comes from one contemporary newspaper feature, supplemented decades later by retellings of that article. The most defensible conclusion is therefore not that Ranadoiro was a hoax or an extraterrestrial visitation, but that genuine alarm, ambiguous lights and rapidly expanding publicity combined to create Asturias’s most memorable UFO flap.

## The witnesses and reported lights

The reports centred on rural communities between the districts of Tineo and Salas, including Ranadoiro, La Corriquera and locations along roads leading from La Espina. By 23 February, *La Nueva España* was describing fear and disbelief across the district. The newspaper stressed that numerous residents claimed to have seen the phenomenon, while growing numbers of visitors were arriving in the hope of seeing it themselves. One local comparison captured the atmosphere: the area had begun to resemble a village festival.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mas.lne.es/aquella-asturias/media/pdf/A1-22980494-1.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mas.lne.es">[mas.lne.es]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mas.lne.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Salvador Martínez, a la izquierda, con su nieto en brazos, se encontró con «el sob&gt; una noche, conduciendo su... quienes decían haber vi...</span></span></span>

The accounts did not describe one consistent object. Instead, witnesses reported a changing collection of red points, bright glows and structured-looking forms.

Valentín Menéndez, a 23-year-old resident known locally as Tito, gave the story its most dramatic driving encounter. He said that at about two in the morning, while returning alone from La Espina, he noticed three red lights keeping pace with his car. According to his account, they followed him for roughly four kilometres and then crossed close in front of his windscreen near La Campona. He feared that he was about to collide with them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://funjdiaz.net/folklore/07ficha.php?ID=5144&amp;NUM=514" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: funjdiaz.net">[Fundación Joaquín Díaz]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">funjdiaz.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fundación Joaquín DíazFundación Joaquín Díaz - Revista de Folklore...</span></span></span>

After reaching home, Menéndez woke his family. His mother, Adamina Fernández, and his sister Marisa reportedly watched three red lights resting above a nearby mountain. Their descriptions changed as the night continued: the triangular arrangement became streaks and later appeared to break into numerous small oval lights. Another family member subsequently said that people at the La Espina petrol station had also watched the display.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://funjdiaz.net/folklore/07ficha.php?ID=5144&amp;NUM=514" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: funjdiaz.net">[Fundación Joaquín Díaz]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">funjdiaz.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fundación Joaquín DíazFundación Joaquín Díaz - Revista de Folklore...</span></span></span>

That apparent corroboration sounds important, but its limitations are equally important. The newspaper did not identify or interview the petrol-station observers individually, establish whether everyone was watching at the same time, or compare their independent descriptions before witnesses had discussed the event. It therefore strengthens the case for a visible light in the distance, but not necessarily for the close pursuit described during the earlier car journey.

Other stories followed the same pattern of a light appearing to accompany a moving vehicle. Anselmo, another Ranadoiro resident, was said to have experienced flashes illuminating the rear of his car while travelling with his sister-in-law. They reportedly wondered whether the vehicle was on fire and at one point felt that something might have settled above it. The journalist acknowledged that Anselmo was unavailable for interview, so this part of the story reached the newspaper indirectly through neighbours.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://funjdiaz.net/folklore/07ficha.php?ID=5144&amp;NUM=514" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: funjdiaz.net">[Fundación Joaquín Díaz]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">funjdiaz.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fundación Joaquín DíazFundación Joaquín Díaz - Revista de Folklore...</span></span></span>

Salvador Martínez described an even brighter encounter while returning by tractor from La Espina at about 10.30 pm. He initially mistook the light for the Moon, then perceived something with three pointed features travelling beside him. He estimated that it remained about seven metres to his left and no more than fifteen metres above the ground, following his turns for approximately six kilometres. At one point, he said, it illuminated the hillside more strongly than daylight.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mas.lne.es/aquella-asturias/media/pdf/A1-22980494-1.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mas.lne.es">[mas.lne.es]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mas.lne.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Salvador Martínez, a la izquierda, con su nieto en brazos, se encontró con «el sob&gt; una noche, conduciendo su... quienes decían haber vi...</span></span></span>

Two stationary encounters broadened the story beyond roads. Edmundo Fernández said he saw a semicircular object between a raised granary and an old house while tending a cow that was giving birth. He described inward-curving projections, three bright points and a blue light apparently hanging beneath one of them. On another occasion, he reported a glow powerful enough to reveal trees hundreds of metres away in the middle of the night.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://funjdiaz.net/folklore/07ficha.php?ID=5144&amp;NUM=514" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: funjdiaz.net">[Fundación Joaquín Díaz]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">funjdiaz.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fundación Joaquín DíazFundación Joaquín Díaz - Revista de Folklore...</span></span></span>

María Lorences, aged 75, reported seeing an immense red light near the ground on two successive evenings. She said it moved forwards, backwards, upwards and downwards for several minutes. The experience frightened her so badly that she was unable to sleep.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://funjdiaz.net/folklore/07ficha.php?ID=5144&amp;NUM=514" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: funjdiaz.net">[Fundación Joaquín Díaz]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">funjdiaz.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fundación Joaquín DíazFundación Joaquín Díaz - Revista de Folklore...</span></span></span>

Taken together, the reports clearly show emotional impact and repeated observation. They do not, however, produce a stable description. The phenomenon appears variously as three lights, a triangle, oval points, streaks, a three-cornered object, a semicircular machine, a low red “sun” and a light source bright enough to illuminate a mountainside. That variation may mean several ordinary stimuli were being grouped under one developing story rather than everyone repeatedly observing the same object.

## Why the evidence remains weak

The Ranadoiro case is memorable because its testimony is vivid, not because its documentation is strong. The principal contemporary source is a newspaper report built from interviews, local recollections and descriptions collected after the story had already begun circulating. It is valuable as evidence of what residents were saying in February 1989, but it is not equivalent to a controlled investigation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mas.lne.es/aquella-asturias/media/pdf/A1-22980494-1.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mas.lne.es">[mas.lne.es]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mas.lne.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Salvador Martínez, a la izquierda, con su nieto en brazos, se encontró con «el sob&gt; una noche, conduciendo su... quienes decían haber vi...</span></span></span>

Several weaknesses prevent a confident reconstruction.

**Distances were estimated at night.** Witnesses gave apparently precise figures such as seven metres, ten metres or two metres above the ground. Yet the distance to an isolated light is extremely difficult to judge without a clearly visible object of known size. A bright source kilometres away can appear small and nearby; once a witness assumes it is close, its apparent motion and brightness may be interpreted accordingly.

**Movement beside a vehicle is ambiguous.** A distant light near the horizon can appear to follow a car because its viewing angle changes very little as the observer moves. This familiar effect is often noticed with the Moon, bright planets, lights on hills and distant vehicles. It does not neatly explain every reported manoeuvre, particularly a light said to cross directly in front of a windscreen, but it offers a straightforward explanation for the repeated claim that a light maintained a car’s or tractor’s speed.

**The accounts were not collected independently.** By the time the newspaper arrived, villagers knew that other people had reported red lights, pursuit experiences and objects over the mountains. Witnesses were discussing the episodes in homes, villages and public places. That does not make them dishonest. It does mean that later perceptions and recollections could be shaped by a shared vocabulary and by expectations about what the light was supposed to do.

**Some testimony was second-hand.** The report openly noted that Anselmo had not been interviewed. Other supposed corroborators, including people at the petrol station, were mentioned but not individually questioned in the surviving article. Details may therefore have passed through several narrators before reaching print.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://funjdiaz.net/folklore/07ficha.php?ID=5144&amp;NUM=514" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: funjdiaz.net">[Fundación Joaquín Díaz]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">funjdiaz.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fundación Joaquín DíazFundación Joaquín Díaz - Revista de Folklore...</span></span></span>

**Descriptions changed between witnesses and even within single episodes.** Three points became streaks and then many ovals. A distant light became a structured object. A glow initially mistaken for the Moon was later assigned pointed features and an extremely low altitude. Such changes can reflect a genuinely variable stimulus, but they can also result from atmospheric distortion, movement through trees, changes in viewing position or attempts to impose a recognisable form on glare.

Most importantly, the episode produced no surviving evidence that could be examined independently. The newspaper said visitors arrived with cameras and even hoped to communicate with extraterrestrials, yet no clear photographic sequence from those gatherings has become part of the documented case.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://funjdiaz.net/folklore/07ficha.php?ID=5144&amp;NUM=514" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: funjdiaz.net">[Fundación Joaquín Díaz]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">funjdiaz.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fundación Joaquín DíazFundación Joaquín Díaz - Revista de Folklore...</span></span></span> There is likewise no publicly established radar plot, air-traffic report, police measurement or physical effect that connects the various sightings.

This distinguishes Ranadoiro from Asturias’s 1969 Gijón case, which entered the Spanish Air Force’s administrative system and survives in the national collection of declassified UFO files. Spain’s Virtual Defence Library provides online access to the military case archive deposited for public consultation, but the Ranadoiro flap is known primarily through the press rather than a comparable official dossier.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es/BVMDefensa/exp_ovni/es/micrositios/inicio.do" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es">[Biblioteca Virtual Defensa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gob.es.</span></span></span>

## Which explanations fit the reports?

No single conventional explanation can be proved from the surviving material. The reports occurred at different times and locations and may not share one cause. A mixed explanation is more plausible than assuming that every witness observed the same object.

Bright astronomical objects are an obvious possibility for some of the more distant lights. The Moon was full on 20 February 1989 and underwent a total lunar eclipse that night. It would have remained conspicuous during the surrounding period and could appear reddish during or close to eclipse conditions. The February astronomical calendar also shows several bright planets and lunar conjunctions during the month.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://in-the-sky.org/newscal.php?maxdiff=7&amp;month=2&amp;year=1989" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: in-the-sky.org">[In-The-Sky.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">in-the-sky.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Calendar of Astronomical Events 1989 - In-The-Sky.orgCalendar of Astronomical Events 1989 - In-The-Sky.org Calendar of Astronomical Event...</span></span></span>

Astronomy alone does not account for every detail, particularly lights reported near farm buildings or sudden illumination behind a vehicle. It does, however, matter that Salvador Martínez initially thought his light was the Moon. That admission suggests the source had at least some qualities of a distant celestial object before its apparent distance and form were reinterpreted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://funjdiaz.net/folklore/07ficha.php?ID=5144&amp;NUM=514" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: funjdiaz.net">[Fundación Joaquín Díaz]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">funjdiaz.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fundación Joaquín DíazFundación Joaquín Díaz - Revista de Folklore...</span></span></span>

Road traffic is another credible contributor. The Sierra de Ranadoiro’s hills, bends, vegetation and separated roads could allow headlamps to appear, vanish, split into several lights or sweep across slopes. A vehicle travelling on another road may seem to match an observer’s movement without being visible as a complete vehicle. Headlamps seen through mist, damp air or branches can become diffuse red or white glows and may briefly illuminate trees or fields.

This possibility is strongest where the descriptions involve lights moving with cars, turning as a tractor turns or shining across the rear of a vehicle. It is weaker for the reported stationary semicircular object, unless that account involved nearby machinery, a lit building or overlapping lights whose context was invisible in darkness.

Atmospheric conditions could have amplified either celestial or artificial sources. Moisture, haze and low cloud can scatter light, enlarge glare and create luminous patches without clear edges. Temperature layers can also distort distant sources near the horizon. None of these mechanisms requires the witnesses to invent what they saw; they change how ordinary lights look and make reliable estimates of shape, distance and speed exceptionally difficult.

The changing colours and forms may also indicate multiple stimuli. A person watching a known “UFO area” for several hours could encounter stars, aircraft, vehicle lights, reflections and local illumination, treating each unfamiliar appearance as another phase of the same phenomenon. Ranadoiro should therefore not be approached as a puzzle requiring one object capable of performing every reported action.

## How publicity turned sightings into a flap

A UFO “flap” is not simply one incident with many witnesses. It is a period in which reports multiply, public attention intensifies and ambiguous observations begin to be interpreted through the same extraordinary framework. Researchers and historians have long noted that publicity can create perceptual contagion: once people are told that unusual objects are present, they watch the sky more closely and classify previously ignored lights as possible UFOs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/story/how-ufo-sightings-became-an-american-obsession" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">How UFO Sightings Became an American Obsession</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The period following his sighting saw a surge in UFO sightings across the U.S. This surge, or &quot;flap,&quot; is linked to societal fears of inva...</span></span></span>

Ranadoiro displays this process unusually clearly. The earliest reported driving experience became known among relatives and neighbours. Other residents began linking their own sightings to it. Curious visitors then travelled from La Espina and elsewhere, carrying cameras and looking deliberately for the supposed object. The contemporary report described so many people gathering that residents compared the scene to a festival.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mas.lne.es/aquella-asturias/media/pdf/A1-22980494-1.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mas.lne.es">[mas.lne.es]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mas.lne.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Salvador Martínez, a la izquierda, con su nieto en brazos, se encontró con «el sob&gt; una noche, conduciendo su... quienes decían haber vi...</span></span></span>

Increased observation naturally produces increased reporting. Rural night skies contain moving aircraft, headlights on distant roads, stars low above ridges, reflections and intermittent lights normally given little attention. Once an area acquires a reputation, every unusual point of light becomes potentially relevant. Reports then feed further visits, which generate further reports.

Social reinforcement also affects interpretation. Someone who sees a distant red light after hearing that triangular red lights have followed a neighbour’s car is less likely to describe it neutrally as an unidentified glow. The existing story supplies an identity, expected behaviour and emotional meaning. Fear can further narrow attention and strengthen the impression that a light is approaching, pursuing or reacting intelligently.

The newspaper’s treatment amplified both sides of the episode. It preserved the witnesses’ names and local landmarks, giving the story immediacy and a strong sense of place. At the same time, its language placed very different observations inside one UFO narrative before their relationships had been established. The article did acknowledge uncertainty and distinguished direct interviews from hearsay, but it offered no systematic testing of sight lines, traffic routes, weather, astronomical positions or independent timelines.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://funjdiaz.net/folklore/07ficha.php?ID=5144&amp;NUM=514" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: funjdiaz.net">[Fundación Joaquín Díaz]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">funjdiaz.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fundación Joaquín DíazFundación Joaquín Díaz - Revista de Folklore...</span></span></span>

Later retellings have mostly preserved the dramatic witness descriptions rather than resolving them. A 2024 article in the *Revista de Folklore* returned at length to the newspaper testimony and treated the event as part of Asturias’s cultural tradition of mysterious lights. It provides useful access to the original accounts, but it does not introduce photographs, measurements or newly discovered official records capable of strengthening the physical case.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://funjdiaz.net/folklore/07ficha.php?ID=5144&amp;NUM=514" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: funjdiaz.net">[Fundación Joaquín Díaz]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">funjdiaz.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fundación Joaquín DíazFundación Joaquín Díaz - Revista de Folklore...</span></span></span>

## The most balanced assessment

The Ranadoiro witnesses should not be dismissed merely because their descriptions sound extraordinary. Several people were named, some spoke directly to a journalist, and their fear appears sincere. The episode was more than an anonymous rumour. A cluster of residents genuinely believed that they had encountered unusual lights around the Sierra de Ranadoiro.

Sincerity, however, cannot establish the nature of a distant light. The accounts were collected after discussion had spread, critical distances were estimated under poor conditions, several details were second-hand and no independent physical or instrumental record emerged. The differences between descriptions are so large that treating them as observations of one coherent machine creates more problems than it solves.

The strongest interpretation is that the flap combined real but unidentified visual stimuli with errors of distance and movement, followed by intense local reinforcement. Celestial objects, vehicle lights, atmospheric distortion and possibly other ordinary sources could each have contributed. The close-encounter elements remain unexplained in the limited sense that they cannot now be reconstructed, not in the stronger sense that conventional explanations were tested and failed.

Within Asturias’s UFO history, Ranadoiro matters precisely because it shows how a regional wave can become culturally powerful without becoming evidentially strong. The event survives through memorable testimony, local geography and a newspaper article that captured a community in a state of fear and fascination. Later reporting has preserved that story, but it has not supplied the missing evidence needed to turn the Ranadoiro lights from an unresolved flap into proof of an extraordinary craft.
