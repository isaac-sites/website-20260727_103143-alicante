## Introduction

The Manises incident entered [Albacete]({{ 'why-albacete-became-central-to-spains/' | relative_url }})’s history shortly after midnight on 12 November 1979, when a Mirage F1 fighter was launched from [Los Llanos]({{ 'los-llanos/' | relative_url }}) Air Base to investigate unidentified lights reported around Valencia. The alarm had begun earlier aboard a passenger aircraft whose crew believed that intense red lights were approaching dangerously close. The airliner diverted to Manises Airport, and reports of further lights prompted the Spanish air-defence command to order the Albacete scramble.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.congreso.es/public_oficiales/L1/CONG/BOCG/E/E_378-I.PDF" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: congreso.es">[Congreso de los Diputados]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">congreso.es</span><span class="citation-popover-title">378-1 PREGUNTA Causas del desvío del avión Super-Cara</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Congreso de los DiputadosBOLETIN OFICIAL DE LAS CORTES GENERALESBOLETIN OFICIAL DE LAS CORTES GENERALES BOLETIN OFICIAL DE LAS CORTES GEN...</span></span></span>

The diversion and the fighter sortie are firmly documented. More dramatic details — an object accelerating away, matching the Mirage’s movements or interfering deliberately with its systems — rest mainly on the pilot’s interpretation of distant lights and cockpit warnings. Later investigators proposed that several ordinary but unrelated stimuli, including industrial flames, bright stars and normal electronic interference, were combined into a single extraordinary narrative. The result is a case with unusually strong evidence that witnesses experienced a genuine aviation alarm, but much weaker evidence that they encountered one coherent, highly capable craft.

## The passenger flight and emergency diversion

The incident began during a Transportes Aéreos Españoles Super Caravelle service carrying 109 passengers from Palma de Mallorca towards the Canary Islands. A parliamentary question submitted the following year identified the service as JK-207 and recorded its departure from Palma at 22:47 local time on 11 November. It named the commander as Javier Lerdo de Tejada, an experienced pilot with more than 8,000 flying hours.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.congreso.es/public_oficiales/L1/CONG/BOCG/E/E_378-I.PDF" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: congreso.es">[Congreso de los Diputados]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">congreso.es</span><span class="citation-popover-title">378-1 PREGUNTA Causas del desvío del avión Super-Cara</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Congreso de los DiputadosBOLETIN OFICIAL DE LAS CORTES GENERALESBOLETIN OFICIAL DE LAS CORTES GENERALES BOLETIN OFICIAL DE LAS CORTES GEN...</span></span></span>

Shortly after 23:00, Barcelona air-traffic control asked the crew to listen to the international emergency frequency because a distress transmission had reportedly been detected north of Valencia. The crew confirmed hearing something but could not understand a clear message. Minutes later, the pilots reported powerful red lights on the left side of the aircraft. According to the parliamentary account, the lights remained in view for roughly eight minutes while the aircraft was still climbing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.congreso.es/public_oficiales/L1/CONG/BOCG/E/E_378-I.PDF" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: congreso.es">[Congreso de los Diputados]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">congreso.es</span><span class="citation-popover-title">378-1 PREGUNTA Causas del desvío del avión Super-Cara</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Congreso de los DiputadosBOLETIN OFICIAL DE LAS CORTES GENERALESBOLETIN OFICIAL DE LAS CORTES GENERALES BOLETIN OFICIAL DE LAS CORTES GEN...</span></span></span>

The pilots believed the lights had approached to an unsafe distance. Popular accounts later described them as following changes in altitude or behaving as if they were deliberately pacing the aircraft, although exact estimates of range and movement are difficult to establish from a night-time visual observation. Without a known size or reliable radar position, a bright distant source can appear to move with an observer, particularly when the observer’s own aircraft is turning or climbing.

Captain Lerdo de Tejada nevertheless faced an immediate safety decision, not a retrospective puzzle. Controllers could not identify the apparent traffic, and the crew considered the lights close enough to create a possible collision risk. The aircraft therefore diverted to Manises Airport near Valencia. That landing is one reason the case became nationally famous: whatever the eventual explanation, an experienced commercial crew altered a passenger flight because of something it could not identify.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.congreso.es/public_oficiales/L1/CONG/BOCG/E/E_378-I.PDF" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: congreso.es">[Congreso de los Diputados]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">congreso.es</span><span class="citation-popover-title">378-1 PREGUNTA Causas del desvío del avión Super-Cara</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Congreso de los DiputadosBOLETIN OFICIAL DE LAS CORTES GENERALESBOLETIN OFICIAL DE LAS CORTES GENERALES BOLETIN OFICIAL DE LAS CORTES GEN...</span></span></span>

The diversion demonstrates that the perception of danger was sincere. It does not, by itself, establish that a physical object was flying alongside the Caravelle. Aviation decisions are deliberately precautionary. A captain does not need proof that another aircraft is present before taking evasive action; uncertainty itself may justify a diversion.

## The order to scramble from Los Llanos

After the Caravelle landed, attention shifted to lights reported from the airport and surrounding airspace. Accounts from Manises described bright objects apparently visible near the field, while military and civilian personnel tried to determine whether they represented unidentified aircraft, distant lights or astronomical sources. The accumulated reports were considered serious enough for the Air Combat Command duty officer to order an interception.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/reports/791111_ejufoas_manises.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[nicap.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Juan Antonio Fernández Peris, from Valenc</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Source details in endnotes.</span></span></span>

At approximately 00:40 on 12 November, Captain Fernando Cámara took off from Los Llanos Air Base in a Mirage F1 of Ala 14. His assignment was to search for and identify the reported lights. This was the point at which an incident centred geographically on the western Mediterranean and Valencia became part of Albacete’s aviation history. The province supplied both the alert aircraft and the pilot who conducted the only military pursuit associated with the case.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/reports/791111_ejufoas_manises.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[nicap.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Juan Antonio Fernández Peris, from Valenc</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Source details in endnotes.</span></span></span>

A scramble is sometimes presented in UFO retellings as evidence that the authorities had confirmed an extraordinary intruder. In practice, it means almost the opposite: a fighter is launched because a potentially important contact remains unidentified. Air-defence units must investigate uncertain reports before their nature is known. The launch shows that the sightings crossed the threshold for operational attention, but not that radar controllers had established the presence of an unconventional vehicle.

Los Llanos was well suited to such a response. The first Mirage F1 fighters had arrived at Ala 14 in 1975, and the aircraft became closely associated with Albacete over nearly four decades of service. The type was designed for rapid interception and air-defence missions, making it the natural platform for investigating uncertain traffic reported within reach of the base. The Manises sortie was unusual because the target information came largely from visual observations rather than a stable, continuously tracked aircraft.

The surviving chronology indicates that Cámara remained airborne for about an hour and a half and returned to Los Llanos at approximately 02:07 with fuel running low. During that period he was directed towards more than one reported light, first in the Valencia and Mediterranean area and later along other bearings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/reports/791111_ejufoas_manises.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[nicap.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Juan Antonio Fernández Peris, from Valenc</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Source details in endnotes.</span></span></span>

This changing sequence matters. The celebrated story is often told as one fighter pursuing one object over a long distance. The more cautious reconstruction is that Cámara investigated several lights reported from different directions, without establishing that they were connected or belonged to the same physical source.

## What the fighter pilot reported seeing

Cámara’s testimony is the strongest evidence supporting an unusual interpretation of the Albacete phase. He has consistently maintained that he observed lights which he could not identify through ordinary flying experience. Another light was described as having a changing colour and a shape resembling a truncated cone or an inverted cup. Those descriptions sound compelling, but they did not come from a close, stable inspection. The fighter’s radar reportedly failed to provide a dependable return corresponding to the most prominent visual lights. The pilot was therefore judging distance and speed largely by appearance. A distant star, planet or ground-based light near the horizon does not grow visibly larger as an aircraft approaches, which can create the impression that it is retreating or keeping pace.

Investigators who later reconstructed the sortie argued that Cámara was repeatedly directed towards very distant points of light. In this interpretation, the inability to reduce the distance was not evidence of exceptional acceleration; it was a clue that the apparent targets were astronomical or terrestrial sources far beyond the area in which the pilot assumed them to be.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/reports/791111_ejufoas_manises.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[nicap.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Juan Antonio Fernández Peris, from Valenc</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Source details in endnotes.</span></span></span>

Cámara has rejected suggestions that he simply mistook familiar industrial flames for the object he saw. His objection deserves weight: an experienced military pilot based in south-eastern Spain would have known common regional landmarks. Yet professional experience does not remove the limits of unaided night observation. Nor does it establish that every light seen during the sortie had the same explanation.

## The cockpit interference dispute

The most technically dramatic part of the story concerns alleged interference with the Mirage. During the mission, Cámara experienced problems with radio communications and indications from the aircraft’s threat-warning equipment. Some retellings describe the fighter’s systems as being deliberately jammed or the aircraft as being targeted by the radar of a guided weapon.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/reports/791111_ejufoas_manises.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[nicap.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Juan Antonio Fernández Peris, from Valenc</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Source details in endnotes.</span></span></span>

The warning equipment was designed to alert the pilot when the aircraft was illuminated by certain radar emissions. A cockpit alert could therefore be operationally serious. It did not, however, identify the source, prove that it came from the light being pursued or demonstrate hostile intent. Military aircraft operate in an electronic environment containing ground radars, shipborne systems and other emitters capable of producing ambiguous indications.

The surviving accounts also distinguish between two problems that are frequently blended together: disrupted voice communications and warnings from the aircraft’s defensive electronics. Radio interference can arise from propagation conditions, overlapping transmissions, equipment limitations or external emitters. A threat-warning indication may likewise be intermittent or unrelated to the object visible outside the cockpit.

No publicly available technical record establishes a continuous, measured link between the visual light, the communications problems and the warning indications. That absence does not mean Cámara imagined the alerts. It means the evidence does not justify the stronger claim that an unidentified craft detected the pursuit and deliberately attacked or disabled the Mirage’s electronics.

This is a recurring weakness in the Manises narrative. Several unusual experiences occurred during the same operation, but temporal overlap is not proof of a common cause. The visual lights, radio interference and cockpit warning may have formed a single event, or they may have been independent features of a complicated night flight.

## Radar claims and the limits of confirmation

The case is often described as a multiple-witness, radar-confirmed encounter. That description requires care. Controllers and military personnel did receive reports and examine possible radar traffic, but the documentation does not provide a simple, continuous track showing one unidentified object moving from the Caravelle to Manises and then away from the Mirage.

Some published accounts refer to several unknown radar echoes, occasionally attaching remarkably precise estimates of size. Radar does not normally measure the physical diameter of a distant target in the straightforward way implied by claims that objects were hundreds of metres wide. Such figures can arise from the displayed width of an echo, assumptions about radar cross-section or later embellishment; they should not be treated as direct measurements of an object’s body.

More importantly, the key lights followed by Cámara were not consistently corroborated by his own radar. Ground controllers could direct him towards reported bearings or uncertain returns, but that is not equivalent to maintaining an unbroken radar lock on the same target. The official file is valuable because it documents an authentic military response, yet it does not supply the decisive sensor record often assumed in popular summaries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es/BVMDefensa/exp_ovni/es/micrositios/inicio.do" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es">[Biblioteca Virtual Defensa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gob.es.</span></span></span>

The evidential picture is therefore uneven:

* The Caravelle’s diversion is well documented.
* Multiple aviation personnel reported unexplained lights.
* A Mirage F1 unquestionably launched from Albacete.
* Cámara reported visual contacts and electronic difficulties.
* There is no public photographic record from the fighter.
* There is no clear, continuous radar track proving that one solid object produced every report.

That combination makes Manises stronger than an isolated anecdote but weaker than a fully instrumented interception.

## Could ordinary lights explain the sortie?

The most developed sceptical reconstruction was produced by investigator Juan Antonio Fernández Peris after years of interviews and study of the declassified material. He concluded that the incident was not one extraordinary encounter but a sequence of misidentifications involving different sources.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/reports/791111_ejufoas_manises.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[nicap.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Juan Antonio Fernández Peris, from Valenc</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Source details in endnotes.</span></span></span>

For the passenger flight, Fernández Peris proposed that the red lights may have been flames from industrial combustion towers in the Escombreras refinery area near Cartagena, distorted or made unusually prominent by atmospheric and viewing conditions. For the later observations around Manises, he identified bright astronomical objects — particularly Sirius and possibly planets or other stars — as plausible sources.

His reconstruction points to a revealing entry from the night itself: by around 01:45, an officer at Manises reportedly told headquarters that many of the lights appeared to follow the movement of the stars and that one suspected object might be a planet. This suggests that at least some personnel were already moving towards an astronomical explanation while Cámara was still airborne.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/reports/791111_ejufoas_manises.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[nicap.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Juan Antonio Fernández Peris, from Valenc</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Source details in endnotes.</span></span></span>

The same basic reasoning was applied to the fighter sortie. A pilot ordered to pursue a light reported by observers on the ground may reasonably assume it is within the operational area. If it is actually a star low on the horizon, however, every attempt to approach will fail. Turns by the aircraft can make the light appear to change direction, while atmospheric scintillation can produce changes in colour and brightness.

This explanation is plausible but not universally accepted. Cámara has insisted that he knew the appearance and position of the refinery flames and that the lights he pursued behaved differently. His testimony prevents the case from being dismissed casually. At the same time, confident recognition by a witness cannot replace independent distance measurements, radar correlation or imagery.

The most defensible conclusion is not that every observation has been proved to be a star or industrial flame. It is that the conventional reconstruction explains several otherwise puzzling features: the lack of closure despite high speed, the absence of stable airborne radar contact, the appearance of multiple lights in different directions and the contemporary suspicion among ground personnel that stars were being misidentified.

## What the official investigation established

The incident generated military paperwork, attracted press coverage and eventually reached the Spanish parliament. In September 1980, deputy Enrique Múgica Herzog formally asked what had caused the Super Caravelle’s diversion, why unidentified traffic had remained in Spanish airspace and what had led to the alert launch of the Mirage from Los Llanos. The parliamentary document preserved key details of the chronology and demonstrated that the case was a matter of public and political concern rather than later folklore.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.congreso.es/public_oficiales/L1/CONG/BOCG/E/E_378-I.PDF" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: congreso.es">[Congreso de los Diputados]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">congreso.es</span><span class="citation-popover-title">378-1 PREGUNTA Causas del desvío del avión Super-Cara</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Congreso de los DiputadosBOLETIN OFICIAL DE LAS CORTES GENERALESBOLETIN OFICIAL DE LAS CORTES GENERALES BOLETIN OFICIAL DE LAS CORTES GEN...</span></span></span>

The declassification process made the Manises documentation accessible to later investigators, although the file is neither a complete recording of every cockpit event nor a definitive scientific analysis.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es/BVMDefensa/exp_ovni/es/micrositios/inicio.do" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es">[Biblioteca Virtual Defensa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gob.es.</span></span></span>

Later access strengthened the case in one sense and weakened it in another. It confirmed that the diversion, reports, communications and scramble were real. It also exposed discrepancies between the paperwork and increasingly dramatic popular versions. The documents did not reveal a hidden radar track, recovered material or an official conclusion that an extraordinary craft had penetrated Spanish airspace.

Nor did they produce one authoritative explanation covering every observation. Instead, the record supports a layered interpretation: a commercial crew perceived a genuine hazard; airport and military personnel then searched an already primed night sky; the Albacete pilot encountered lights he could not identify; and subsequent storytelling linked all of these perceptions into a single pursuing object.

## Albacete’s place in the case

For Albacete, the significance of Manises lies in the operational response rather than the original sighting location. Nothing in the surviving evidence suggests that the reported object reached Los Llanos or that Albacete province experienced a related ground observation that night. The province matters because its air base turned a civilian safety incident into a military interception.

That distinction helps keep the local history accurate. The fighter’s launch demonstrates the readiness and regional reach of Ala 14 at a time when the Mirage F1 was still a relatively new part of Albacete’s military landscape. It also created the case’s most memorable witness: a trained interceptor pilot who remained convinced that at least some of what he saw was not an ordinary star, aircraft or industrial light.

Yet the Albacete phase also illustrates why pilot testimony, though important, is not conclusive on its own. Cámara was flying at night, responding to changing directions from controllers, observing lights without reliable range information and managing radio and warning-system problems. These are precisely the conditions in which perception can be sincere, skilled and still mistaken.

The Manises incident therefore deserves a central place in Albacete’s UFO history, but not because it proves that a fighter chased an alien vehicle. Its lasting value is more grounded: it records the moment when an unresolved civilian report triggered a real scramble from Los Llanos, produced a prolonged and difficult search, and left behind enough documentation to compare official chronology, witness memory and conventional explanation. The case remains disputed, but later research has weakened the idea of one clearly tracked extraordinary craft more than it has weakened the reality of the original aviation alarm.
