## Introduction

[Barcelona]({{ 'how-strong-is-barcelonas-ufo-evidence/' | relative_url }}) became one of the main centres of Spanish UFO research not because it produced an exceptional body of physical evidence, but because local investigators built organisations, publications and archives capable of turning scattered reports into a national field of study. The Centre for Interplanetary Studies, founded in Barcelona in 1958, collected witness questionnaires, correspondence, press cuttings, magazines and case files for decades. Its members also published some of Spain’s earliest specialist UFO literature and maintained links with researchers abroad.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ovniteca.net/organizaciones/centro-de-estudios-interplanetarios-cei" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ovniteca.net">[ovniteca.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ovniteca.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Centro de Estudios Interplanetarios</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Centro de Estudios Interplanetarios - CEI &#124; OvnitecaCentro de Estudios Interplanetarios - CEI &#124; Ovniteca Centro de Estudios Inter...</span></span></span>

Those collections are historically valuable, but they are not neutral stores of established facts. Researchers decided which reports deserved attention, how witnesses were questioned and which explanations were considered plausible. Early interpretations often assumed that unexplained objects might be extraterrestrial machines. Later investigators placed greater weight on misidentification, psychology, media influence and deliberate fraud. Barcelona’s UFO archive therefore preserves two histories at once: what people reported seeing, and how successive generations learned to interpret those reports differently.

## The Centre for Interplanetary Studies after 1958

The Centre for Interplanetary Studies emerged from the excitement surrounding the early space age. The organisation traces its origins to public interest generated in Barcelona during 1957 by the International Astronautical Congress and the Soviet launch of Sputnik 1. In October 1958, figures including Eduardo Buelta, Antonio Ribera, Màrius Lleget and Antoni Pelegrí established a permanent association devoted to unidentified flying objects. It is generally regarded as the oldest enduring organisation of its kind in Spain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ovniteca.net/organizaciones/centro-de-estudios-interplanetarios-cei" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ovniteca.net">[ovniteca.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ovniteca.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Centro de Estudios Interplanetarios</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Centro de Estudios Interplanetarios - CEI &#124; OvnitecaCentro de Estudios Interplanetarios - CEI &#124; Ovniteca Centro de Estudios Inter...</span></span></span>

Its first phase already showed how strongly interpretation shaped the emerging archive. Between 1959 and 1961, the centre issued nine numbers of an early bulletin. One prominent line of enquiry was Buelta’s proposed relationship between waves of UFO reports and the oppositions of Mars, the periods when Mars and Earth lie relatively close. This was an attempt to impose a statistical pattern on sighting reports, but it also reflected an era when extraterrestrial visitation, especially from nearby planets, remained a widely entertained hypothesis among UFO writers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ovniteca.net/organizaciones/centro-de-estudios-interplanetarios-cei" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ovniteca.net">[Ovniteca]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ovniteca.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Centro de Estudios Interplanetarios</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Centro de Estudios Interplanetarios - CEI &#124; OvnitecaCentro de Estudios Interplanetarios - CEI &#124; Ovniteca Centro de Estudios Inter...</span></span></span>

Activity later diminished, and the organisation was effectively rebuilt at the end of the 1960s. The revived group approved new rules, established premises in Barcelona and began reconstructing its research material because it had inherited little of the original centre’s physical archive. Members created a library, assembled press and periodical collections, issued questionnaires and collected reports from across Spain rather than limiting themselves to Catalonia.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ovniteca.net/organizaciones/centro-de-estudios-interplanetarios-cei" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ovniteca.net">[Ovniteca]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ovniteca.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Centro de Estudios Interplanetarios</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Centro de Estudios Interplanetarios - CEI &#124; OvnitecaCentro de Estudios Interplanetarios - CEI &#124; Ovniteca Centro de Estudios Inter...</span></span></span>

From 1970 to 1981, its principal public voice was *Stendek*. Although based in Barcelona, the magazine functioned as a Spanish and international exchange network. It published case investigations, landing catalogues, statistical discussions, translations and contributions from researchers outside Spain. Notices invited readers to report sightings to the Barcelona office, after which questionnaires could be sent to gather details about the witness, location, duration, appearance and movement of the object.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufomagazines.com/stendek-cei-eissn-2604-1383-stendek-vol-02-no-05a-extra-july-1971/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufomagazines.com">[UFO Magazine Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufomagazines.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">stendek cei eissn 2604 1383 stendek vol 02 no 05a extra july 1971</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">This special issue of STENDEK, the informative service of the Centro de Estudios Interplanetarios (CEI), is dedicated to a comprehensive...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: july 1971</span></span></span>

This process mattered because many reports that entered later books and catalogues did not begin as official records. They began as letters, newspaper stories or answers to forms circulated by private investigators. The centre helped preserve material that might otherwise have vanished, but its files could only be as reliable as the original testimony and the methods used to obtain it.

## Antonio Ribera gave Barcelona national influence

Antonio Ribera was central to Barcelona’s importance in Spanish UFO culture. A writer, translator and diving pioneer as well as an investigator, he co-founded the centre and became its honorary president in 1968. His command of several languages allowed him to translate foreign reports and ideas for Spanish readers while introducing Spanish cases into international UFO networks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ia800406.us.archive.org/28/items/Papers_dOvnis_2a_epoca_No_25/Papers_dOvnis_2a_epoca_No_25.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ia800406.us.archive.org">[ia800406.us.archive.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ia800406.us.archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Papers d&#x27;Ovnis, 2a època, núm Interplanetarios (CEI</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Conserva su nombre en lengua catalana en memoria de su creador Joan Crexell i Playà El CEI no comparte necesariamente las opiniones expre...</span></span></span>

His books helped establish the vocabulary through which a generation understood unidentified objects. His early works presented UFOs as a serious unresolved problem and generally treated the extraterrestrial hypothesis as worthy of sustained investigation. He also directed *Horizonte*, a Barcelona-published magazine inspired by the French cultural periodical *Planète*. Although it covered subjects beyond UFOs, its mixture of science, speculation and unexplained phenomena helped bring previously specialist claims into a broader reading market.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ia800406.us.archive.org/28/items/Papers_dOvnis_2a_epoca_No_25/Papers_dOvnis_2a_epoca_No_25.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ia800406.us.archive.org">[ia800406.us.archive.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ia800406.us.archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Papers d&#x27;Ovnis, 2a època, núm Interplanetarios (CEI</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Conserva su nombre en lengua catalana en memoria de su creador Joan Crexell i Playà El CEI no comparte necesariamente las opiniones expre...</span></span></span>

Ribera’s influence extended through conferences and personal correspondence. He helped organise the first National Congress of Ufology in Barcelona in 1977 and maintained exchanges with investigators in Europe and the Americas. The surviving correspondence is important because it shows that Spanish ufology was not simply a collection of isolated local enthusiasts. It was a publishing and social network in which reports, photographs, theories and judgements of witness credibility circulated between countries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ia800406.us.archive.org/28/items/Papers_dOvnis_2a_epoca_No_25/Papers_dOvnis_2a_epoca_No_25.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ia800406.us.archive.org">[ia800406.us.archive.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ia800406.us.archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Papers d&#x27;Ovnis, 2a època, núm Interplanetarios (CEI</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Conserva su nombre en lengua catalana en memoria de su creador Joan Crexell i Playà El CEI no comparte necesariamente las opiniones expre...</span></span></span>

However, Ribera also illustrates the risks created when a respected researcher becomes closely attached to a favoured interpretation. He devoted several books to the Ummo affair, a supposed extraterrestrial contact story built around elaborate letters and documents. The case attracted intellectuals and UFO investigators for decades, but José Luis Jordán Peña later admitted responsibility for initiating the deception. Although not every document attributed to Ummo has an uncontested chain of authorship, the confession and the case’s accumulated inconsistencies substantially weakened its status as evidence of alien contact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://es.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ummo" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

For Barcelona’s UFO history, the lesson is not merely that one famous claim proved unreliable. It is that research authority could amplify a case. Books, conferences, correspondence and magazine coverage gave disputed material a durable afterlife, even after the original evidential basis had deteriorated.

## The archive grew into more than a case collection

Over time, the centre assembled a substantial working archive. Descriptions of its holdings refer to more than 600 books, several thousand magazines and bulletins, unpublished research papers, correspondence, questionnaires and case documentation. Its material supported an Iberian catalogue intended to organise reports from Spain and Portugal into a searchable body of information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ovniteca.net/organizaciones/centro-de-estudios-interplanetarios-cei" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ovniteca.net">[Ovniteca]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ovniteca.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Centro de Estudios Interplanetarios</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Centro de Estudios Interplanetarios - CEI &#124; OvnitecaCentro de Estudios Interplanetarios - CEI &#124; Ovniteca Centro de Estudios Inter...</span></span></span>

The most useful holdings are often the least spectacular. A newspaper cutting can establish when a story first appeared and whether later retellings added details. A completed questionnaire may preserve a witness’s wording before it was rewritten by journalists. Correspondence between investigators can reveal doubts omitted from published accounts. Successive magazine articles can show how an incident changed from an uncertain light in the sky into a supposedly structured craft.

The archive also preserves the social infrastructure of UFO research:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* **Witness forms and case reports** record what investigators asked and what information witnesses supplied.
* **Press collections** show how local and national media selected, simplified or sensationalised sightings.
* **Private correspondence** reveals disagreements, tentative identifications and abandoned lines of enquiry.
* **Specialist magazines** document changing theories and the international circulation of cases.
* **Catalogues** make broad comparison possible, but can also flatten reports of very different quality into apparently equivalent entries.

</div>

Digitisation has made part of this legacy easier to inspect. Complete or near-complete runs of *Stendek* and later issues of *Papers d’Ovnis* are now preserved through online specialist archives, including the Swedish Archives for the Unexplained and collections devoted to rare periodicals. The centre itself has also placed historical material and commentary online.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.afu.se/centro-de-estudios-interplanetarios-cei-collections/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: afu.se">[afu.se]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">afu.se</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Centro de Estudios Interplanetarios (CEI) archives</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Centro de Estudios Interplanetarios (CEI) archives</span></span></span>

Digital access changes the balance between dramatic retelling and documentary checking. A reader no longer has to rely entirely on a later author’s summary of what an old magazine claimed. Original issues can be compared, names and dates checked, and omissions identified. Yet digitisation remains incomplete, and a scanned magazine is not the same thing as a fully documented case file.

## From spacecraft to witnesses and cultural context

Barcelona’s organised ufology did not remain intellectually fixed. *Stendek* distinguished itself from more openly occult or sensational publications by promoting field investigation, statistical comparison and attention to witness testimony. Its contributors were generally wary of the “contactee” tradition in which individuals claimed friendly meetings or repeated communication with extraterrestrials. The magazine nevertheless operated within a field where the possibility of visiting machines was often treated as a serious working hypothesis.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://iapsop.com/archive/materials/stendek/index.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: iapsop.com">[IAPSOP]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">iapsop.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Stendek [IAPSOP]Stendek [IAPSOP] Periodical: Stendek</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Servicio Informativo C.E.I.. 1970--1981 Quarterly Barcelona, Spain. Language: Spanish. Editor: Joan Crexells; Ped...</span></span></span>

As the decades passed, researchers increasingly examined the human setting of a sighting. The questions shifted from simply “What craft was present?” towards “How was the observation made, remembered and reported?” Psychological expectation, media imagery, astronomical confusion and the influence of earlier UFO stories became more prominent explanations. Later issues of *Papers d’Ovnis* openly published sceptical arguments and criticism of interpretations that converted ordinary aircraft, meteors or satellite re-entries into examples of deliberate alien camouflage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ia801509.us.archive.org/9/items/Papers_dOvnis_2a_epoca_No_34/Papers_dOvnis_2a_epoca_No_34.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ia801509.us.archive.org">[ia801509.us.archive.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ia801509.us.archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Papers d Ovnis 2a epoca No 34</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Papers d Ovnis 2a epoca No 34</span></span></span>

This change formed part of a broader reassessment of Spanish UFO history. Anthropologist Ignacio Cabria, who studied at the University of Barcelona and had participated in UFO investigation before adopting a more critical position, has described Spanish ufology as a cultural movement shaped by science fiction, mass media, technological optimism, religion and changing ideas about extraterrestrial life. In this interpretation, a UFO report is still a real historical event as testimony or belief, even when no extraordinary object can be established.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ovniteca.net/bibliografia/historia-cultural-de-los-ovnis-en-espana" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ovniteca.net">[ovniteca.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ovniteca.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">historia cultural de los ovnis en espana</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">historia cultural de los ovnis en espana</span></span></span>

The shift does not mean that every old case has been solved. It means that “unidentified” is no longer automatically treated as a physical category pointing towards an exotic vehicle. It may instead describe an incomplete investigation, a missing record, ambiguous testimony or an observation that cannot be reconstructed decades later.

## What private archives preserve — and where they fall short

Barcelona’s UFO collections are strongest when used to reconstruct how a claim developed. They can show that a report existed by a particular date, identify the investigator who handled it and preserve descriptions unavailable elsewhere. They are particularly valuable for understanding cases that never reached police, aviation or military authorities.

Their weaknesses are equally important.

**Selection was uneven.** Investigators were more likely to collect striking stories than routine identifications. An archive can therefore create the impression of a mysterious pattern simply because explained reports were discarded or never submitted.

**Witness accounts were often retrospective.** Memory becomes less reliable when interviews occur long after an event, especially after newspaper reports or discussions with other witnesses.

**Questionnaires could guide interpretation.** Forms designed around assumptions about craft shape, beams, occupants or physical effects might encourage witnesses to fit ambiguous experiences into established UFO categories.

**Copies may be mistaken for independent sources.** A newspaper article, magazine summary and catalogue entry may all descend from one original claim. Counting them separately exaggerates corroboration.

**Physical evidence is uncommon.** Most files contain testimony and correspondence rather than calibrated photographs, original negatives, radar data, instrument readings or materials with a documented chain of custody.

The 1978 report involving Barcelona, L’Hospitalet and Sabadell shows why these distinctions matter. A private UFO group’s enquiry helped ensure that the allegation entered an official Air Force file. But the surviving correspondence shows that the reported radar detection at El Prat airport was not confirmed by the airport control centre. The archive preserves an important episode in local UFO history, while simultaneously weakening the most dramatic version of it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://diaricatalunya.cat/en/barcelones/general/lhospitalet-has-its-own-declassified-ufo-file-in-spain" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: diaricatalunya.cat">[Diari de Catalunya]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">diaricatalunya.cat</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on diaricatalunya.cat.</span></span></span>

That is the central value of Barcelona’s UFO archive. It does not provide a warehouse of proven extraordinary encounters. It provides the material needed to separate an original observation from later additions, to distinguish official confirmation from an investigator’s request for information, and to see how confidence rose or fell as new documents appeared.

## A legacy of preservation and revision

Barcelona’s researchers helped make Spanish UFO history researchable. Without the Centre for Interplanetary Studies, *Stendek*, Ribera’s international correspondence and the later preservation of private collections, many reports and debates would survive only as scattered newspaper references. The city’s publishing networks also connected local witnesses with a wider Spanish and international audience.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ovniteca.net/organizaciones/centro-de-estudios-interplanetarios-cei" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ovniteca.net">[ovniteca.net]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ovniteca.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Centro de Estudios Interplanetarios</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Centro de Estudios Interplanetarios - CEI &#124; OvnitecaCentro de Estudios Interplanetarios - CEI &#124; Ovniteca Centro de Estudios Inter...</span></span></span>

But preservation should not be confused with validation. The same archive records speculative planetary theories, serious attempts at witness interviewing, premature claims, exposed frauds and increasingly sceptical reassessments. Its historical importance lies precisely in that mixture.

The most defensible interpretation is therefore comparative rather than celebratory or dismissive. Barcelona became a national centre of ufology because it created durable institutions and documentary habits. Those institutions sometimes strengthened reports by collecting neglected testimony. At other times, later documents weakened famous claims by exposing absent radar records, recycled sources, ordinary explanations or the influence of hoaxers. The archive’s lasting contribution is not proof that extraordinary craft visited the province, but a detailed record of how Spaniards investigated, promoted, doubted and repeatedly reinterpreted the UFO idea.
