## Introduction

Weather or radar error could plausibly explain at least part of [Ceuta]({{ 'ceutas-ufo-mystery-beyond-the-radar/' | relative_url }})’s best-known UFO report, but the surviving evidence is too incomplete to identify one cause with confidence. The 2 December 1954 incident is usually described as a military radar contact that climbed, slowed almost to a halt, changed altitude abruptly and remained under observation for nearly an hour. Read literally, those movements look extraordinary. Yet the original radar plots, equipment specifications, calibration records, weather profile and complete witness statements are not publicly available. What survives is mainly a later reconstruction of numerical readings and brief summaries attributed to French military records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/reports/541202ceuta_report.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[nicap.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO ReportUFO Report 10. That gap matters because radar does not display an object directly. It displays returned radio energy, interpreted through the design of the instrument, the operator’s settings and assumptions about how the beam travelled through the atmosphere. Around the Strait of Gibraltar, strong contrasts between sea air, land heating, humidity and layered winds make non-standard propagation a serious possibility. Aircraft, balloons, distant surface reflections and instrument effects also remain credible alternatives. None is proved, but together they weaken any claim that the reported radar performance must represent a physically extraordinary craft.

## How anomalous propagation works

Radar normally estimates distance by timing how long a transmitted pulse takes to return. Direction is inferred from where the antenna is pointing, while altitude may be calculated using a separate height-finding radar or an elevation measurement. These calculations assume that radio waves follow a broadly predictable path through the lower atmosphere. That assumption is usually adequate, but it is not always true.

Changes in temperature and moisture can alter the air’s refractive index, causing a radar beam to bend more or less than expected. The United States National Weather Service defines anomalous propagation as a non-standard radar path caused by unusual temperature or moisture gradients. When the beam bends sharply downwards, it may strike the ground or sea at a distance and return echoes from places the radar would not normally illuminate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://forecast.weather.gov/glossary.php?word=AP" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: forecast.weather.gov">[National Weather Service]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">forecast.weather.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Weather Service NOAA&#x27;s National Weather Service</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">For parcels that remain unsaturated during lifting, the (dry adiabatic) lapse rate is 9.8°C per kilometer. ### Why the Strait favours unusual propagation

Ceuta sits beside a narrow maritime passage where Atlantic and Mediterranean air and water masses interact. The Strait is only about 14 kilometres wide at its narrowest point and contains complex currents, terrain and coastal boundaries. These features do not prove that anomalous propagation occurred on 2 December 1954, but they create exactly the kind of environment in which sharp humidity and temperature layers can form close to the sea.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.codar.com/images/about/2016Soto-Navarro_JGR.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: codar.com">[codar.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">codar.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on codar.com.</span></span></span>

Maritime radar research shows that so-called atmospheric ducts can trap radio energy within a shallow layer and carry it far beyond the normal radar horizon. The United States Navy describes such ducts as channels produced by particular combinations of temperature and water vapour; targets or surface features may then be detected at unexpectedly great distances. Coastal air–sea interaction can change the effect from one frequency or height to another.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.navy.mil/Press-Office/News-Stories/Article/3262836/nps-research-in-electromagnetic-waves-hunts-for-ships-ghost-signals/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: navy.mil">[U.S. Navy]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">navy.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on navy.mil.</span></span></span>

For Ceuta, a ducted beam might have illuminated ships, coastline, hills or aircraft beyond the radar’s expected coverage. As the refractive layer changed, the return could have strengthened, weakened, shifted or disappeared. If an operator treated every plotted position as the movement of one object, changing propagation could be converted into an apparently erratic track.

There is, however, no surviving atmospheric sounding for the radar site in the accessible case material. The published account does not supply temperature, humidity, inversion height, radar frequency or antenna elevation. Anomalous propagation is therefore a physically credible mechanism, not a demonstrated solution.

### Can refraction create false altitude changes?

The most dramatic feature of the case is the reported variation from roughly 23,000 to 59,000 feet, including an apparent fall of about 20,000 feet in one minute. At first sight, a reflection from land or sea seems unable to produce a high-altitude reading. The difficulty is that a displayed height is not necessarily the true height of whatever reflected the pulse.

A radar calculates altitude from range and elevation under assumptions about the beam path. If the beam has bent, or if the operator has locked on to the wrong part of a broad or intermittent echo, the resulting height can be wrong. A height finder’s horizontal beam may also cover a comparatively wide slice of sky, making it hard to determine whether successive returns come from precisely the same bearing. The technical reconstruction of the Ceuta report notes that a height-only display could understate sideways motion and make a moving aircraft appear temporarily stationary while it crossed the beam.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/reports/541202ceuta_report.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[Nicap]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO ReportUFO Report 10. This offers one possible explanation for the strange pairing of speed and altitude in the published graph. A real target moving mainly across the radar’s line of sight may show little change in range. If the antenna then has to be redirected to reacquire it, the reconstructed sequence can contain abrupt jumps that look like acceleration, hovering or sudden descent. Without the original plan-position track showing range and bearing, it is impossible to know whether the quoted speeds describe the target’s complete motion or only its movement towards and away from the station.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/reports/541202ceuta_report.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[Nicap]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO ReportUFO Report 10. Atmospheric refraction alone does not neatly reproduce every reported number. Its importance is more basic: it makes those numbers less secure than they appear.

## Aircraft, balloons and mixed targets

A conventional explanation does not have to reproduce the published track as the path of one physical object. The record may combine a genuine aircraft or balloon with propagation effects, imperfect tracking or more than one return. That mixed explanation is less dramatic, but it is often more realistic than asking one balloon, one aeroplane or one radar fault to account for every detail.

### An aircraft seen through an imperfect measurement

The reported maximum speed of about 137 mph is not exceptional for an aircraft, and much of the contact’s duration could fit a military or civilian aeroplane moving around the region. The problem is the apparent near-hovering at extreme altitude and the abrupt height changes. Those features rule out an ordinary aircraft only if the derived speed and altitude are accurate.

The detailed case analysis suggests that a fixed-wing aircraft climbing or travelling largely across the radar beam could appear slow on a range-based plot. A separate height finder should normally reduce that ambiguity, but the surviving account does not establish how the search radar and height-finding equipment were coordinated, whether both were continuously locked on, or whether the published diagram was reconstructed from intermittent readings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/reports/541202ceuta_report.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[Nicap]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO ReportUFO Report 10. The Strait was already an important aviation and military corridor. A normal aircraft detected under distorted propagation conditions is therefore a serious candidate. It becomes more plausible if the “visual confirmation” was no more than a crew reporting an indistinct object in approximately the relevant part of the sky. The accessible record does not provide the aircrew’s original words, viewing distance, shape, lighting, duration or certainty.

### A high-altitude balloon

A balloon naturally accounts for slow movement, long duration and operation at altitudes beyond those routinely reached by most 1954 aircraft. High-altitude balloons can drift for hours in stratospheric winds and may carry a payload that produces a stronger radar return than the envelope itself. Modern defence experience confirms that slow, cold balloons can be difficult to classify because radar networks and filters are often tuned primarily for faster aircraft or missiles.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://time.com/6254681/chinese-balloons-us-air-defense-network-failure/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: time.com">[Time]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">time.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Why America&#x27;s Air Defense Network Failed to Detect the Chinese Spy Balloons</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Le ballon repéré le 1er février au-dessus du Montana faisait 60 mètres de hauteur et transportait un équipement de surveillance varié. Balloons were also used extensively for meteorology, research and military purposes during the early Cold War. Their movement need not follow winds measured near the ground, because wind speed and direction can differ greatly between atmospheric layers.

A balloon does not, by itself, explain a true 20,000-foot descent in one minute followed by stable flight. Such a drop would imply a major failure or a mistaken altitude reading. Nor does an uncontrolled balloon readily explain a sequence of acceleration, levelling and deceleration if all values are treated as precise. The balloon hypothesis works best as part of a combined explanation: a slowly drifting target whose apparent height and speed were distorted by tracking geometry, changing echoes or incorrect association of separate returns.

### More than one target mistaken for one

The accessible French-record transcription appears to describe at least two radar episodes on 2 December, one lasting 58 minutes and another shorter contact associated with a different site and an unsuccessful F-86 search. It gives different directions, times and reported altitude ranges, including a later track said to vary between 4,000 and 70,000 feet.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufologie.patrickgross.org/foiafr/2dec1954ba151.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[Ufologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOs at close sight: my own small French FOIA, French Air Force - December 2, 1954, radar echo...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 2, 1954</span></span></span>

Later UFO summaries do not always separate these sequences clearly. Some versions emphasise an hour-long afternoon contact near Ceuta; others add a later interception episode or merge details drawn from multiple radar stations. This raises the possibility that later narratives compressed several detections into a single continuous mystery.

Even within one hour, operators could have transferred attention from one weak echo to another. An aircraft, balloon, surface reflection and clutter patch need not appear simultaneously to become linked in a retrospective account. Once the assumption of a single target is removed, the extreme “performance” may be an artefact of joining unrelated measurements.

## Equipment effects that can imitate movement

Radar faults do not necessarily produce a meaningless screen full of noise. They can create orderly-looking returns with apparently measurable ranges and speeds. This is why the absence of the original scope photographs and technical log is so damaging to the Ceuta case.

**Multiple-time-around echoes.** A radar sends pulses at regular intervals and normally assigns a return to the most recently transmitted pulse. A sufficiently distant echo may arrive after the next pulse has been sent, causing the system to place the target at a false range. The Ceuta technical assessment identifies this as one possible route to misleading distance information, although the pulse repetition frequency and unambiguous range of the unknown radar set are not available.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/reports/541202ceuta_report.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[Nicap]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO ReportUFO Report 10. DATE: December 2, 1954 TIME: 1410 local CLASS: R/V ground radar/air visual LOCATION: SOURCES: Vallee CS 196...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 2, 1954</span></span></span>

**Multiple reflections.** Radar energy can bounce between a strong target and another reflecting surface before returning to the antenna. Marine radar manuals warn that large ships, bridges and other solid objects may generate secondary echoes at multiples of the true range. Ceuta’s combination of water, ships, steep coast and built structures would provide numerous reflecting surfaces, although no specific reflector can be identified for the 1954 event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://manualsdump.com/en/manuals/furuno-furuno_marine_radar-1953c-bb-1943c-bb-1933c-bb-1823c-bb-1833c-bb/239203/78" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: manualsdump.com">[Manuals Dump]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">manualsdump.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on manualsdump.com.</span></span></span>

**Side-lobe returns.** A radar antenna does not transmit all its energy in one perfectly narrow beam. Smaller side lobes can detect strong objects away from the antenna’s apparent pointing direction, creating misplaced or duplicated echoes. Operator adjustments can suppress some of these effects, but the surviving report says nothing about gain, clutter controls or whether the return persisted under changed settings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://manualsdump.com/en/manuals/furuno-furuno_marine_radar-1953c-bb-1943c-bb-1933c-bb-1823c-bb-1833c-bb/239203/78" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: manualsdump.com">[Manuals Dump]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">manualsdump.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on manualsdump.com.</span></span></span>

**Track loss and reacquisition.** A weak return may disappear for several sweeps and then reappear nearby. If it is assumed to be the same target, calculations can show an abrupt climb, descent or speed change. Modern tracking systems also generate temporary positional errors when targets manoeuvre, signals are garbled or tracks are closely spaced; a 1950s operator manually plotting intermittent returns faced an even harder task.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications//atpubs/aim_html/chap4_section_5.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faa.gov">[Federal Aviation Administration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Federal Aviation Administration Chapter 4</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Air Traffic Controlwhich are not under ATC control and cannot be seen on radar. **Slow-target filtering.** Moving-target indication is designed to remove ground clutter and other nearly stationary echoes. It can also suppress genuine slow objects, while changes to the filter or gain may cause them suddenly to appear. The FAA notes that moving-target filtering is one of the standard ways to reduce anomalous-propagation clutter. The report does not state whether the Ceuta radar had such processing, how it was configured or whether its settings changed during the observation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.faa.gov/air_traffic/publications/atpubs/aim/aim0405.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faa.gov">[Federal Aviation Administration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">See FIG 4-5-1.) ## Why the failed interception proves little

Some retellings say two F-86 fighters were sent towards a later radar contact but found nothing. The surviving record transcription describes an unsuccessful mission and says the blip disappeared near Ouezzane.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufologie.patrickgross.org/foiafr/2dec1954ba151.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[Ufologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOs at close sight: my own small French FOIA, French Air Force - December 2, 1954, radar echo...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 2, 1954</span></span></span>

A failed interception can be interpreted in opposite ways. UFO writers may regard it as evidence that the target evaded military aircraft. A conventional reading is that the fighters were sent to a stale, inaccurate or false radar position. If a return came from ducting, range ambiguity or a balloon moving with high-level winds, the interception aircraft might never reach visual range.

The failure becomes especially important because it deprives the case of independent confirmation. No detailed pilot account, photograph, airborne radar record or identified physical object is available. The fighters’ inability to see the target does not prove a radar error, but it does not strengthen the claim of an extraordinary craft either.

The frequently repeated statement that another airborne crew saw the object at the same time would be more valuable if its original report survived. The available summary does not explain whether the crew saw a structured object, a point of light, a cloud-like feature or simply traffic in the indicated area. Radar–visual cases are strongest when the visual and instrument observations can be matched precisely in time, bearing and behaviour. That standard cannot presently be applied to Ceuta.

## What each explanation still cannot prove

No single conventional proposal fully reconstructs the reported sequence from the evidence now available.


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* **Anomalous propagation** explains how distant terrain, sea clutter, ships or aircraft might appear in unexpected places, but no local weather profile has been produced to demonstrate a duct or inversion during the event.
* **A normal aircraft** fits some speeds and the region’s aviation context, but not the quoted hovering and extreme height changes unless those readings were wrong or incomplete.
* **A balloon** fits the long duration, low speed and high altitude, but not the most abrupt reported manoeuvres if they are accepted literally.
* **Multiple targets** can account for contradictory movement and altitude, but the surviving track data are too sparse to show where one return might have been substituted for another.
* **Equipment or operator error** is always possible, yet the radar model, maintenance condition, control settings and operators’ full reports are missing.

</div>

The same limitation applies to an exotic interpretation. Calling the object unidentified does not establish that it possessed the flight performance shown in later diagrams. Those diagrams depend on measurements whose reliability cannot now be tested.

## The most defensible reading

The Ceuta radar case remains unresolved in the narrow historical sense: the available material does not identify the source of every reported return. It is not, however, strong evidence that a craft physically hovered near 59,000 feet and then plunged thousands of feet in seconds.

The Strait’s coastal atmosphere provides a credible setting for anomalous propagation. Ordinary aircraft or balloons could have supplied real reflecting targets. Height-finding geometry, intermittent contact, false range, multiple echoes or the joining of separate radar episodes could then have transformed ordinary movement into an extraordinary-looking profile. Official aviation and meteorological guidance confirms that these are recognised radar limitations rather than explanations invented solely for UFO reports.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://forecast.weather.gov/glossary.php?word=AP" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: forecast.weather.gov">[weather.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">forecast.weather.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Weather Service NOAA&#x27;s National Weather Service</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">For parcels that remain unsaturated during lifting, the (dry adiabatic) lapse rate is 9.8°C per kilometer. Later reporting has therefore weakened the strongest version of the story more than it has strengthened it. The discovery or reproduction of brief military forms supports the claim that radar personnel recorded unusual contacts, but it also exposes multiple times, sites, directions and altitude ranges that are not always kept distinct in later summaries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufologie.patrickgross.org/foiafr/2dec1954ba151.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[Ufologie]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOs at close sight: my own small French FOIA, French Air Force - December 2, 1954, radar echo...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 2, 1954</span></span></span>

A firm conclusion would require the original radar plots, the exact equipment type, pulse settings, maintenance and calibration logs, meteorological soundings, operator interviews and complete aircrew reports. Without them, “radar error” cannot be proved—but neither can the remarkable motion commonly attributed to the target. The case is best classified as an incompletely documented radar anomaly with several credible conventional explanations.
