## Introduction

The Cando event began as an apparent impact. On the morning of 18 January 1994, people across part of Galicia reported a brilliant object crossing the sky, while residents near Cando, in the municipality of Outes in A Coruña province, heard a powerful explosion. A damaged patch of hillside was later found close to the projected end of the luminous object’s path. Trees had been uprooted or thrown downhill, and the hollow in the ground looked enough like a crater to encourage talk of a meteorite — and eventually a crashed UFO.

The investigation did not recover a meteorite, spacecraft material or any other foreign debris. Laboratory work found local, unshocked soil and rock rather than the physical traces expected from a high-energy impact. The researchers therefore judged an impact to be very improbable and proposed an unusual natural alternative: an explosive release of underground gas, possibly accompanied by an electrical luminous phenomenon. That explanation remains a hypothesis rather than a conclusively demonstrated mechanism, but it fits the surviving evidence far better than the later UFO-crash legend.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1945-5100.1998.tb01607.x" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">j.1945 5100.1998.tb01607.x</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Soil Science Department, University of Santiago de Compostela, Spain... ## The fireball, explosion and damaged hillside

Shortly before dawn, witnesses around Santiago de Compostela saw an exceptionally bright object moving across the sky. Descriptions differed, as unaided eyewitness accounts of brief luminous events commonly do, but investigators were able to reconstruct an apparent path towards the western part of the province. Reports of a loud explosion and disturbed ground near Cando seemed to provide the missing endpoint: something bright had crossed the sky, something had exploded, and a crater-like feature had appeared in roughly the expected area.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1945-5100.1998.tb01607.x" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">j.1945 5100.1998.tb01607.x</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Soil Science Department, University of Santiago de Compostela, Spain... DOCOBO, J.A., SPALDING, R.E., CEPLECHA, Z., DIAZ-FIERROS, F., TA...</span></span></span>

The site was not a neat, circular impact crater. The scientific paper described an irregular surface hollow approximately 29 metres by 13 metres and about 1.5 metres deep. Mature pine trees more than 20 metres tall had been carried downhill and across a nearby road. Soil and plant fragments were lodged several metres above ground in trees below the disturbed area, showing that earth and vegetation had been projected outward with considerable force. The downhill edge, however, remained relatively intact, with a steep inner wall — a pattern the investigators regarded as inconsistent with an ordinary, slow landslip.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1945-5100.1998.tb01607.x" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">j.1945 5100.1998.tb01607.x</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Soil Science Department, University of Santiago de Compostela, Spain... DOCOBO, J.A., SPALDING, R.E., CEPLECHA, Z., DIAZ-FIERROS, F., TA...</span></span></span>

These details explain why the impact interpretation initially seemed reasonable. The damage was violent, localised and difficult to attribute to routine erosion. The hollow also lay within about a kilometre of the projected endpoint derived from the luminous-object reports. Local and later retrospective press accounts consequently used the language of an object “falling” or creating a crater, although published dimensions have sometimes been distorted in retelling. The peer-reviewed investigation’s figure of roughly 1.5 metres deep is more reliable than reports describing a fantastically deep hole.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1945-5100.1998.tb01607.x" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">j.1945 5100.1998.tb01607.x</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Soil Science Department, University of Santiago de Compostela, Spain... DOCOBO, J.A., SPALDING, R.E., CEPLECHA, Z., DIAZ-FIERROS, F., TA...</span></span></span>

The timing also encouraged witnesses and reporters to treat the light, sound and ground disturbance as parts of one event. Yet proximity in time and place does not by itself establish a physical impact. The investigators still had to determine whether the object behaved like a meteoroid, whether the hillside contained impact products and whether the damage pattern could have been produced by something emerging from the ground rather than striking it from above.

## What investigators found — and failed to find

Researchers associated with the Ramón María Aller Astronomical Observatory at the University of Santiago de Compostela began examining the reports soon after the incident. The eventual study brought together astronomy, soil science and specialist knowledge of bright meteors. Its most important conclusion was negative: despite searching the disturbed area, the team found no meteorite fragments, artificial components or anomalous material that could be identified as the remains of a falling object.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1945-5100.1998.tb01607.x" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">j.1945 5100.1998.tb01607.x</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Soil Science Department, University of Santiago de Compostela, Spain... DOCOBO, J.A., SPALDING, R.E., CEPLECHA, Z., DIAZ-FIERROS, F., TA...</span></span></span>

The soil and rock at the site belonged to the hillside itself. They showed no evidence of shock alteration — the distinctive physical changes that intense pressures can produce in minerals during a substantial hypervelocity impact. The researchers also found no exotic residue capable of turning the supposed crater into evidence for an aircraft, missile or unknown craft. In their published judgement, the absence of foreign material and shock effects made an impact “very improbable”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1945-5100.1998.tb01607.x" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">j.1945 5100.1998.tb01607.x</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Soil Science Department, University of Santiago de Compostela, Spain... DOCOBO, J.A., SPALDING, R.E., CEPLECHA, Z., DIAZ-FIERROS, F., TA...</span></span></span>

That finding is particularly damaging to the UFO-crash version. A claim that a solid craft struck the hillside hard enough to excavate the ground and throw full-grown trees would normally lead to expectations of fragments, melted or deformed material, fuel residues, manufactured components or at least an impact pattern centred on an incoming body. None was reported in the scientific study. Suggesting that every trace was secretly removed requires an additional conspiracy for which the publicly available evidence provides no support.

The luminous object also presented problems for a conventional meteor explanation. The investigators concluded from the visual evidence that it was not a normal meteoric fireball. Its reported duration and apparent behaviour did not fit comfortably with the brief, rapid paths normally associated with bright meteors. Modern instrumented fireball studies rely on timed camera records, measured velocities, triangulated trajectories and recovered material; Cando depended mainly on varied eyewitness descriptions and a later-found patch of disturbed ground.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1945-5100.1998.tb01607.x" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">j.1945 5100.1998.tb01607.x</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Soil Science Department, University of Santiago de Compostela, Spain... DOCOBO, J.A., SPALDING, R.E., CEPLECHA, Z., DIAZ-FIERROS, F., TA...</span></span></span>

This does not mean that every celestial explanation can be excluded with absolute certainty. A fragile body can disintegrate in the atmosphere and leave little recoverable material, while eyewitness estimates of duration and direction can be inaccurate. However, an impact hypothesis must explain both the lack of extraterrestrial matter and the unusual shape and internal structure of the hollow. The published investigation found that combination sufficiently difficult that it moved away from impact as the leading interpretation.

## Why a natural eruption became the preferred model

The researchers proposed that the ground disturbance may have resulted from a sudden release of gas beneath the hillside. In this model, gas accumulated underground or moved through the saturated ground before erupting upwards with enough force to strip away soil and propel trees downhill. The rising plume could then have produced electrical charge separation and ignition, creating a luminous phenomenon that witnesses interpreted as a fireball travelling through the sky.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1945-5100.1998.tb01607.x" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">j.1945 5100.1998.tb01607.x</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Soil Science Department, University of Santiago de Compostela, Spain... DOCOBO, J.A., SPALDING, R.E., CEPLECHA, Z., DIAZ-FIERROS, F., TA...</span></span></span>

This proposal was attractive because it reversed the assumed direction of the event. Instead of a body arriving from above and excavating the hillside, energy and material would have burst out from below. Such a process offers a more direct explanation for why only local soil and vegetation were scattered, why no projectile was found and why the disturbed slope did not resemble a standard impact crater.

It could also account for the apparent connection between the explosion and the light without requiring a meteorite or vehicle. A bright electrical or combustion effect above the erupting gas might appear to move, change shape or descend when observed from different positions. The reports would then record a real luminous phenomenon, but not necessarily a solid object following the trajectory later inferred from eyewitness lines of sight.

The gas-eruption model should not be described as a fully proved solution. No source reservoir was directly identified, and an exact sequence from underground release to a travelling-looking luminous object was not observed instrumentally. The peer-reviewed article presented the mechanism as a possible explanation capable of reconciling the evidence, not as an experimentally demonstrated reconstruction. Scientific-news summaries have therefore continued to describe disagreement between a terrestrial gas event and some form of atmospheric or celestial explosion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1945-5100.1998.tb01607.x" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">j.1945 5100.1998.tb01607.x</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Soil Science Department, University of Santiago de Compostela, Spain... A cautious classification is consequently more accurate than either extreme. Cando was not shown to be an extraterrestrial crash, but neither was every detail reduced to a routine, familiar process. It appears to have been an unusual natural event whose components were imperfectly recorded: a genuine explosion or forceful ground disturbance, a widely seen light and an uncertain relationship between the two.

The word “crater” also gave the incident more certainty than the physical evidence justified. In ordinary reporting, almost any newly noticed depression associated with an explosion may be called a crater. In impact science, however, the term implies a structure created by an incoming body and supported by characteristic morphology, ejecta, mineral changes or projectile material. The Cando paper repeatedly placed the word in quotation marks, signalling that the feature looked crater-like but had not been demonstrated to be an impact crater.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1945-5100.1998.tb01607.x" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">j.1945 5100.1998.tb01607.x</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Soil Science Department, University of Santiago de Compostela, Spain... The passage of time made the narrative still easier to reshape. The initial search for a meteorite, the later rejection of impact and the tentative gas hypothesis were sometimes retold as competing versions rather than stages in an investigation. In UFO literature, the absence of debris could then be presented not as evidence against a craft but as evidence that authorities had removed it. That move makes the claim resistant to testing: both finding debris and failing to find it can be made to support the same conclusion.

## Cando’s place in A Coruña’s UFO history

Cando differs from A Coruña’s better-known military and radar cases. There was no documented radar track, air-defence response or official file establishing the presence of an unidentified aircraft. Its UFO connection arose mainly through retrospective interpretation of a luminous event and damaged terrain. It is therefore less a strong vehicle sighting than a study in how an unexplained natural disturbance can become a crash legend.

The case is still valuable because the investigation went beyond merely labelling the event “unidentified”. Scientists inspected the supposed impact area, tested the material and published a physical interpretation in a specialist journal. Their work did not explain every observation with certainty, but it established several firm limits: no meteorite was recovered, no artificial wreckage was documented, the local material was not shocked, and a high-speed impact was considered very unlikely.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1945-5100.1998.tb01607.x" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: onlinelibrary.wiley.com">[Wiley Online Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">onlinelibrary.wiley.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">j.1945 5100.1998.tb01607.x</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Soil Science Department, University of Santiago de Compostela, Spain... DOCOBO, J.A., SPALDING, R.E., CEPLECHA, Z., DIAZ-FIERROS, F., TA...</span></span></span>

Later reporting has weakened rather than strengthened the crashed-craft claim. No verifiable debris, photograph of wreckage, authenticated military document or new physical sample has emerged to overturn the original findings. What has survived is the memorable outline — fireball, blast, crater — stripped of the qualifications that made the scientific account more complicated.

Cando is best described as an unusual and incompletely resolved natural event, not a confirmed meteorite strike and still less an established UFO crash. The gas-eruption model remains provisional, but it addresses the physical evidence without requiring missing wreckage or an undocumented recovery operation. Within A Coruña’s UFO history, the incident matters chiefly as a warning that several genuine mysteries do not automatically combine into one extraordinary object.
