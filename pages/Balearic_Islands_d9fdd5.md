## Introduction

The islands’ wider UFO reputation grew during the late 1970s, when press coverage, organised sky watches and public enthusiasm turned isolated reports into a regional craze. Mallorca’s Puig Major, Menorca’s airport approaches and Ibiza’s Es Vedrà became recurring settings in the story. Yet the surviving record is uneven. Some incidents have contemporary radio transcripts and military paperwork; others survive mainly as newspaper retrospectives, folklore or internet retellings. The history therefore rewards careful separation between genuinely unresolved observations, cases that were probably meteors or aircraft, and legends that accumulated long after the alleged event.

## The 1978 Menorca airport encounter

On the evening of 24 October 1978, the captain of an AVIACO DC-9 approaching Menorca Airport asked the control tower whether other traffic had been reported nearby. According to the subsequent Air Force file, he could see two apparent aircraft to his left, roughly eight nautical miles away and below 500 feet. ### Two objects, or two different observations?

The evidence does not prove that the pilot and controller saw the same thing. The pilot described two lights resembling fast jet aircraft in formation. He thought their lighting was comparable to the white lights used on military jets to illuminate their wing edges. The controller, observing later from the ground, reported one luminous object. Estimates of distance and altitude were based on visual judgement rather than radar ranging or triangulation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://planetabenitez.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/1978-10-24_avistamiento_en_menorca.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: planetabenitez.com">[J.J. The case file reportedly considered the possibility that the pilot had seen two fighters, but the inquiry could not conclusively establish their identity. The absence of declared traffic strengthens the case that something was genuinely unaccounted for, yet it does not eliminate military aircraft, incomplete coordination or mistaken estimates. No photograph, radar plot or physical evidence appears in the publicly available record.

The fairest verdict is therefore **unresolved but limited**. There is credible testimony and good contemporary documentation, but insufficient instrument data to determine what was seen. The file supports the reality of an unusual aviation observation, not a claim of non-human technology.

## Mallorca’s late-1970s UFO fever

The Menorca incident occurred during a period of heightened public interest across the islands. By 1978, UFO reporting had become part of popular Spanish culture, encouraged by books, radio programmes, newspaper stories and highly publicised cases elsewhere in the country. In Mallorca, the excitement culminated in organised gatherings at which members of the public expected or hoped to witness extraordinary objects.

On 22 June 1978, hundreds of people reportedly assembled near Puig Major, Mallorca’s highest mountain, for an anticipated attempt at contact with extraterrestrials. No contact occurred. The gathering is important not as evidence of a sighting but as evidence of a social “flap”: a period in which expectation, publicity and repeated sky watching increased the number and visibility of reports.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.menorca.info/balears/noticias/2023/02/19/1885047/ovnis-baleares-hace-mas-anos-desato-ovnimania-avistamientos-1.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: menorca.info">[Menorca - Es diari]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">menorca.info</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Es diariOvnis en Baleares &#124; Hace más de 45 años se desató una ‘ovnimanía&#x27; de avistamientosFebruary 18, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 18, 2023</span></span></span>

Puig Major was a particularly effective setting for such stories. Its mountain profile, restricted military installations and prominent radar presence encouraged speculation. A light near a defence site could be interpreted as significant even when ordinary explanations—aircraft, stars, atmospheric effects or military activity—were more likely. The geography also made accurate distance judgements difficult: a light viewed against a dark mountain or open sea could appear nearby, stationary or unusually large without giving the observer reliable depth cues.

This atmosphere does not mean that every report was imaginary. It means that reports were shaped by a culture already primed to interpret ambiguous lights as UFOs. The late-1970s Balearic wave is best understood as a mixture of real observations, conventional phenomena, incomplete aviation information and public enthusiasm rather than as one coherent series of related craft.

## The February 1979 Mallorca fireball

A useful contrast with the [Menorca case]({{ 'menorca-case/' | relative_url }}) came on 14 February 1979, when a flight departing Palma reported a very bright object near the Mallorcan mountains. The crew described a white ball with green reflections crossing beneath them from left to right on a south-west to north-east path. Palma air-traffic control also received information that the tower had noticed something unusual in the same general direction.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://planetabenitez.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/1979-02-14_avistamiento_en_mallorca.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: planetabenitez.com">[J.J. The most revealing detail is the crew’s own immediate language: the pilot referred to the object as a meteor. The Air Force summary likewise characterised the report as a meteor crossing the aircraft’s route. Its brightness, greenish colour, rapid movement and disappearance are all compatible with a fireball—a particularly bright meteor produced when a fragment of rock or other material enters the atmosphere.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://planetabenitez.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/1979-02-14_avistamiento_en_mallorca.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: planetabenitez.com">[planetabenitez.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">planetabenitez.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">1979 02 14 avistamiento en mallorca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">BenítezAVISTAMIENTO DE FENOMENOS EXTRAÑOS (EXPEDIENTES OVNI)AVISTAMIENTO DE FENOMENOS EXTRAÑOS (EXPEDIENTES OVNI) AVISTAMIENTO DE FENOMEN...</span></span></span>

This case demonstrates why a report can enter a UFO archive without remaining mysterious. Military files collected unusual aerial observations because they might affect air safety or national defence, not because investigators had concluded that every object was exotic. The Mallorca record preserves a striking event, but the evidence within the document itself points strongly towards an astronomical explanation.

The February 1979 sighting should therefore be classed as **plausibly explained**, not unresolved. It also provides a warning against treating every declassified document as proof that the authorities encountered something beyond conventional understanding.

## Ibiza, Es Vedrà and the Manises connection

No Balearic location is more closely associated with paranormal claims than Es Vedrà, the steep rocky islet off Ibiza. It has been linked in popular accounts to magnetic anomalies, lost civilisations, mysterious lights and UFO activity. Much of this material belongs to modern tourism folklore rather than to a strong documentary record. Local reporting has noted that scientific investigations have not established the extraordinary magnetic properties frequently attributed to the islet.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.diariodeibiza.com/news/mysteries-es-vedra/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: diariodeibiza.com">[Diario de Ibiza]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">diariodeibiza.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">However, scientific investigations have found no evidence of unusual magnetic properties in the area.... Es Vedrà entered Spain’s better-known UFO narrative through the Manises incident of 11 November 1979. A commercial aircraft travelling from Mallorca towards the Canary Islands reported red lights that appeared to approach or follow it. The crew diverted and landed at Manises Airport near Valencia, after which a Spanish Air Force Mirage F1 was sent to investigate. Because the flight had passed the Ibiza area, later retellings often place Es Vedrà at the centre of the encounter.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manises_UFO_Incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Manises UFO incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Manises UFO incident</span></span></span>

The Manises case matters to Balearic history because the flight originated in Palma and the first phase unfolded over or near the western islands. Nevertheless, the emergency landing and military interception occurred outside the province, so it should not be treated as an exclusively Ibizan event. Claims that the object emerged from Es Vedrà, was attracted by its supposed magnetism or proves that the islet is a UFO base go well beyond the documented aviation report.

Conventional interpretations of Manises have included distant industrial lights, astronomical objects and misperception under difficult night-flying conditions. The witnesses have rejected parts of those explanations, and debate continues, but the case does not supply evidence for Es Vedrà’s alleged paranormal properties. Its later association with the islet shows how a documented aviation mystery can be absorbed into an older landscape of legends.

Modern viral videos have reinforced that process. Lights filmed near Es Vedrà are frequently labelled UFOs before aircraft paths, satellites, drones, reflections or camera artefacts have been ruled out. Recent local reporting has repeatedly discussed rows of lights over Ibiza in the context of Starlink satellites, whose aligned appearance shortly after launch can look highly unusual to observers unfamiliar with them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://lavozdeibiza.com/sociedad/ovni-en-ibiza-la-explicacion-al-supuesto-ufo-que-se-vio-desde-es-vedra/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: lavozdeibiza.com">[lavozdeibiza.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">lavozdeibiza.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on lavozdeibiza.com.</span></span></span>

## What Spain’s declassified files reveal

Spain’s Ministry of Defence now provides online access to Air Force UFO files concerning unusual phenomena reported in Spanish airspace. The collection covers reports compiled from the early 1960s to the mid-1990s. Physical copies were deposited with the Air Force’s central library in the early 1990s, and digitisation later made them accessible through the Defence Virtual Library.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/details/SpanishUFOFiles" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[archive.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on archive.org.</span></span></span>

For the Balearic Islands, the files are valuable because they preserve details that popular retellings often omit: exact times, communications with controllers, witness estimates, weather notes, investigative correspondence and the authorities’ proposed explanations. They also show that “officially investigated” is not the same as “officially confirmed as extraordinary”.

The records have several limitations. Many investigations began after delays, when accurate reconstruction was already difficult. Witnesses usually lacked calibrated instruments, and visual estimates of distance, speed and altitude could be highly uncertain. Radar records were not always preserved or included. In some files, the purpose was simply to decide whether the material still required classification, rather than to conduct a scientific study capable of identifying the phenomenon beyond reasonable doubt.

Declassification also creates a misleading impression of completeness. A surviving file proves that a report entered an official system, but it does not prove that every relevant observation was recorded, that all military activity was disclosed to investigators or that every document created at the time survives in the public collection. Conversely, gaps in a file are not evidence of a cover-up. They may reflect ordinary administrative practice, privacy restrictions, missing records or the low priority given to an incident once no defence threat was found.

## Why aviation reports deserve attention

Pilot and controller cases are often regarded as stronger than ordinary sightings because the witnesses understand aircraft movement and routinely observe the sky. The Menorca report benefits from that expertise. The pilot recognised the lights as resembling fast jets, while the controller used binoculars and checked with other control centres. Their testimony carries more weight than a vague report from an unidentified observer.

Professional expertise, however, does not remove the underlying limits of night-time vision. Even experienced pilots can misjudge the distance of an isolated light without a visible horizon or known reference point. A bright object can appear to move when the observer’s aircraft turns, and separate lights can seem connected. Stars, planets, meteors, satellites and distant aircraft may produce unfamiliar effects depending on angle, weather and cockpit motion.

The quality of a case therefore depends less on the witness’s job title than on the total evidence:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* **Strong contemporary documentation:** recordings, written statements and precise timing.
* **Independent corroboration:** observers separated by location or role.
* **Instrument support:** radar, photographs, calibrated video or navigational data.
* **A testable trajectory:** enough directional information to compare with aircraft, satellites, planets or meteors.
* **A clear chain of custody:** original records rather than later recollections.

</div>

Menorca performs well on documentation and witness credibility but poorly on instrument confirmation. Mallorca’s 1979 fireball has good documentation and a strong conventional match. Es Vedrà stories generally perform poorly on all five measures unless tied to a specific, independently recorded incident.

## A balanced assessment of the islands’ UFO history

The Balearic record contains one particularly credible unresolved aviation case, several reports with probable conventional explanations, and a much larger layer of popular folklore. The 24 October 1978 Menorca encounter remains the central incident because known traffic was checked, more than one aviation professional reported something unusual and an official inquiry followed. Yet the pilot’s own comparison with military jets and the absence of radar or photographic evidence leave ordinary aircraft-related explanations open.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://planetabenitez.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/1978-10-24_avistamiento_en_menorca.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: planetabenitez.com">[J.J. Benítez]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">planetabenitez.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">1978 10 24 avistamiento en menorca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">BenítezAVI STAMI ENTO DE FENOMENOS EXTRAÑOS (EXPEDI ENTES OVNI)AVI STAMI ENTO DE FENOMENOS EXTRAÑOS (EXPEDI ENTES OVNI) AVI STAMI ENTO...</span></span></span>

Mallorca’s February 1979 event is much less mysterious: the recorded description and the crew’s immediate reaction support a bright meteor. The Puig Major gatherings show how intense public expectation could develop around military landscapes without producing reliable evidence of extraordinary craft. Ibiza’s Es Vedrà reputation is culturally important, but claims of magnetic powers and persistent alien activity are not supported by comparable official documentation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://planetabenitez.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/1979-02-14_avistamiento_en_mallorca.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: planetabenitez.com">[planetabenitez.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">planetabenitez.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">1979 02 14 avistamiento en mallorca</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">BenítezAVISTAMIENTO DE FENOMENOS EXTRAÑOS (EXPEDIENTES OVNI)AVISTAMIENTO DE FENOMENOS EXTRAÑOS (EXPEDIENTES OVNI) AVISTAMIENTO DE FENOMEN...</span></span></span>

Later reporting has therefore both strengthened and weakened the province’s UFO history. Declassification strengthened the historical foundation by confirming that real pilots, controllers and military officials filed reports and sometimes conducted formal inquiries. At the same time, access to the original papers weakened many sensational retellings by revealing cautious witness language, conventional possibilities and major evidential gaps.

The most defensible conclusion is not that the Balearic Islands were visited by unknown craft, nor that every sighting was trivial. It is that the archipelago produced several genuine identification problems during a period of intense UFO interest. One or two remain unresolved because the surviving data are insufficient. Others are weak, exaggerated or convincingly explained. That distinction is what makes the islands’ UFO history worth examining: it shows how aviation uncertainty, military secrecy, landscape, media attention and folklore can combine to turn brief lights in the Mediterranean sky into stories that last for decades.
