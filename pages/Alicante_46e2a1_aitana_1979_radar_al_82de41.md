## Introduction

Aitana’s 1979 alerts were not one continuous encounter but two separate episodes that later became linked in accounts of Spain’s UFO wave. On 6 February, the EVA-5 air-surveillance station in [Alicante]({{ 'why-alicantes-ufo-files-still-invite/' | relative_url }}) reportedly detected unusual echoes while the tanker *Tamames*, sailing near Formentera, observed yellow-red lights and confusing radar returns. On 13 March, EVA-5 tracked an unidentified echo approaching the Spanish mainland, prompting a Mirage III fighter to take off from Manises for an identification mission.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ia800700.us.archive.org/24/items/SpanishUFOFiles/1979-02-06_avistamiento_en_aguas_de_baleares.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ia800700.us.archive.org">[ia800700.us.archive.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ia800700.us.archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AVISTAMIENTO DE FENOMENOS EXTRAÑOS (EXPEDIENTES OVNIAVISTAMIENTO DE FENOMENOS EXTRAÑOS (EXPEDIENTES OVNI) AVISTAMIENTO DE FENOMENOS EXTRA...</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Alicante_46e2a1_aitana_1979_radar_al_82de41-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for 1979 Alerts" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The surviving evidence supports a modest but significant conclusion. Real military and maritime alerts occurred, and the March contact was taken seriously enough to produce a fighter scramble. However, the February lights were closely associated with a parachuting exercise, while the publicly accessible record of the March interception contains too little detail to establish what generated the radar track. The importance of the cases lies less in evidence for an extraordinary craft than in showing how uncertain radar information, unannounced activity and public UFO interest converged over the western Mediterranean in 1979.

## The February lights seen from the *Tamames*

At 21:17 on 6 February 1979, the crew of the butane tanker *Tamames* saw yellowish-red lights to port while travelling from Alcúdia towards Cartagena. The lights resembled distress signals closely enough for the ship to alter course and approach them. Initially, its radar showed no corresponding target. After about 15 or 20 minutes, the lights went out and strong marks began whitening the radar screen in a way compared in the log to a RACON, a radar beacon used to assist navigation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ia800700.us.archive.org/24/items/SpanishUFOFiles/1979-02-06_avistamiento_en_aguas_de_baleares.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ia800700.us.archive.org">[ia800700.us.archive.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ia800700.us.archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AVISTAMIENTO DE FENOMENOS EXTRAÑOS (EXPEDIENTES OVNIAVISTAMIENTO DE FENOMENOS EXTRAÑOS (EXPEDIENTES OVNI) AVISTAMIENTO DE FENOMENOS EXTRA...</span></span></span>

The disturbance did not end with the first display. According to the ship’s log, the initial radar signals faded and stopped at about 23:50. At 00:45, numerous diffuse echoes appeared ahead of the vessel, including two apparently larger returns. They seemed to travel in roughly the same direction as the ship but at a lower speed, disappeared and then reappeared in other nearby positions. No lights identifying these radar targets were visible, despite good visibility, and the crew reported no abnormal operation of the ship’s machinery or instruments.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ia800700.us.archive.org/24/items/SpanishUFOFiles/1979-02-06_avistamiento_en_aguas_de_baleares.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ia800700.us.archive.org">[ia800700.us.archive.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ia800700.us.archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AVISTAMIENTO DE FENOMENOS EXTRAÑOS (EXPEDIENTES OVNIAVISTAMIENTO DE FENOMENOS EXTRAÑOS (EXPEDIENTES OVNI) AVISTAMIENTO DE FENOMENOS EXTRA...</span></span></span>

Contemporary reporting made the event sound more dramatic. Captain José Luis González was quoted as saying that more than 50 UFOs had surrounded the ship for approximately six hours. That description helped transform an ambiguous maritime incident into a national UFO story. Yet the ship’s formal record is more restrained: it describes lights, radar effects and uncertainty, not clearly observed structured craft performing controlled manoeuvres.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.noudiari.es/local-ibiza/defensa-desclasifica-un-expediente-ovni-ocurrido-a-15-millas-de-formentera/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: noudiari.es">[Noudiari.es]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">noudiari.es</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Defensa desclasifica un &#x27;Expediente OVNI&#x27; ocurrido a 15 millas de Formentera</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">23, 2016 — Defensa desclasifica un &#x27;Expediente OVNI&#x27; ocurrido a 15 millas de Formentera - Noudiari.es Home Local Defensa desclasifica un...</span></span></span>

Aitana entered the story because EVA-5 was also said to have detected echoes over the sea at the time. This apparent overlap between an air-defence radar and observations from a ship is the strongest reason the February case remains associated with Alicante. It suggests that the episode was not invented after the voyage. It does not, by itself, prove that both systems were detecting the same physical objects. Radar displays on a mountain surveillance site and on a moving vessel operate under different conditions and can be affected by different forms of interference, propagation and nearby activity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://alicantepress.com/archive/33209/defensa-investigo-al-menos-8-casos-ovni-en-la-provincia-de-alicante-en-los-anos-70-y-80" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: alicantepress.com">[ALICANTE PRESS periódico digital]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">alicantepress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on alicantepress.com.</span></span></span>

## Parachutists provide the clearest explanation

The most important later information is that parachutists carrying lights were operating in the area as part of a recording for the Spanish national radio programme *Testigo Directo*. Press reports published within days of the event identified the supposed UFOs as illuminated parachutists. Surviving radio material also includes participants describing their activity. This supplies a direct, ordinary source for at least the striking lights that first attracted the crew’s attention.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://maldita.es/malditahemeroteca/20230322/historia-buque-tamames-ovnis/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: maldita.es">[Maldita]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">maldita.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">La historia del buque Tamames que avistó 50 ovnis que resultaron ser paracaidistas · Maldita.esLa historia del buque Tamames que a...</span></span></span>

That explanation fits several elements of the observation. Illuminated parachutists could produce multiple moving lights, descend or drift without behaving like conventional aircraft, and vanish when their flares or lamps were extinguished. Seen from several miles away at night, their height, separation and distance would have been extremely difficult to judge. An exercise not known to the ship could therefore look both mysterious and potentially dangerous.

The parachuting explanation does not reproduce every detail of the *Tamames* radar account. It does not automatically explain why the screen was strongly whitened or why diffuse returns apparently followed and reappeared around the ship for hours. Those effects may have had more than one cause, including radar interference, anomalous propagation, sea clutter or unrelated vessels. The record does not contain the instrument data needed to choose confidently between them. The sensible interpretation is therefore layered rather than all-or-nothing: the visible “UFOs” were probably parachutists, while some reported radar behaviour remains poorly reconstructed rather than demonstrably extraordinary.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ia800700.us.archive.org/24/items/SpanishUFOFiles/1979-02-06_avistamiento_en_aguas_de_baleares.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ia800700.us.archive.org">[ia800700.us.archive.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ia800700.us.archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AVISTAMIENTO DE FENOMENOS EXTRAÑOS (EXPEDIENTES OVNIAVISTAMIENTO DE FENOMENOS EXTRAÑOS (EXPEDIENTES OVNI) AVISTAMIENTO DE FENOMENOS EXTRA...</span></span></span>

This distinction also explains why later descriptions differ. Some local reporting has continued to call the incident unexplained, largely because not every radar mark was identified. Fact-checking based on the declassified file and contemporary newspapers gives greater weight to the parachuting exercise. The later evidence substantially weakens the claim that the *Tamames* encountered a formation of unknown craft, even though it leaves legitimate questions about what appeared on the vessel’s radar.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.noudiari.es/local-ibiza/defensa-desclasifica-un-expediente-ovni-ocurrido-a-15-millas-de-formentera/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: noudiari.es">[Noudiari.es]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">noudiari.es</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Defensa desclasifica un &#x27;Expediente OVNI&#x27; ocurrido a 15 millas de Formentera</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">23, 2016 — Defensa desclasifica un &#x27;Expediente OVNI&#x27; ocurrido a 15 millas de Formentera - Noudiari.es Home Local Defensa desclasifica un...</span></span></span>

## The March radar track towards Spain

A separate alert occurred at 10:59 on 13 March 1979. EVA-5 reportedly detected an unidentified radar echo moving towards the Iberian Peninsula. The air-defence response was a scramble: a Mirage III was launched from Manises to investigate the unknown traffic. A scramble is an urgent operational take-off ordered so that a fighter can identify, intercept or monitor a contact that cannot be accounted for through normal flight information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://alicantepress.com/archive/33209/defensa-investigo-al-menos-8-casos-ovni-en-la-provincia-de-alicante-en-los-anos-70-y-80" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: alicantepress.com">[ALICANTE PRESS periódico digital]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">alicantepress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on alicantepress.com.</span></span></span>

The choice of aircraft and base was routine for the region. Manises was then home to Ala 11 and its Mirage III fighters. The first Spanish Mirage IIIs had arrived there in 1970, replacing older F-86 Sabres and providing a supersonic interception capability for eastern Spanish airspace. EVA-5, positioned high in the Sierra de Aitana, formed part of the national radar network responsible for watching and reporting activity over the western Mediterranean approaches.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ejercitodelaireydelespacio.defensa.gob.es/EA/ejercitodelaire/es/Unidades/Unidad/edd28d67-7a2d-11ee-99f3-005056bf91c5/?path=%2Fsites%2Finternet.es%2F.content%2Funidad%2Funidad_00004.xml&amp;resourceId=edd28d67-7a2d-11ee-99f3-005056bf91c5" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ejercitodelaireydelespacio.defensa.gob.es">[Ejército del Aire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ejercitodelaireydelespacio.defensa.gob.es</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ejército del Aire- Unidades</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Image: Distintivo del Escuadrón de Vigilancia Aérea nº5 (EVA 5) y Acuartelamiento Aéreo Aitana Image: Imagen histórica del Escuadrón de V...</span></span></span>

What happened after take-off is much less clear. The easily accessible summaries confirm the radar detection and launch but provide no reliable public account of the fighter making visual contact, locking its own radar onto a target or observing exceptional manoeuvres. They do not identify the pilot, interception altitude, flight duration or final classification of the echo. This absence matters because later UFO narratives can make the word “scramble” sound like proof of an extraordinary encounter. In military practice, it proves only that the track was unidentified and operationally important at that moment.

No contemporary witness narrative comparable to the *Tamames* log is readily available for the March alert. Nor is there enough published technical information to determine whether the echo represented an aircraft lacking identification, a transient radar anomaly, unusual propagation or another ordinary source. The episode should consequently be classified as unresolved but weakly documented, rather than as a confirmed visual-and-radar UFO interception.

## Why a fighter was launched

Air-defence personnel do not need to believe a radar contact is exotic before ordering an interception. Their immediate questions are practical: Is it an aircraft? Is it authorised? Is it entering controlled or defended airspace? Does it pose a safety or security risk? A track moving from the Mediterranean towards the mainland could require identification even if the most likely explanation was ultimately mundane.

EVA-5’s location made it particularly relevant to such decisions. Spanish air-surveillance squadrons were established to detect and track air traffic and to feed information into the wider command-and-control network. Mountain sites such as Aitana were placed at high altitude to extend radar coverage, especially over sea approaches where ground-based observation was limited.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ejercitodelaireydelespacio.defensa.gob.es/EA/ejercitodelaire/es/Unidades/Unidad/edd28d67-7a2d-11ee-99f3-005056bf91c5/?path=%2Fsites%2Finternet.es%2F.content%2Funidad%2Funidad_00004.xml&amp;resourceId=edd28d67-7a2d-11ee-99f3-005056bf91c5" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ejercitodelaireydelespacio.defensa.gob.es">[Ejército del Aire]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ejercitodelaireydelespacio.defensa.gob.es</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Ejército del Aire- Unidades</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Image: Distintivo del Escuadrón de Vigilancia Aérea nº5 (EVA 5) y Acuartelamiento Aéreo Aitana Image: Imagen histórica del Escuadrón de V...</span></span></span>

Radar, however, does not display a simple photograph of an object. Operators interpret returned radio energy, and older systems could show false or misleading tracks caused by equipment effects, atmospheric refraction, interference or clutter. A target might also be real but unidentified because its transponder was absent, incompatible or not correlated with a known flight plan. Launching a fighter was therefore a way to reduce uncertainty, not an official judgement that the radar had found a non-human vehicle.

The March event is best understood as a failed or undocumented identification attempt within this defensive process. Something appeared sufficiently credible on the Aitana display to justify action, but the surviving public account does not show that the interception converted the radar alert into stronger physical evidence.

## Two alerts, not one sustained incident

The February and March reports are often placed together because they occurred five weeks apart, involved the Mediterranean sector and passed through Aitana’s surveillance system. Yet they should not be merged into a single continuing mystery.

The February episode included independent maritime witnesses, visible lights and radar effects aboard the *Tamames*. It also has a persuasive explanation for its most dramatic feature: illuminated parachutists. The March episode was a daylight radar alert followed by a military launch, but it lacks a comparable witness account and remains thinly documented. Their evidential profiles are therefore almost opposite. February is richly described but largely explained; March appears operationally serious but is too sparsely recorded for a firm conclusion.

Nor should the March launch be confused with the much better-known Manises incident of 11 November 1979. In November, a passenger aircraft diverted after its crew reported red lights, and a Mirage F1 was sent from Los Llanos Air Base in Albacete. The Aitana alert in March involved a Mirage III from Manises and appears to have begun with radar rather than an airliner’s visual report. Treating the two scrambles as the same event has encouraged errors in aircraft type, base and chronology.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://alicantepress.com/archive/33209/defensa-investigo-al-menos-8-casos-ovni-en-la-provincia-de-alicante-en-los-anos-70-y-80" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: alicantepress.com">[alicantepress.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">alicantepress.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on alicantepress.com.</span></span></span>

The comparison is still useful. Together, the March and November launches show that Spanish air defence responded to unidentified contacts more than once during 1979. They also show why the year acquired such prominence in Spanish UFO history: unrelated lights, radar anomalies and aviation incidents accumulated quickly enough to appear part of a shared wave, even when their individual causes and evidential strength differed greatly.

## Exercises, radar uncertainty and the 1979 wave

The Aitana alerts illustrate how military readiness could generate UFO stories without requiring an extraordinary object. Parachute exercises created unfamiliar lights. Air-defence radar produced tracks that operators had to assess quickly. Fighters were launched because uncertainty itself mattered in controlled airspace. Journalists then reported the most vivid witness language, while the technical qualifications and incomplete follow-up were less memorable.

The political and military setting also favoured caution. Spain’s air-surveillance system was responsible for monitoring busy Mediterranean routes during a period of changing defence arrangements and growing civil aviation. An unidentified track approaching the coast could not simply be ignored. The correct operational response might be to launch an interceptor even when the eventual explanation was an exercise, an uncorrelated aircraft or a radar effect.

This is why the March scramble deserves a place in Alicante’s UFO history despite the weak public record. It demonstrates that EVA-5 was not merely a backdrop for civilian folklore. The station was an active part of a defence network capable of turning a fleeting radar uncertainty into a real military operation. At the same time, the February case warns against treating every simultaneous light and echo as confirmation of one mysterious vehicle.

## How strong is the case today?

The documentary foundation is stronger than that of many local UFO stories. The *Tamames* log survives in a declassified Air Force file, and official and local histories confirm EVA-5’s surveillance role and the presence of Mirage III fighters at Manises. The core chronology—February lights and radar reports, followed by a separate March echo and scramble—is therefore credible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ia800700.us.archive.org/24/items/SpanishUFOFiles/1979-02-06_avistamiento_en_aguas_de_baleares.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ia800700.us.archive.org">[archive.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ia800700.us.archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AVISTAMIENTO DE FENOMENOS EXTRAÑOS (EXPEDIENTES OVNIAVISTAMIENTO DE FENOMENOS EXTRAÑOS (EXPEDIENTES OVNI) AVISTAMIENTO DE FENOMENOS EXTRA...</span></span></span>

The extraordinary interpretation is considerably weaker. The February lights have a well-supported parachuting explanation. The associated radar effects are insufficiently documented for technical reconstruction. The March echo caused a genuine response but has no publicly established visual confirmation or unusual performance data. A fighter taking off is evidence of uncertainty and precaution, not evidence that the target possessed impossible capabilities.

The fairest assessment is that Aitana’s 1979 alerts form a genuine air-defence case family with mixed outcomes. One incident was probably driven by an exercise whose appearance surprised nearby mariners; the other remains unidentified in the limited surviving account. Neither justifies a claim of extraterrestrial visitation. Together, however, they reveal how operational ambiguity became embedded in Spain’s 1979 UFO wave—and why Alicante’s mountain radar station occupies such a prominent place in the country’s declassified UFO history.
