## Introduction

[Valencia]({{ 'valencias-ufo-cases-between-mystery-and/' | relative_url }})’s UFO history is unusually useful for understanding how governments handle unexplained aerial reports because the province is represented in Spain’s official military archive. The Spanish Air Force files do not prove that extraordinary craft were present; instead, they show how military authorities recorded, assessed and classified reports that initially appeared unusual. For Valencia, the archive’s most important value is the paper trail behind cases such as the 1979 [Manises incident]({{ 'manises-case-f32e0b/' | relative_url }}) and other reports investigated through official channels.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es/BVMDefensa/exp_ovni/es/catalogo_imagenes/grupo.do?path=102241" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es">[Biblioteca Virtual Defensa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Biblioteca Virtual DefensaBiblioteca Virtual de Defensa &gt; Expedientes OVNI &gt; Avistamiento de fenómenos extraños en Valencia, Motril y Mad...</span></span></span>

Spain’s released UFO records are therefore best understood as investigation records rather than “proof files”. They document observations, witness statements, technical assessments and administrative decisions. The central question raised by the archive is not whether the files confirm UFO claims, but what they reveal about how aviation and defence institutions examined uncertain events.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es/BVMDefensa/exp_ovni/es/consulta/busqueda.do" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es">[Biblioteca Virtual Defensa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gob.es.</span></span></span>

## Military reports and released documents

The Spanish Air Force maintained a collection of UFO reports for several decades, with cases entering military channels when pilots, air traffic personnel, radar operators or other observers reported unusual aerial events. The archive was later opened through a declassification process, making many of these records available through Spain’s Ministry of Defence digital collections. The official archive allows searches by location, date and other details, including provincial cases connected with Valencia.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es/BVMDefensa/exp_ovni/es/consulta/busqueda.do" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es">[Biblioteca Virtual Defensa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gob.es.</span></span></span>

The released material includes reports from across Spain covering the Cold War period and later decades. The documents generally contain a description of the event, information gathered during the investigation, analysis by military personnel and a final classification. Their importance is that they preserve the government’s working record: what was reported, what checks were made and why a case was considered solved, unresolved or insufficiently explained.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es/BVMDefensa/es/consulta/registro.do?id=42382" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es">[Biblioteca Virtual Defensa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gob.es.</span></span></span>

For Valencia, the strongest example is the Manises incident of 11 November 1979. The official archive contains records relating to unusual phenomena reported in Valencia, Motril and Madrid during that period, including the Manises case material. The event involved a passenger aircraft crew reporting unusual lights, followed by military involvement and an Air Force response.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es/BVMDefensa/exp_ovni/es/catalogo_imagenes/grupo.do?path=102241" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es">[Biblioteca Virtual Defensa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Biblioteca Virtual DefensaBiblioteca Virtual de Defensa &gt; Expedientes OVNI &gt; Avistamiento de fenómenos extraños en Valencia, Motril y Mad...</span></span></span>

The significance of the Manises file is not that it settled the UFO question. Rather, it shows why some reports became historically important: the witnesses included trained aviation professionals, the incident affected flight operations, and military authorities treated the report seriously enough to investigate. Later interpretations have proposed conventional explanations for aspects of the sighting, but the case remains a major example of how an unusual observation can move from public report into official aviation records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es/BVMDefensa/exp_ovni/es/catalogo_imagenes/grupo.do?path=102241" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es">[Biblioteca Virtual Defensa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Biblioteca Virtual DefensaBiblioteca Virtual de Defensa &gt; Expedientes OVNI &gt; Avistamiento de fenómenos extraños en Valencia, Motril y Mad...</span></span></span>

## How investigations classified sightings

The Air Force approach was largely administrative and investigative rather than speculative. A report entering the system did not automatically represent an unknown technology or extraordinary event. Military investigators examined possible explanations such as aircraft, astronomical objects, atmospheric effects, operational activity or errors in observation. The final classification reflected the evidence available rather than the original excitement surrounding a sighting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es/BVMDefensa/es/consulta/registro.do?id=42382" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es">[Biblioteca Virtual Defensa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gob.es.</span></span></span>

This distinction is important because the word “unidentified” describes a temporary state of knowledge, not a conclusion about origin. A pilot, radar operator or civilian observer may accurately report seeing something they cannot identify, while investigators may later determine that the cause was a conventional object or phenomenon. The Spanish files are valuable precisely because they show this process rather than only the initial claim.

The role of civilian researcher Vicente-Juan Ballester Olmos was also significant in the study and organisation of the released material. His work helped catalogue and analyse Spanish Air Force UFO documentation, contributing to a more systematic examination of the records rather than relying only on individual stories or media reports.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es/BVMDefensa/es/consulta/registro.do?id=42382" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es">[Biblioteca Virtual Defensa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gob.es.</span></span></span>

For researchers examining Valencia, the archive provides a way to compare different types of reports. A dramatic aviation case such as Manises can be studied alongside less prominent provincial reports, allowing investigators to examine whether patterns emerge or whether individual events are better explained by local circumstances.

## What the archive reveals about Valencia’s UFO history


<div class="content-callout content-callout--key content-callout--takeaway" markdown="1">

The main lesson from the Spanish Air Force files is that official attention does not equal official confirmation. Valencia’s inclusion in the archive shows that unusual aerial reports were considered important enough to document, but the records themselves do not establish that any case involved non-human technology or unknown physical craft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es/BVMDefensa/exp_ovni/es/consulta/busqueda.do" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es">[Biblioteca Virtual Defensa]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bibliotecavirtual.defensa.gob.es</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on gob.es.</span></span></span>

</div>

The files are most valuable because they preserve the intermediate stage between a witness report and a final judgement. They show the practical questions authorities asked: What was observed? Who reported it? Could the object be identified? Were there aviation, weather or astronomical explanations? What remained uncertain after investigation?

This makes the archive an important source for understanding the governance of unexplained sightings in Valencia. It demonstrates that military institutions treated some reports as aviation or security matters requiring review, while also showing the limits of official investigations. A classified or investigated case is not automatically an extraordinary case; it is evidence of a process.

## Limits of official conclusions

The Spanish Air Force records have helped move discussion of Valencian UFO cases away from rumours and towards documents, but they do not remove all uncertainty. Some files contain incomplete information, limited technical data or observations that cannot be fully reconstructed decades later. The absence of a definite explanation in a report does not necessarily mean that the most unusual explanation is correct.

The archive also reflects the priorities and capabilities of the period. Investigators worked with the radar systems, aviation data and reporting methods available at the time. Modern analysts may reassess older cases using additional information, while some questions may remain impossible to answer because original evidence was never collected or preserved.

For Valencia, the enduring importance of the Spanish Air Force files is therefore historical and analytical. They show how a major UFO region was documented by official institutions and provide a rare record of how authorities responded when witnesses reported something they could not immediately identify. The documents make the story more reliable as history, but they leave the final interpretation dependent on the strength of the evidence in each individual case.
